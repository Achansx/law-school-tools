The deck establishes that executive privilege is real but not absolute: presumptive privilege is strongest for military and diplomatic communications and is rooted in Article II and separation of powers, but courts can review privilege claims and the privilege must yield to specific criminal justice needs *(p. 4)*. The balancing test requires that evidence be relevant, specific, and necessary, and the court can examine materials in camera *(p. 4)*. United States v. Clinton v. The deck flags the unforeseen consequences theme: the Clinton case "underestimated the real-world consequences of compliance," and "legal rulings can have unforeseen political consequences" *(pp. 7-8)*. The deck's closing legal principles: presidents are not above the law, judicial authority can check executive power, and separation of powers does not preclude judicial review *(p. 8)*.

This page treats the two cases as bookends marking the outer limits of presidential immunity and privilege: criminal evidentiary process plus pre-presidential civil liability. The "Youngstown framework in the background" gloss is editorial cross-reference; the deck does not invoke Youngstown. The synthesis that "the President is not above the law in either the executive-power or personal-liability sense" sharpens the deck's "not above the law" point into a two-dimensional frame. ## Lecture Outline

### I. United States v Nixon (1974)

**Context:** The Watergate scandal led to a subpoena of the Oval Office tapes *(p. 2)*. The misconduct occurred while Nixon was in office seeking re-election: an attempt to burglarize/bug Democratic HQ at the Watergate complex, followed by a coverup involving Nixon and his AG John Mitchell, with the coverup conversations captured by a then-unknown White House taping system *(p. 2)*. Nixon claimed executive privilege to withhold the tapes, refused, and cited national security concerns; the special prosecutor and judiciary demanded compliance, and the case reached the Supreme Court in 1974 *(p. 2)*.

**Holding (8-0; Burger, C.J.):**
1. The matter is justiciable: the dispute between the Special Prosecutor (acting under DOJ regulations with independent authority) and the President is a real case or controversy, not an intra-executive matter the President can resolve unilaterally.
2. Executive privilege exists -- presumptive privilege is strongest for military and diplomatic communications and is rooted in Article II and separation of powers *(p. 4)*. This privilege is not expressly stated in the Constitution but is implied by the structure of Article II.
3. But executive privilege is not absolute -- courts can review privilege claims, and the privilege must yield to specific criminal justice needs *(p. 4)*. The need for evidence in criminal trials outweighs secrecy, and separation of powers does not exempt a president from legal process *(p. 3)*.

**The standard Nixon establishes:**
- The balancing test requires that evidence be relevant, specific, and necessary *(p. 4)*
- The privilege must be weighed against the particular evidentiary need, not denied categorically
- A court reviews the contested materials in camera to determine which portions are privileged *(p. 4)*

**Significance:** *Nixon* gave executive privilege constitutional grounding -- the President can legitimately claim it. But it simultaneously foreclosed absolute privilege: the President cannot use privilege to block criminal accountability. Nixon complied with the order, which led to his resignation *(p. 3)*.

### II. Clinton v Jones (1997)

**Context:** Paula Jones sued President Clinton for sexual harassment; the conduct occurred while Clinton was Governor of Arkansas, the lawsuit proceeded while Clinton was President, and Jones wanted discovery that might include Clinton's habits *(p. 5)*. Clinton argued for temporary immunity while in office, the lower court allowed the case to proceed, and the Supreme Court granted certiorari in 1997 *(p. 5)*.

**Holding (9-0; Stevens, J.):**
1. The President has no immunity for unofficial conduct before the presidency *(p. 6)*. Presidential immunity, where it exists, attaches to the President's official acts (see *Nixon v Fitzgerald*). It does not extend to private conduct occurring before the presidency.
2. Civil litigation would not significantly disrupt presidential duties *(p. 6)*. The Court found that requiring a sitting President to defend a civil suit would impose only minimal burdens on the exercise of executive power.
3. Courts can manage schedules to avoid presidential interference *(p. 6)*. Clinton was required to comply with the discovery process *(p. 6)*.

**What the Court got wrong (per subsequent events):** The discovery process led to Clinton's deposition, in which Clinton denied involvement with Monica Lewinsky under oath; the investigation uncovered perjury, leading to impeachment, though Clinton was impeached but not convicted in the Senate *(p. 7)*. The deck describes this as the case "underestimat[ing] the real-world consequences of compliance" and notes that the "[r]uling confirmed presidents are accountable under the law" *(p. 7)*. More broadly, the deck flags that "[l]egal rulings can have unforeseen political consequences" and that "[t]he Court strives for impartiality but must consider practical effects" *(p. 8)*.

**Significance:** The President is not above the law in a private civil capacity *(p. 8)*. A sitting President can be required to testify, produce documents, and defend civil claims arising from unofficial conduct. The Court carved out no blanket protection based on the office.

## Key Takeaways

- Executive privilege has constitutional status (rooted in Article II and separation of powers), but is qualified, not absolute.
- *United States v Nixon* (8-0): a specific, demonstrated criminal evidentiary need outweighs a generalized presidential communications privilege. The privilege must yield to the rule of law in a criminal proceeding.
- *Clinton v Jones* (9-0): presidential immunity does not extend to unofficial, pre-presidential conduct. The President is subject to civil process for private actions like any other citizen.
- Together, the cases establish that the President has significant but bounded immunities and privileges.
- The Court's empirical error in *Clinton v Jones* -- predicting minimal disruption -- is a recurring reminder that courts often underestimate the political consequences of their legal holdings.
