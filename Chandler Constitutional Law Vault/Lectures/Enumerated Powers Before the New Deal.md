## Lecture Outline

**The Fugitive Slave Problem: Prigg v. Pennsylvania (1842)**

Article IV is "the Glue": Section 2's Fugitive Slave Clause requires a person held to service or labor in one state, escaping into another, to be "delivered up on Claim of the Party to whom such Service or Labour may be due" -- but the deck flags that there is no explicit enforcement provision; *Puerto Rico v. Branstad* (1987) is cited for the proposition that the Clause is "about to become important again in abortion disputes" *(slide 3)*. The Fugitive Slave Issue brought three subproblems: incitement prevention, false accusations, and sanctuary efforts *(slide 4)*. Antebellum measures included the House gag rule (1836-1844), state prohibitions on abolitionist speech, and the Negro Seamen Act (1822) requiring incarceration of free Black sailors -- held unconstitutional in *Elkison v. Delesseliene* (1823, D.S.C., Justice Johnson riding circuit) but replicated elsewhere *(slide 5)*. The false-accusation problem turned on what procedures should distinguish the free person from the fugitive -- affidavits, jury trial, judicial determination -- and whether children of fugitives born in free states were themselves fugitives (never resolved by the Supreme Court) *(slide 6)*. Sanctuary efforts in Northern states infuriated the slaveholding population *(slide 7)*.

The Fugitive Slave Act of 1793 empowered slaveholders to seize fugitives and present them before federal judges or local magistrates for a removal certificate; harboring and obstructing were also crimes *(slide 8)*. Pennsylvania's 1826 law criminalized slave-catchers who carried away any "negro or mulatto" with intent to sell or detain them as a slave -- the deck flags whether this is commandeering and references *Testa v. Katt* *(slide 8)*. Margaret Morgan, a slave who left Maryland for Pennsylvania in 1832 and had a child with a free man, was returned by slave-catchers; under *partus sequitur ventrem* her child too was potentially a slave -- her former owner's widow saw "an asset" *(slide 9)*. Pennsylvania and Maryland sought an agreed-on case to resolve federal power over fugitive slaves "just like Dred Scott" *(slide 9)*.

Story's opinion disclaims politicization; his theory looks to the nature and objects of the particular provision with the lights of contemporary history and gives words operation to fairly secured ends *(slide 10)*. The deck reads Story's reasoning: everyone knows the South would never have agreed to union without ability to prevent fugitive slaves from gaining freedom; the Constitution gave slave owners the right to repossess this human property like any other; if the Constitution guarantees the right, it provided the remedy; feds can't commandeer states (linking to *Printz*); a first-Congress passage (linking to *McCulloch*); and a broad reading of Necessary and Proper *(slide 11)*. The deck poses the food-for-thought question: why isn't Prigg reviled with the same vigor as Dred Scott -- language? importance? Story taught at Harvard? *(slide 11)*. On the orange passage about state magistrates: state magistrates may exercise the authority unless prohibited by state legislation *(slide 12)*. The deck notes William Lloyd Garrison's reaction and the unknown fate of Margaret Morgan and her child -- the husband accused of stealing a jacket on a steamship, jumping off to escape a mob and drowning *(slide 10)*.

**Legal Tender Cases: Hepburn, Knox, and Juilliard**

The deck begins with Article I, Section 8, Clause 5: Congress shall have Power to coin Money and regulate its Value -- asking whether paper is "coining" and whether iron or cheap metal would have changed the analysis *(slide 13)*. In *Hepburn v. Griswold* (1870), Chase, C.J., addressed how Lincoln was going to pay soldiers without enough gold or silver: option one was promise-of-redemption paper; option two was fiat money "may be used to satisfy all debts public and private," the path Congress took in 1863 (with Treasury Secretary Chase grudgingly going along) *(slide 14)*. The 4-3 Supremes held the law unconstitutional *(slide 14)*.

Why only seven votes? The deck explains that to prevent Andrew Johnson from appointing more justices, the Judicial Circuits Act of 1866 reduced the Court from ten to six; the Judiciary Act of 1869 set it at the current nine, where it has stayed for 154 years *(slide 15)*. The deck enumerates Chase's reasoning: no express power to make paper a legal tender; this is not the same as coining nor as issuing without forcing use; the policy argument that paper money causes problems; "contrary to spirit of constitution" (linking to Roberts's *NFIB*); and reluctant acquiescence by some justices *(slide 16)*.

*Knox v. Lee* (1871) (Strong, J.) is extremely important practically; Hepburn had a very short life span as the Court composition changed (no one changed their mind), and Knox overruled it 5-4 without a majority opinion *(slide 17)*. The deck lists the interpretive themes to think about: interpreting from overall structure, aggregation, and purpose (link to *Griswold*); emergency constitutionalism (link to *Dames & Moore*); stare decisis (link to *Casey*) *(slide 17)*. Knox involved Confederate seizure of sheep from a Northerner living in Texas and the question whether the judgment could be paid in junky paper money -- "perhaps a case about how tough we are going to be on the Confederacy"; the Solicitor General reportedly forgot about *Knox v. Lee* in the *Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health* argument *(slide 17)*. The emergency reasoning: the deck reads the opinion as explaining how it was a true emergency, asserting that the degree of necessity is for Congress, and using ex post justifications *(slide 18)*.

The deck reproduces the resulting-powers passage: Congress has often exercised, without question, powers "not expressly given nor ancillary to any single enumerated power" -- "resulting powers, arising from the aggregate powers of the government" (Story's *Commentaries*), with examples of the right to sue, to make contracts, to require oaths, to build a capitol or a presidential mansion, and the penal code *(slide 19)*. The deck asks whether "resulting powers" are in addition to N&P and subject to the same limits such as the pretext passage and "NFIB proper" *(slide 19)*. The "Entirety Theme": it is not indispensable that a federal power be specified in the Constitution's words or clearly traceable to one specified power; its existence may be deduced from more than one substantive power expressly defined, or from them all combined *(slide 20)*. The deck flags that the entirety theme will reappear in *Griswold* on the rights side, asks where opposition to it will be seen, and notes that the last Supreme Court citation was in 1878 in a dissenting opinion *(slide 20)*. The Knox standard: before holding legal tender acts unconstitutional, the Court must be convinced they were not appropriate means in any degree, or were prohibited; a consideration of time and circumstance is important *(slide 21)*. The deck asks how this holding might be relevant to COVID laws *(slide 21)*.

*Juilliard v. Greenman* (1884) extended the legal tender power to peacetime, with heavy reliance on *McCulloch* *(slide 22)*. On the original "borrow money and emit bills" draft where "emit bills" was stricken, the deck calls the inference inconclusive (cf. the Taft-Hartley discussion in *Youngstown*) *(slide 22)*. The deck quotes the political-question framing: whether exigency justifies resort to paper money is a political question for Congress, not a judicial question, drawing a comparison to COVID and student loan / rent moratoria *(slide 22)*.

**Commerce Clause: The Pre-New Deal Restrictive Era**

*United States v. Dewitt* (1869) involved a federal ban on low-flashpoint oils with no interstate requirement; the theory was that banning this oil would stimulate sales of other federally-taxable oil *(slide 23)*. Chase, C.J., found the effect "far too remote" -- the deck calls this "rejection of prophylactic theory of commerce clause," linking to *NFIB* (but cf. *Wickard* and *Raich*); this was state police regulation *(slide 23)*. The Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890 declared every contract, combination, or conspiracy in restraint of trade among the several states illegal *(slide 24)*.

*United States v. E.C. Knight Co.* (1895) involved American Sugar Refining's threat to gain a national sugar monopoly by buying out four competitors *(slide 25)*. The deck distills the majority's moves: a notion of things "belonging to commerce" (of which manufacture is not one); rejection of the "ultimate effect" test; proof of illegitimacy by arguing a state could regulate this field with only indirect effects; slippery slope; and if commerce-regulation is exclusive, Congress will be overly burdened *(slide 25)*. Justice Harlan (the first) dissented, purporting to acquiesce in the direct/indirect distinction but seeing this as direct, then offering a real theory of improper obstruction -- channeling Marshall's "trust Congress" theme of *Gibbons* and "let the end be legitimate" theme from *McCulloch*; political power should be congruent with economic reality (the adaptability theme) *(slide 25)*.

*Champion v. Ames* (1903) (Harlan, J.) upheld a federal statute penalizing interstate transport of lottery tickets *(slide 26)*. The statute clearly regulated interstate commerce, so what does Harlan do for the rest of the opinion? -- Congress may legislate over commerce based on its understanding of morality, and "the possible abuse of a power is not an argument against its existence" (channeling *McCulloch*'s trust that the court will see through pretexts) *(slide 27)*. The deck asks students to think about whether *Gonzalez v. Raich* is really the 2004 version of *Champion v. Ames* with morality shifted from "pestilential" lotteries to "gateway" marijuana *(slide 27)*. Fuller's dissent rested on a distinction between "articles of commerce" and "non-commercial articles," asking how one explains *Paul v. Virginia* declining to regulate commerce in insurance, and asserting that no one would pretend that persons could be kept off trains because they were going to engage in the lottery business *(slide 28)*.

*Hammer v. Dagenhart* (1918) (Day, J.) struck the Keating-Owen Act of 1916 barring interstate shipment of goods produced by child labor *(slide 29)*. The deck reads the majority's reasoning: the goods shipped are harmless and shipping them interstate doesn't actually harm anyone (unlike the Lottery case, *Hipolite Egg*, or *Hoke*/*Caminetti*); any harm is already done locally; the federal government cannot regulate prophylactically to prevent child-made goods from undercutting other states' regulations; the rebuttals about no requirement that states exercise police power, the Tenth Amendment, and the pretext that Marshall warned about in *McCulloch* *(slide 30)*. The "Apocalypse Theme": if Congress can regulate matters entrusted to local authority by prohibiting interstate movement, "all freedom of commerce will be at an end, and the power of the states over local matters may be eliminated, and thus our system of government be practically destroyed" *(slide 31)*.

**The New Deal Confrontation: Schechter Poultry and Butler**

The Great Depression (1929-1937ish): GDP down 15%, unemployment rising to 24.9% (no CARES or unemployment insurance), industrial production down 46%, agricultural prices dropped more than half, deflationary spiral; FDR was elected 472-59 and Democrats gained 101 House seats and 12 Senate seats *(slide 32)*. The Court of 1933 included Van Devanter, McReynolds, Sutherland, and Butler (the Four Horsemen); Brandeis, Cardozo, and Stone (the Three Musketeers); and Hughes and Roberts (swing votes) *(slide 33)*. The National Industrial Recovery Act -- a centerpiece of FDR's initial Depression effort with banking reforms, AAA, fair competition, union organization rights, price controls, and the Public Works Administration -- was passed on Day 104, with regulatory power delegated to industry itself subject to executive approval; the Live Poultry Code was promulgated thereunder *(slide 34)*.

*Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States* (1935) (Hughes, C.J.) (9-0): the deck calls it "fascinating and big" and asks whether the Court is willing to strike down a centerpiece of law designed to address a grave emergency *(slide 35)*. The opinion is a major case on the commerce clause and the non-delegation doctrine -- the beginning of an effort to grapple with the executive/administrative state, with forward links to *Gundy v. United States* and the Major Questions Doctrine in *Biden v. Nebraska* *(slide 35)*. The deck flags the relationship between *Dewitt* / *EC Knight* and *Schechter* *(slide 35)*. Emergency constitutionalism: extraordinary conditions do not create or enlarge constitutional power; the Constitution established a national government with adequate powers, but those powers are limited by the constitutional grants; assertions of extraconstitutional authority were anticipated and precluded by the Tenth Amendment -- channeling Justice Davis in *Ex Parte Milligan* on the pernicious consequences of any doctrine that constitutional provisions can be suspended during great exigencies *(slide 36)*.

The non-delegation doctrine in *Schechter*: Congress could not delegate its legislative authority to trade or industrial associations to enact the laws they deem wise and beneficent *(slide 37)*. The NIRA supplied no standards for any trade or activity, did not prescribe rules of conduct, but authorized the making of codes to prescribe them; § 3 set up no standards aside from general aims of rehabilitation, correction, and expansion; the President's discretion in approving or prescribing codes was virtually unfettered *(slide 38)*. On the chickens already in interstate commerce: the deck notes the poultry had come to a halt, comparing this to *Katzenbach v. McClung* (barbecue that has come to rest) *(slide 39)*. On effect on interstate commerce: Congress can regulate some things based on interstate-commerce effect, so why not labor? *(slide 40)*. The slippery-slope passage: if the commerce clause reached all enterprise and transactions with indirect effects, federal authority would embrace practically all activities and state authority would exist only by sufferance of the federal government; the deck asks what the race-to-the-bottom argument is and how the Court handles it *(slide 40)*.

Professor commentary on the religious dimension: the straight killing law is facially neutral -- one sticks one's hand in the coop and the first chicken pulled out is the one received; Jewish butchers were jailed for, among other things, refusing to accept a religious law that required customers to have the right to select their own chicken; the deck cites Steve Horwitz, *That's Not Kosher: How Four Jewish Butchers Brought Down the First New Deal*, and asks how *Schechter* comes out today if the Department of Agriculture had passed a similar regulation *(slide 41)*.

*United States v. Butler* (1936) addressed New Deal legislation increasing agricultural commodity prices via a processing tax with no requirement that the commodities have any relation to interstate commerce *(slide 43)*. The deck poses the Madison-Hamilton debate on the contours of the taxing and spending powers, with Story as "the ref" *(slide 43)*. How does Madison have the last laugh? -- the AAA invades the reserved rights of the states; it is a statutory plan to regulate and control agricultural production, beyond the powers delegated to the federal government; the tax, appropriation, and direction for disbursement are but means to an unconstitutional end; the Tenth Amendment says powers not granted are prohibited; the attainment of a prohibited end may not be accomplished under the pretext of granted powers; the deck asks whether this anti-pretext theory survives to the end of the course *(slide 44)*. The seeds of conditional-spending doctrine: a permissible conditional appropriation is distinguished from an impermissible contractual obligation to submit to regulation that otherwise could not be enforced -- with the example of an educational appropriation that would be bad if conditioned on teaching doctrines subversive of the Constitution; link to *Dole v. South Dakota* and *NFIB v. Sebelius* *(slide 45)*. The "rebuke" passage: no framer or commentator suggested that the spending clause authorized obliterating the independence of states or converting the United States into a central government exercising uncontrolled police power *(slide 46)*. The deck closes by channeling Anti-Federalist 33 (Brutus): a power exercised without limitation will "wait upon the ladies at their toilet … enter the house of every gentleman … attend him to his bedchamber" -- a power that swallows up all state governments *(slide 47)*.

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

The lecture poses the question of "emergency constitutionalism" across multiple cases: *Knox* uses the Civil War emergency to justify legal tender *(slide 18)*; *Juilliard* extends it to peacetime *(slide 22)*; *Schechter* squarely rejects the Depression as sufficient to expand constitutional power *(slide 36)*. The deck draws the parallel to COVID and student loan / rent moratoria *(slides 21-22)*.

The *Schechter* religious freedom angle: the deck asks how *Schechter* comes out today if the Department of Agriculture had passed a similar regulation, and frames the case as effectively *Sherbert v. Verner* in disguise -- a facially neutral law that imposes a burden on religious exercise *(slide 41)*.

*Butler*'s anti-pretext theory: the majority finds the processing tax unconstitutional as a device to regulate agriculture, an area reserved to the states; the deck asks students to "see if this anti-pretext theory makes it to the end of the course" *(slide 44)*.

## Key Takeaways

- The pre-New Deal period shows the Court repeatedly wrestling with the line between local and interstate, and between direct and indirect effects, without a stable formula.
- Knox's "resulting powers" and "entirety theme" represent the broadest judicial endorsement of implied power outside of McCulloch; yet the doctrine largely disappears from cited authority after 1878.
- Champion v. Ames establishes that Congress may use the commerce power to regulate against perceived moral harms, creating the template for later drug and vice legislation.
- Hammer v. Dagenhart's rejection of the prophylactic rationale creates the critical distinction between regulating goods (the lottery tickets in Champion) and using the commerce power to force local policy uniformity (the child labor situation).
- Schechter's non-delegation holding is as important as its commerce clause holding and resurfaces in Biden v. Nebraska's Major Questions Doctrine.
- Butler plants the seeds of conditional spending doctrine (the coercion limit) that South Dakota v. Dole and NFIB would later develop.
