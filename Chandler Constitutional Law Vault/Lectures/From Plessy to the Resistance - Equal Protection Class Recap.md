## Lecture Outline

### Brown and the Road to Massive Resistance

Brown v Board of Education (1954) overruled Plessy 9-0, holding that separate is inherently unequal, with Warren engineering unanimity (slide 1). Brown II (1955) ordered desegregation with "all deliberate speed" -- a formula that became a license for delay, the emphasis landing on "deliberate" (slide 1).

Cooper v Aaron (1958) confronted Arkansas's argument that it wasn't a party to Brown, since non-parties aren't bound by a judgment; the Court shut it down by having all nine Justices individually sign, like "polling the jury," and asserting judicial supremacy -- Marbury redux, with pre-Civil War theories of federalism not to be tolerated (slide 1).

The Resistance Playbook included the Southern Manifesto signed by every Southern senator except LBJ, interposition resolutions, and the clever move of closing the public schools entirely and distributing vouchers for private academies -- given a choice between integrated schools and no public schools, they chose no schools (slide 1). The voucher strategy exploited the state action doctrine: if the state distributes money and parents "choose" segregated schools, that's private action beyond constitutional reach, and you get 90% of what you want without direct state involvement (slide 1). The Constitution doesn't require a public school system (slide 1).

### The Affirmative Duty to Integrate

Griffin v Prince Edward County (1964) (9-0) used the drowning analogy: you have no duty to rescue a drowning stranger, but once you start swimming out, a duty attaches; likewise, a district that had never operated segregated schools could choose not to run public schools at all, but Prince Edward County had been using the muscle of the state to enforce segregation for decades, and having created the harm it cannot wash its hands by closing schools and distributing vouchers (slide 2). The Court held that "the time for mere 'deliberate speed' has run out" and that courts may order officials to levy taxes to reopen schools (slide 2).

Green v County School Board (1968) (9-0) addressed "freedom of choice" in New Kent County, where there was no residential segregation so parents could just pick a school (slide 2). After three years, zero white students chose the Black school and 85% of Black students remained at the all-Black school; the Court held that where you previously operated a dual system, you must take affirmative steps to build a unitary system, and it must actually work -- freedom of choice is a means, not an end (slide 2).

Alexander v Holmes County (1969) killed "all deliberate speed" definitively -- "terminate dual school systems at once" (slide 2). Swann v Charlotte-Mecklenburg (1971) gave district courts broad equitable power -- busing, mathematical ratios, noncontiguous zones -- but Burger limited it to districts with a history of de jure segregation, and the Burger Court began to equivocate, with the de jure/de facto distinction becoming the pivot (slide 2).

### Milliken and the Inter-District Problem

Milliken v Bradley (1974) (5-4) was the first major restriction on remedies (slide 3). The cartoon-map setup: Detroit was about 70% Black, the suburbs ("Whiteland") overwhelmingly white, so rearranging students within Detroit was just "moving the deck chairs on the Titanic" and a fervent integrationist needed to cross the district line (slide 3). But who drew those lines? The state of Michigan, which could change them in a day (slide 3). The holding: before inter-district relief may be ordered, it must first be shown that a constitutional violation in one district produces a significant segregative effect in another, and the Court would not make the suburbs "collateral damage in some grand effort to integrate" (slide 3).

Marshall's dissent: "Unless our children begin to learn together, there is little hope that our people will ever learn to live together," predicting (correctly) that Milliken insulates white flight -- a segregation-minded parent who didn't want kids bused across town would just move to the suburbs (slide 3). The racial dot maps tell the story: Detroit today is 2.5% white with a powder-blue suburban ring; Houston you can almost trace by the freeways -- white west of the loop, Hispanic east of 45, Black south; Milwaukee is one of the most segregated cities in America (slide 3). The average Black student attends a school that is 47% Black, though Black Americans are about 13% of the population (slide 3).

### Intent, Not Impact

Washington v Davis (1976) is flagged as a "Top 20 Supreme Court case" (slide 4). D.C. required police applicants to pass Test 21, a verbal-ability exam with a 40/80 minimum; Black applicants failed at 4x the white rate; there was no evidence D.C. intended to discriminate, the test measured skills police actually need, but the disparate impact was "eminently foreseeable" (slide 4). The plaintiffs' best argument was Griggs v Duke Power (1971): under Title VII, disparate impact alone establishes liability, and maybe that standard should apply to equal protection too -- that argument fails (slide 4).

Why was it too radical? Name a government program that does not differentially affect a particular racial group: income and wealth correlate with race, so virtually every tax, welfare, regulatory, and licensing statute would face strict-scrutiny challenge, and hundreds of innocent regulations would run afoul of equal protection (slide 4). Congress can create statutory disparate-impact remedies -- and did, in Title VII -- but the Court won't constitutionalize that choice (slide 4).

Impact as evidence -- the Bayesian point: if you have control and you see something happen, usually you're happy enough with it; the actual effect of your policy is evidence of intent, because otherwise you'd do something different; a piece of the puzzle, but not enough by itself (slide 4).

Personnel Administrator v Feeney (1979) extended the intent requirement to sex: Massachusetts' veterans' preference overwhelmingly favored men (in the 1970s, not many women in the military), but there was no evidence Massachusetts said "we'll hurt women by enacting a veterans' preference" -- the evidence was that Massachusetts liked veterans, and disproportionate advantage is not enough (slide 4).

### Parents Involved and the Colorblind Constitution

Parents Involved v Seattle (2007) (5-4, Roberts, CJ): "the nice liberals of Seattle" adopted a race-conscious tiebreaker for school assignments voluntarily, not under court order, with the goal of genuine racial balance (slide 5). If you believe in anti-subordination this is wonderful; if you believe in colorblindness this is a horror -- explicitly assigning students by race, with a white kid unable to go to one school and a Black kid unable to go to another (slide 5). Colorblindness wins, with Roberts's tautology: "The way to stop discrimination on the basis of race is to stop discriminating on the basis of race" -- the victory of the colorblind conception of the Constitution, moving from late Rehnquist into the Roberts Court (slide 5).

The voucher parallel: the movement in Texas and elsewhere to depopulate public schools through vouchers and charters echoes the freedom-of-choice era -- nobody's closing schools outright, but the effect may be similar; who knows the intent, but look at the effect (slide 5).

Justice Kennedy's concurrence (controlling) holds that race-aware measures that do not classify individual students may survive (slide 6). The black-letter recap (slide 6): the affirmative duty under Green/Alexander; no inter-district remedy without cross-district violation under Milliken; intent not impact under Washington v Davis/Feeney; no voluntary racial classification under Parents Involved, with "racial balancing" for its own sake patently unconstitutional (slide 6). And the deck closes: "But wait, SFFA is coming" (slide 6).

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

**The Voucher Strategy as State Action Problem.** Prince Edward County's closure-and-voucher plan tested the state action doctrine: if the state writes checks and parents choose segregated private academies, is the resulting segregation the state's? The Court in Griffin held yes -- a district with a history of using state coercive machinery to enforce segregation cannot launder its constitutional obligation by converting public funding to private choice. The drowning-rescue analogy captures the distinction: no general duty to integrate a school system you never segregated, but a duty to undo a harm you created.

**The Bayesian Logic of Disparate Impact.** Washington v Davis frames the evidentiary question as Bayesian updating: if you observe that your policy produces a racially skewed result and you do not change the policy, that pattern is evidence that you wanted the result or at least found it acceptable. But the inference from "acceptable to the actor" to "intended by the actor" requires additional facts. A policy adopted for race-neutral reasons and left in place despite observed skewed results presents a harder inference than a policy adopted in a context saturated with racial animus.

**The Colorblindness Question.** Parents Involved poses the tension between anti-subordination and anti-classification in its sharpest form: a majority-white city voluntarily pursuing integration is struck down because the mechanism -- individual racial classification -- is constitutionally forbidden regardless of purpose. If anti-classification applies symmetrically and regardless of historical context, what work, if any, does the Equal Protection Clause do affirmatively to address historical patterns of segregation that Milliken's inter-district rule made self-perpetuating?

## Key Takeaways

- Brown and the Warren Court imposed an affirmative duty on formerly de jure systems; Milliken's inter-district limitation confined that duty within district boundaries and thereby insulated white flight from constitutional remedy.
- Washington v Davis and Feeney restrict Equal Protection claims to situations of discriminatory purpose, constitutionalizing a high threshold that disparate impact alone cannot cross.
- Parents Involved completes the colorblindness turn: even benign, voluntary, integrative race-conscious measures using individual classification violate Equal Protection.
- Exam note: the state action doctrine is a threshold question that the Resistance exploited (vouchers, private academies) and that remains central to identifying when Equal Protection claims can even be brought.
- The de jure / de facto distinction is the pivot of the post-Milliken era: federal constitutional remedies run only to intentional governmental segregation, leaving de facto segregation produced by residential patterns and private choices beyond constitutional reach.
