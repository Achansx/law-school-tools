## Lecture Outline

### Articles of Confederation and Constitutional Foundation

The session opens by contrasting the Articles of Confederation's structural weaknesses with the Constitution's design: no executive branch, judiciary, or federal taxing power (the federal government depended on the states); no Bill of Rights; and a unanimous-vote requirement to amend the Articles, with supermajorities (9 votes) needed to do basically anything *(slide 2)*. The Federalist Papers are described as marketing documents to sell the Constitution to the states *(slide 2)*.

### Judicial Review and Early Supremacy — Marbury, Martin, McCulloch

The deck packages Marbury, McCulloch, and Gibbons together as showing the power of judicial review where Justices declare laws unconstitutional *(slide 3)*. *Marbury v. Madison* establishes the principle of judicial review with respect to judicial statutes -- Section 13 of the Judiciary Act of 1789 was unconstitutional because it purported to grant SCOTUS original jurisdiction in circumstances not described in Article III; Marshall wanted to decide this case to establish judicial review *(slide 3)*. The deck also includes the right-to-remedy point: just because you have a right to something does not mean the court has a remedy, but the general rule is that for every right there is a remedy *(slide 3)*. *Martin v. Hunter's Lessee* holds that Section 25 of the Judiciary Act is constitutional and that under Article III Congress may grant federal courts appellate jurisdiction over state-court decisions involving constitutional law -- the constitution is "by the people of the US" and is meant to apply in states, not insulting the states because it recognizes what the people want *(slide 4)*. *McCulloch v. Maryland* establishes that "necessary" just means useful or convenient, that N&P must be combined with furthering another enumerated power, and that the creation of the bank is incidental to powers expressly given to the government *(slide 5)*. The deck includes Marshall's "pretext passage" -- the Court can look into the purpose behind what Congress did *(slide 5)*. The intergovernmental tax immunity note: we want to prevent the states from picking on the federal government by picking taxes that discriminate against it *(slide 5)*.

### Commerce Clause — Historical Arc by Era

The review organizes Commerce Clause doctrine across the presenters' five eras *(slide 6)*:

**1801–1835 (Marshall):** Federal power is supreme; the Commerce Clause develops into a broader reach *(slide 6)*. *Gibbons v. Ogden* (1824) holds that the federal coastal license preempted New York's steamboat monopoly, establishing that Congress's power to regulate "commerce" includes the power to regulate navigation and that federal power is supreme over conflicting state laws *(slide 7)*. The deck pairs Gibbons with Prigg v. Pennsylvania (1842) (not CC) as an illustration of federal supremacy *(slide 7)*.

**1890s–1936:** The Court draws a line between state production and national transportation *(slide 6)*. The deck collects Dewitt (1869, "local police regulation" with too-remote effect), E.C. Knight (1895, "manufacture" is distinct from "commerce" and any effect is merely indirect), Champion (1903, the power to regulate includes the power to prohibit transportation of specific items between states even for moral reasons), and Hammer v. Dagenhart (1918, attempting to regulate "local" conditions of manufacture rather than commerce; later overruled) *(slide 8)*. The deck flags an increase in moralistic use of the clause during this era *(slide 8)*. Schechter Poultry (1935) struck down NIRA wage-and-hour regulations because the poultry had "come to a halt" within the state and effects on interstate commerce were only "indirect" *(slide 9)*. The deck pairs Schechter with United States v. Butler (1936, non-CC, first major case to limit spending power because the AAA processing tax was not a true tax and the goal was controlling acreage) *(slide 9)*.

**1937–1994 (New Deal and post-war expansion):** The aggregation principle emerges *(slide 6)*. NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel (1937) holds that Congress may regulate intrastate activities that have such a "close and substantial relation" to interstate commerce that their control is essential to protect that commerce *(slide 9)*. *United States v. Darby* (1941) overrules Hammer, holds that Congress may regulate labor conditions of manufacture because the shipment of those goods is commerce, and treats the Tenth Amendment as a "mere truism" *(slide 10)*. Wickard v. Filburn (1942) extends regulation to purely local non-commercial activity that in the aggregate has a substantial economic effect on interstate commerce *(slide 10)*. *Heart of Atlanta Motel* (1964) and Katzenbach v. McClung (1964) use the Commerce Clause as a vehicle for civil rights, applying rational basis at a low standard *(slide 11)*.

**1995–2005 (Rehnquist):** The Court slams brakes on federal expansion of the CC and establishes three categories, stopping regulation of non-commercial activity *(slide 6)*. *United States v. Lopez* (1995) struck down the Gun-Free School Zones Act because gun possession in a school zone is a non-economic activity that does not "substantially affect" interstate commerce, establishing the three categories of interstate commerce: channels, instrumentalities, and activities that substantially affect *(slide 12)*. United States v. Morrison (2000) extends Lopez: Congress cannot regulate non-economic violent criminal conduct based solely on its aggregate effect on the national economy *(slide 12)*. Gonzales v. Raich (2005) applies Wickard aggregation to homegrown medical marijuana as part of a "class of activities" with substantial effect on the national drug market *(slide 12)*.

**2006–present (Roberts):** Focus on more nuanced aspects of CC -- activity versus inactivity, no coercion of states *(slide 6)*. *NFIB v. Sebelius* (2012) holds that Congress cannot use the Commerce Clause to compel individuals to become active in commerce (activity vs. inactivity), but the mandate survives under the taxing power; the Medicaid expansion is unconstitutionally coercive as a "gun to the head" *(slide 13)*.

**Summary rule:** Congress has authority to regulate (1) channels of interstate commerce, (2) instrumentalities of interstate commerce, and (3) activities that substantially affect interstate commerce, but cannot regulate inactivity or compel individuals to enter the market *(slide 14)*.

### Dormant Commerce Clause

State laws are unconstitutional if they discriminate against interstate commerce by favoring in-state economic interests, regulate commerce occurring entirely outside the state (extraterritorial regulation), or impose burdens that clearly outweigh local benefits under Pike balancing *(slide 15)*. *National Pork Producers Council v. Ross* (2023) holds that Prop 12 does not violate the Dormant Commerce Clause because it applies equally to in-state and out-of-state producers, does not purposefully discriminate, and does not impose a substantial burden -- a state may generally ban the in-state sale of certain consumer products if the state lacks discriminatory intent *(slide 17)*.

### Supremacy Clause — Preemption

The deck distinguishes express preemption (Congress explicitly states federal override), implied preemption (intent inferred), field preemption (comprehensive federal regulation occupies the entire field), and conflict preemption (impossible to comply with both, or state law poses an obstacle to congressional objectives) *(slide 18)*. *Arizona v. United States* (2012): Arizona's provisions making it a state crime to fail to carry federal alien registration documents, criminalizing unauthorized aliens seeking work, and allowing warrantless arrests of suspected removable aliens were preempted; the provision requiring officers to check immigration status during lawful stops was allowed to proceed *(slide 19)*.

### Article IV Privileges and Immunities

A state law violates Article IV P&I when it discriminates against nonresidents with respect to a fundamental right related to national unity, unless the state has a substantial reason and the unequal treatment bears a close relationship to the state's objective *(slide 20)*. *Corfield v. Coryell* identifies the protected set as fundamental rights -- governmental protection, the right to acquire property, the right to liberty, and the right to the pursuit of happiness and safety -- and held that New Jersey's ban on nonresidents gathering oysters/clams was constitutional because P&I does not extend to state-owned resources like fish *(slide 21)*. *Hicklin v. Orbeck* (1978) strikes "Alaska Hire" because its broad discrimination against nonresidents lacks substantial justification and is not closely related to Alaska's unemployment issues *(slide 22)*. *Saenz v. Roe* (1999) holds California's residency-based welfare limits violated PI-IV by penalizing the right to travel: newly arrived citizens must be treated equally to long-term residents *(slide 23)*.

### Tenth Amendment — Anti-Commandeering

The doctrine prohibits the federal government from compelling states or state officials to enforce or administer federal laws *(slide 24)*. *New York v. United States* (1992) holds the take-title provision exceeded congressional authority by coercing states into regulatory action *(slide 25)*. *Printz v. United States* (1997) strikes the Brady Act's interim provision compelling state CLEOs to conduct background checks because it violates dual sovereignty and improperly commandeers state executive power *(slide 26)*. *Hodel v. Virginia Surface Mining* is the middle ground: Congress sets a federal "template" and states either submit a plan meeting federal guidelines to regulate themselves or decline, in which case the federal government regulates them directly -- this is constitutional *(slide 27)*. *Reno v. Condon* (2000) is not commandeering because the law is generally applicable (applies to public and private entities alike), regulates the state as a participant in a market (data) rather than as a sovereign regulator, and does not require states to enact or enforce a federal regulatory program *(slide 28)*.

### Eleventh Amendment — State Sovereign Immunity

The text bars only suits against a state by citizens of another state or foreign citizens *(slide 29)*. *Chisholm v. Georgia* (1793) originally allowed federal jurisdiction over suits "between a state and citizens of another state," holding Georgia could be sued by a South Carolinian for debt; the backlash was so strong Congress and states passed the Eleventh Amendment to undo the decision *(slide 30)*. *Hans v. Louisiana* arose because Louisiana defaulted on $87,500 in bonds issued during Reconstruction and a Louisiana citizen sued Louisiana -- the Eleventh Amendment's text only bars suits by citizens of another state; Justice Bradley's majority read the Eleventh Amendment as "not an exhaustive boundary, but an illustration of a deeper background postulate," holding a state cannot be sued in federal court by its own citizen on a federal claim without its consent *(slide 31)*. The *Ex Parte Young* doctrine permits suit against a state official in their official capacity for prospective injunctive relief; the "double fiction" treats the suit as against the individual for immunity purposes and as state action for constitutionality; cannot get money damages from the state treasury *(slide 32)*. *Whole Woman's Health v. Jackson*: Texas's SB 8 delegated enforcement to the "world at large" via private civil suits, and the Court held the suit could proceed only against state licensing officials *(slide 33)*.

### Article III Standing — The Irreducible Constitutional Minimum

Federal judicial power extends only to "Cases" and "Controversies," with the rationale being separation of powers (no roving judicial commission) and adversarial system (a fair fight between parties with a real stake); standing must be present in every case, with no exceptions *(slide 34)*. The *Lujan* test: (1) Injury-in-Fact (concrete and particularized, not a generalized grievance; actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical); (2) Traceability (fairly traceable to the defendant's challenged action); (3) Redressability (likely, not merely speculative, that a favorable decision will remedy the injury) *(slide 35)*. *Lujan* itself denied standing because intent to visit "some day" without concrete plans (like a plane ticket) is not an imminent injury, and the U.S. was only one of many international funders so pulling U.S. money might not stop the project *(slide 36)*. *Allen v. Wright* (1984) rejects stigma as insufficient unless the plaintiff is personally denied a benefit and finds causation too speculative -- it is unclear if denying tax exemptions would actually force schools to desegregate or parents to return to public schools *(slide 37)*. *Clapper v. Amnesty Int'l* (2013) denies standing because the injury relies on a "highly attenuated chain of possibilities"; the rule: you cannot manufacture standing by incurring costs to avoid a speculative future injury *(slide 38)*. *Massachusetts v. EPA* (2007): states are entitled to "special solicitude" in the standing analysis because they are sovereigns protecting their territory and citizens, with documented harm of 2 inches of sea-level rise already observed *(slide 39)*. *Biden v. Nebraska* (2023): MOHELA is a state-created "instrumentality," so harm to MOHELA's $2/month servicing fees is harm to the State of Missouri *(slide 40)*. *California v. Texas* (2021) denies standing for either individual or state plaintiffs because a moral desire to follow a law that has no penalty for non-compliance is not a legal injury, and the theory that you can challenge Provision A because it is tied to Provision B remains an open, "bizarre" theory *(slide 41)*. *United States v. Texas* (2023) (Kavanaugh, J.) holds states lack Article III standing to contest the Executive's prosecution or arrest policies, given the "Take Care" clause belongs to the Executive and there is no historical tradition of courts ordering more arrests *(slide 42)*. Exceptions to the no-standing-for-underenforcement rule: selective prosecution claims based on Equal Protection, wholesale abdication, and (open question) congressional authorization such as the Laken Riley Act *(slide 43)*. Independent and Adequate State Grounds: the Supreme Court will not review a state court decision that rests on an independent and adequate state law ground, with the Michigan v. Long presumption that if the state court decision is "interwoven" with federal law and lacks a clear statement, SCOTUS assumes it relied on federal law *(slide 44)*.

## Key Takeaways

- The Commerce Clause doctrine is best understood as five distinct doctrinal eras, not a single continuous line.
- The Dormant Commerce Clause and the Anti-Commandeering doctrine are the two main federalism limits on federal power operating in opposite directions — one constrains states, the other constrains Congress.
- The three-part *Lujan* standing test requires concrete, particularized, and actual or imminent injury — generalized grievances and "some day" plans do not satisfy the injury prong.
- The Ex Parte Young doctrine appears repeatedly across State Sovereign Immunity questions.<!-- corrected 2026-05-26: removed phantom cross-reference to Rust v. Sullivan, which is not in the source deck -->
