## Professor Emphasis

### From source

The deck is short — eight slides — and frames *SEC v. Jarkesy* around two questions: what does the Seventh Amendment require for SEC civil-penalty actions, and where does the public-rights doctrine sit after the decision (p. 1). The deck's two key holdings are stated as: civil penalties for securities fraud resemble common-law fraud claims, the Seventh Amendment guarantees a jury-trial right for such claims, and the public-rights doctrine does not apply to cases that involve private rights and penalties (p. 5). The doctrinal upshot is framed as a rule: the government may use non-Article III judges only for public-rights cases, and the key test is whether the case resembles a traditional common-law cause of action (p. 6).

### Editor's framing

This page treats Jarkesy as a separation-of-powers case in Seventh Amendment clothing — the practical effect is to shift certain agency civil-penalty regimes out of in-house adjudication and into Article III courts, but the doctrinal vehicle is the jury-trial right and the public-rights doctrine. The deck does not connect Jarkesy to Humphrey's Executor or Seila Law as agency-design siblings; the Connections section below flags that linkage as editor's framing rather than slide-anchored. The deck also leaves the OSHA/Atlas Roofing question implicit — it does not say Atlas Roofing was overruled, only that the public-rights doctrine does not extend to private-rights penalty actions like Jarkesy's.

## Lecture Outline

### What the SEC is and why it matters here

The SEC was created by the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 and is an independent federal agency tasked with enforcing securities laws and regulating the securities industry (p. 2). The deck flags that recent separation-of-powers disputes have arisen over the structure of independent commissions — challenges to multi-member leadership and removal protections — and cites *Seila Law LLC v. CFPB* (2020) and *Collins v. Yellen* (2021) as recent examples (p. 2).

### The two routes for fraud charges

Before Dodd-Frank, the SEC could impose penalties only in federal court against unregulated parties (p. 3). The Dodd-Frank Act of 2010 expanded the SEC's power to seek civil penalties in administrative proceedings, and the deck notes Dodd-Frank also created the CFPB (which the Supreme Court has trimmed and which the deck says Trump has essentially shut down) (p. 3). The expanded administrative route allowed the SEC to bring cases within its own agency against any defendant, not just regulated entities (p. 3). The deck flags a track-record asymmetry: the SEC won over 90% of in-house cases versus 69% in federal court, raising due-process concerns (p. 3). Post-Jarkesy, the SEC can no longer impose civil penalties in administrative hearings for securities fraud (p. 3).

### The facts and procedural posture

The SEC charged hedge fund manager Jarkesy with securities fraud and used an administrative law judge for adjudication under the Dodd-Frank Act (p. 4). Jarkesy challenged the process, arguing it violated his Seventh Amendment right to a jury trial; the Fifth Circuit ruled in his favor, and the Supreme Court affirmed (p. 4).

### The holding

Three propositions carry the deck's statement of the holding (p. 5):

- Civil penalties for securities fraud resemble common-law fraud claims (p. 5).
- The Seventh Amendment guarantees a right to a jury trial for such claims (p. 5).
- The public-rights doctrine does not apply to cases that involve private rights and penalties (p. 5).

### The public-rights doctrine after Jarkesy

The deck states the post-Jarkesy doctrine in four points (p. 6):

- The government may use non-Article III judges only for public-rights cases (p. 6).
- Public-rights cases involve statutory entitlements or government enforcement actions (p. 6).
- Private-rights cases — fraud, contract disputes — require jury trials (p. 6).
- The key test: does the case resemble a traditional common-law cause of action? (p. 6).

### The two-column Article III / non-Article III map

Slide 7 presents a two-column table sorting case types by whether Article III judges and juries are constitutionally required (p. 7):

- *Require Article III judge and jury:* securities fraud with civil penalties; FTC imposing financial penalties; traditional contract or tort disputes; criminal contempt proceedings (p. 7).
- *Can use non-Article III judge:* immigration deportation cases; Social Security and Medicare disputes; patent validity challenges (inter partes review); bankruptcy proceedings (p. 7).

### A taxonomy of public-rights disputes

Slide 8 surveys the public-rights side of the line in a category-by-category table (p. 8). Government claims include tax disputes, customs enforcement, public land disputes, and government contract disputes (p. 8). Statutory entitlements include Social Security, veterans' benefits, and Medicare reimbursement (p. 8). Regulatory schemes include FCC licensing, EPA enforcement actions, and SEC disciplinary proceedings (p. 8). Bankruptcy proceedings cover certain aspects of bankruptcy adjudication but not state-law tort claims, per *Stern v. Marshall* (p. 8). Intellectual property covers patent-validity challenges before the Patent Trial and Appeal Board, per *Oil States Energy Services* (p. 8). Immigration proceedings cover deportation and asylum claims before immigration judges; military and veterans affairs covers court-martial reviews and veterans' claims before the Board of Veterans' Appeals (p. 8). Labor and employment covers NLRB unfair-labor-practice disputes and workers' compensation for federal employees, per *Crowell v. Benson* (p. 8).

## Key Takeaways

- Jarkesy reframes the constitutional limit on agency adjudication through the Seventh Amendment, not directly through Article III: when the underlying claim resembles a common-law cause of action and seeks civil penalties, the jury-trial right attaches (p. 5).
- The post-Jarkesy public-rights doctrine survives — non-Article III judges can still adjudicate statutory entitlements, government enforcement against the government's own property and revenue, and the historically recognized public-rights categories listed in slide 8 (pp. 6, 8).
- The doctrinal test the deck supplies is the common-law-resemblance test: does the case look like a traditional common-law cause of action? If yes, Article III and the Seventh Amendment attach (p. 6).
- The Article III / non-Article III taxonomy on slide 7 is the operational summary: securities-fraud penalties, FTC financial penalties, traditional contract/tort disputes, and criminal contempt go to Article III courts; immigration, Social Security/Medicare, patent IPR, and bankruptcy can stay with non-Article III adjudicators (p. 7).
- The deck flags but does not develop the agency-structure consequences — track-record asymmetry in administrative versus federal-court proceedings (90% vs. 69%) and the broader project of trimming independent-agency power (pp. 2-3).
