## Lecture Outline

### The New Deal Crisis: Schechter and the Four Horsemen

The deck recalls the Schechter themes -- emergencies do not enlarge constitutional powers (Milligan, Youngstown, Dames & Moore, Guantanamo); the Tenth Amendment is very meaningful; states are important; the Constitution does not require feds to have power to prevent a race to the bottom; and the ability to distinguish direct from indirect *(slide 2)*. *Schechter Poultry* (1935) and *Carter v. Carter Coal* (1936) applied the direct/indirect framework: activities directly affecting interstate commerce could be regulated; activities indirectly affecting it could not.

FDR's signature domestic legislation -- NIRA, AAA, and more -- was ruled unconstitutional; the deck asks students to think about what would have happened had the ACA or the Civil Rights Act of 1964 been ruled unconstitutional *(slide 3)*. The threat to pack the Court got serious as of 1937 with the Judicial Procedures Reform Bill of 1937 *(slide 3)*. The deck asks why we don't see more court-packing today and how it would undercut the Marbury norm of judicial independence *(slide 4)*.

### NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp. (1937): The "Switch in Time"

The National Labor Relations Act regulated unions, collective bargaining, and created a federal agency to enforce; "affecting commerce" was statutorily defined to include burdening, obstructing, or tending to lead to a labor dispute burdening interstate commerce *(slide 5)*. Chief Justice Hughes wrote for the majority -- the deck notes that the Chief's choice to author the majority is a good indicator of an opinion's importance *(slide 5)*.

The deck reproduces the "Saving Construction" passage: courts are not at liberty to deny effect to specific provisions Congress had constitutional power to enact by superimposing ambiguous general declarations; the cardinal principle is to save and not to destroy *(slide 6)*. The Act may be construed to operate within constitutional authority because its terms reach only what burdens or obstructs commerce *(slide 6)*. The deck flags that the saving-construction move recurs in *NFIB* and elsewhere *(slide 6)*.

The deck then quotes the key delicate-balance passage: intrastate activities may be regulated if they have "such a close and substantial relation to interstate commerce that their control is essential or appropriate to protect that commerce from burdens and obstructions," but the power may not extend so far as to "effectually obliterate the distinction between what is national and what is local and create a completely centralized government" -- the question is "necessarily one of degree" *(slide 7)*. The deck notes the hedge was "probably designed to get Justice (Owen) Roberts vote" *(slide 7)*.

The Four Horsemen dissented across consolidated cases, citing *Schechter* and *Carter*: mining brings commerce into existence, commerce disposes of it; a Clothing Company producing less than half of one percent of national output that discharged ten of ten thousand workers could only affect interstate commerce by a chain of indirect and progressively remote events *(slide 8)*. The deck tags this passage for callback at *Morrison* and *Clapper* *(slide 8)*.

### United States v. Darby (1941): The Tenth Amendment as "But a Truism"

*Darby* upheld the Fair Labor Standards Act, which set minimum wages for the production of goods in interstate commerce, in a unanimous opinion by Chief Justice Stone *(slide 9)*. Stone's key passage: the power of Congress over interstate commerce extends to intrastate activities that so affect interstate commerce as to make their regulation appropriate means to the exercise of the commerce power *(slide 9)*. *Hammer v. Dagenhart* was overruled and motive was no longer to matter *(slide 9)*. The deck flags the complete turnover of the Court: Roberts, Black, Reed, Frankfurter, Douglas, Murphy, Byrnes, Jackson *(slide 9)*.

The deck quotes the now-routine statement that, while manufacture is not interstate commerce, the shipment of manufactured goods interstate is such commerce and Congress may prescribe the rule by which commerce is governed -- citing *Gibbons*, the *Lottery Case*, *Hipolite Egg*, and *Kentucky Whip & Collar* *(slide 10)*. On motive: even though the prohibition's purpose is regulation of wages and hours of persons engaged in manufacture, "the motive and purpose of a regulation of interstate commerce are matters for the legislative judgment upon the exercise of which the Constitution places no restriction" *(slide 11)*. Congress may regulate intrastate activities where they have a "substantial effect on interstate commerce"; *Hammer v. Dagenhart* is overruled *(slide 11)*.

The deck reproduces *Darby*'s shocking treatment of the Tenth Amendment: "The amendment states but a truism that all is retained which has not been surrendered"; the amendment was declaratory of the constitutional relationship as it existed before the amendment *(slide 12)*. The deck then asks the construction questions: are other amendments nothing but a truism (First, Second)? what is special about this amendment? this is one of two neutered amendments (the other being the Ninth, killed by *UPW v. Mitchell* (1947)); what principles of construction is "but a truism" consistent with? why would the states that were reluctant to sign the Constitution insist on an amendment that was "but a truism"? *(slide 13)*.

### Wickard v. Filburn (1942): The Aggregation Principle

*Wickard* came under the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938, which imposed quotas on crop production (including crops grown for internal consumption with no sale) on the theory that collectively limiting output raises prices and assures adequate farmer returns *(slide 14)*. Farmer Filburn sowed 23 acres instead of his allotted 11, fed the excess 239 bushels to his hungry livestock, and refused a $117.11 penalty -- about 1/100 millionth of the nation's wheat supply *(slide 14)*. The deck notes that the case is not gluten-free and that productivity per acre has doubled since *(slide 14)*.

The deck's question set on *Wickard*: was there a saving construction (no)? did the direct/indirect test survive (no)? how does Filburn's consumption of his own wheat substantially affect interstate commerce -- "not the right way to look at it"; you look at the aggregate effects under a counterfactual law, and if those effects would affect interstate commerce, "you've got it" *(slide 15)*. Name something Congress cannot regulate in a post-Wickard world? Maybe things that are themselves not "commercial" -- the deck's playful example is daydreaming during class *(slide 15)*.

### United States v. South-Eastern Underwriters (1944)

*South-Eastern Underwriters* (1944) applied federal antitrust laws to the insurance industry, distinguishing *Paul v. Virginia* (1868) and *Deer Lodge County* (1913) and leaving a dormant commerce clause issue *(slide 16)*. The aftermath: McCarran-Ferguson Act (1945) and *Prudential v. Benjamin* (1946); the deck notes that McCarran-Ferguson was amended in January 2021 (CHIRA) to subject health insurers to parts of the antitrust law *(slide 16)*.

### The Post-World War II Baseline

The deck summarizes the post-WWII baseline: Congress has immense power under the Commerce Clause to regulate interstate commerce and intrastate commerce with a substantial effect on it, with "commerce" comprehending agriculture, mining, and intangibles such as information, insurance, and banking *(slide 17)*. Congress can combine Commerce Powers with Necessary and Proper, subject only to McCulloch's convenience test with "proper" doing little work *(slide 17)*. The deck asks: are there any limits? -- and previews tackling segregation, personal behaviors and criminality affecting commerce, pot, gun toting, and rape *(slide 17)*.

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

The lecture repeatedly returns to a thought experiment: name something Congress cannot regulate after Wickard *(slide 15)*. The honest answer is that after the aggregation principle, almost any activity -- if performed in the aggregate by enough people -- can be shown to substantially affect interstate commerce. Activities that are not themselves "commercial" (the deck offers daydreaming as a playful example) might escape *(slide 15)* -- the thread the Rehnquist Court would pick up in *Lopez* (1995) and *Morrison* (2000).

The court-packing episode raises a parallel question: what would have happened if the ACA or the Civil Rights Act of 1964 had been struck down? *(slide 3)*. This page reads the counterfactual as underscoring how consequential the New Deal constitutional revolution was -- not just for commerce regulation, but for the entire New Deal administrative state.

## Key Takeaways

- The New Deal cases replaced the categorical direct/indirect test with a substantial-effects test and, ultimately, the aggregation principle.
- The "saving construction" technique -- reading statutes narrowly to avoid constitutional problems -- first appears prominently in Jones & Laughlin and recurs throughout the course.
- Darby's "Tenth Amendment as truism" language effectively neutralized the amendment as a limit on federal power until the Rehnquist Court partially revived it in New York v. United States (1992) and Printz v. United States (1997).
- Wickard's aggregation principle is the most expansive formulation of Commerce Clause power. It extended congressional authority to wholly local, non-commercial activity based on collective market effects.
- The post-WWII baseline left the Commerce Clause nearly unlimited in scope -- setting up the Rehnquist Court retrenchment as the major subsequent doctrinal event.
- Emergencies do not expand constitutional powers (Schechter); this theme recurs in Youngstown and Dames & Moore.
