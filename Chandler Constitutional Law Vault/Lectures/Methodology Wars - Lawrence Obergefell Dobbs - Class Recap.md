## Lecture Outline

### Lawrence v Texas (2003)

**The Level-of-Generality Move.** Bowers v Hardwick (1986) asked whether there is a right to "homosexual sodomy"; at that level, history condemns it *(slide 2)*. Kennedy in Lawrence reframes the question as "Whether adults are free to engage in private conduct in the exercise of their liberty"; at this level, the autonomy tradition is robust *(slide 2)*. The level-of-generality choice is outcome-determinative: the Glucksberg/Footnote 6 methodology of asking at the most specific level available produces the Bowers result; the Lawrence methodology of asking at the level of the underlying interest produces the opposite.

**The Equal Protection Alternative.** The Texas statute also had an equal protection problem: it criminalized conduct for same-sex partners but not opposite-sex, a sex-based classification analogous to Loving *(slide 2)*. Kennedy went on both grounds -- due process and equal protection -- to foreclose a legislative workaround, because Texas could have rewritten the statute to criminalize sodomy for everyone and relied on prosecutorial discretion *(slide 2)*.

**Strategic Ambiguity.** Kennedy never names a tier of scrutiny, never labels the right "fundamental," and invalidates the statute without slotting the analysis into any established framework *(slide 2)*. Scalia's dissent argues this is rational basis in disguise and that the law wins under rational basis *(slide 2)*. Lawrence overrules Bowers and the methodology becomes "emerging awareness" replacing ancient roots *(slide 2)*.

### Obergefell v Hodges (2015)

Kennedy's majority displaces Glucksberg's "deeply rooted in history and tradition" framework by asking whether four structural principles that justify constitutional protection of marriage apply equally to same-sex couples *(slide 3)*:

(1) **Individual Autonomy.** Choices about marriage shape identity and destiny; the right to personal choice is inherent in the concept of autonomy *(slide 3)*.

(2) **Two-Person Union.** Marriage responds to the universal fear that a lonely person might call out only to find no one there; same-sex couples seek this bond equally *(slide 3)*.

(3) **Safeguarding Children.** Without marriage recognition, children of same-sex couples bear the stigma of knowing their families are somehow lesser *(slide 3)*.

(4) **Social Order Keystone.** Marriage is foundational to the state's social order; no difference exists between same-sex and opposite-sex couples on this principle *(slide 3)*.

Kennedy's methodological move: rather than asking whether same-sex marriage is "deeply rooted" (it obviously isn't), he asks whether the reasons the Constitution protects marriage apply equally to same-sex couples, with due process and equal protection converging *(slide 3)*.

Roberts' dissent: "Celebrate the achievement. But do not celebrate the Constitution. It had nothing to do with it" *(slide 3)*. The methodological critique: the Court is not interpreting a text or a tradition -- it is substituting its own moral judgment for the democratic process.

### Dobbs v Jackson Women's Health Organization (2022)

Alito's majority restores the Glucksberg methodology explicitly: "deeply rooted in history and tradition" plus "careful description" -- the Footnote 6 approach without citing Footnote 6 *(slide 4)*. Abortion has no tradition of protection; most states criminalized it at the Founding and at the time of the Fourteenth Amendment, so Roe and Casey were "egregiously wrong" *(slide 4)*. The standard of review drops from strict scrutiny to rational basis -- the deck calls this "a methodological earthquake" *(slide 4)*.

**Abortion as categorically different?** Alito insists Dobbs does not threaten Griswold, Lawrence, or Obergefell because abortion uniquely involves "potential life" *(slide 4)*. But Thomas concurs and urges the Court to "reconsider all" SDP precedents, calling the Griswold line "an oxymoron" *(slide 4)*. The tension: if the methodology that kills abortion rights is Glucksberg, and Lawrence and Obergefell cannot survive Glucksberg, are the majority's assurances persuasive? The reasoning casts doubt even if the holding does not extend *(slide 4)*. The deck reproduces Alito's "ordered liberty" passage: "Ordered liberty sets limits and defines the boundary between competing interests … but the people of the various States may evaluate those interests differently" *(slide 4)*.

### The Kowalski Hypothetical

The recap devoted five slides to Kowalski, an IQS-style problem on the "frontier of SDP" *(slide 5)*.

**The parties.** David and Marco Kowalski, a married same-sex couple (post-Obergefell), adopted M.S. three years ago at age 14; they are fit, loving, secular parents with no allegation of unfitness and want M.S. to receive full standard chemotherapy *(slide 5)*. M.S., age 17 (turns 18 in September 2026), has aggressive AML leukemia and wants an immunotherapy trial (35-50% survival) over standard chemo (60-70%), citing 70-85% infertility risk; Dr. Chatterjee characterizes her decision as "a values judgment, not a scientific error" *(slide 5)*.

**The Franklin Act.** The State of Franklin's Mature Minor Medical Decisions Act allows a juvenile court to override parental medical decisions if the minor is found mature by clear and convincing evidence; parental views get "substantial weight" but are not dispositive; Judge Barrera authorized M.S.'s refusal *(slide 5)*.

**The constitutional question.** Does the Franklin Act, by permitting override of fit parents' medical decisions, violate the Fourteenth Amendment? *(slide 5)*

**Footnote 6 indeterminacy.** Both sides lose at the most specific level: the parents' right is the right of adoptive parents of three years to compel a mature minor to undergo high-toxicity chemo she has rationally refused (no tradition either way); M.S.'s right is the right of an unemancipated minor to refuse life-saving treatment over fit parents' objection (no tradition either way) *(slide 6)*. Moving up one level, parental authority (Meyer, Pierce, Troxel) meets bodily integrity (Cruzan, common-law battery); both traditions are deep, and Footnote 6 is indeterminate -- a problem it was not designed to handle *(slide 6)*.

**The Obergefell irony.** The Kowalskis' family exists because Obergefell rejected Glucksberg's narrow methodology; M.S. now invokes that same broad reading of liberty against them, and they cannot consistently champion broad SDP for their family and narrow SDP against their child *(slide 6)*.

**The class vote.** Roughly one-third for "leave the statute alone (constitutional)," roughly one-third for "strike it down (unconstitutional)," with undecided as the plurality -- "exactly what a well-designed frontier problem should produce" *(slide 6)*.

**Three analytical frameworks.** Answer A (statute constitutional) names: three interests, not two (the state's parens patriae role recognized in Cruzan as a third force, with Missouri's "unqualified interest in preservation of life" as model) *(slide 7)*; Footnote 6 indeterminacy *(slide 7)*; the anti-Lochner principle (striking down the Act would constitutionalize a particular balance and prevent experimentation -- Dobbs returned the abortion balance to states; the same logic applies) *(slide 7)*; narrow tailoring satisfied (unlike Troxel's "breathtakingly broad" statute, the Act is limited to life-altering decisions, with a full hearing, clear and convincing evidence, independent counsel, and substantial weight to parents) *(slide 7)*. Answer B (statute unconstitutional) names: one fundamental right vs. one developing interest (parental right under Troxel "perhaps the oldest" fundamental liberty interest; M.S.'s interest under Rivera explicitly "not a fully vested fundamental right"; a non-fundamental interest cannot overcome strict scrutiny) *(slide 8)*; Glucksberg kills M.S.'s claim (the mature-minor doctrine is a 20th-century policy innovation, not a constitutional tradition) *(slide 8)*; Dobbs supports the parents because the parental right IS fundamental here, unlike abortion *(slide 8)*; the "revolution problem" that the parental-rights framework of Meyer, Pierce, Troxel, and Yoder unravels if the state can override fit parents whenever a judge finds the child "mature" *(slide 8)*. Answer C (objective memorandum) flags Issue 1 (parental rights under Troxel/Parham/Meyer/Pierce; same-sex adoptive parents hold equal rights under Henderson; Henderson's "duration and depth" language weakens the three-year-adoption Troxel-presumption predicate) *(slide 9)* and Issue 2 (M.S.'s liberty interest under Cruzan; Glucksberg's "careful description"; Rivera's "developing liberty interest"; the level-of-generality problem outcome-determinative) *(slide 9)*. The deck's closing line: "The methodology war is the heart of the problem … this connects directly to Michael H. → Lawrence → Obergefell → Dobbs" *(slide 9)*.

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

**The Kowalski Problem.** See the Lecture Outline above for full analysis. The key exam-technique lessons: (1) identify both sides' rights claims and describe each at the specific and general levels; (2) apply Footnote 6 rigorously even if the result is indeterminacy; (3) identify the anti-Lochner principle as a structural argument for state authority to balance; (4) address the narrow-tailoring question for the Franklin Act under strict scrutiny; (5) note the Obergefell irony explicitly if writing an objective memorandum.

**The Loving Paradox.** If fundamental rights must be deeply rooted in history and tradition at the specific level (Glucksberg/Footnote 6), how is Loving correct? Anti-miscegenation laws were deeply rooted -- 41 states had such laws at various points. Loving is universally accepted as right, yet it cannot be reconciled with a purely backward-looking methodology. This is the strongest case against Footnote 6 as the exclusive methodology -- it proves that history and tradition can themselves be the constitutional problem.

## Key Takeaways

- The level-of-generality choice is outcome-determinative in SDP cases: narrow framing (Footnote 6, Glucksberg, Bowers) almost always produces a "no fundamental right" conclusion; broad framing (Lawrence, Obergefell) almost always finds a protected interest.
- Dobbs restores Glucksberg and rational basis for abortion; Thomas's concurrence signals ongoing pressure on Griswold, Lawrence, and Obergefell.
- The Kowalski problem exemplifies frontier SDP analysis: Footnote 6 is indeterminate, and the outcome turns on which party's right you characterize as the operative fundamental right and what level of scrutiny you apply to the competing interest.
- Exam technique: when Footnote 6 is indeterminate (both parties have plausible fundamental rights claims), identify the anti-Lochner principle as the structural tiebreaker that favors state authority and explicitly engage the narrow tailoring of whatever procedural safeguards the statute provides.
