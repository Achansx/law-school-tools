# Economic Substantive Due Process - From Calder to BMW v Gore

## Professor Emphasis

### From source

The deck opens by foregrounding the Fourteenth Amendment's textual reference to "process" and asking how it became a vehicle for protecting substance, "and specifically, for protecting economic liberty against regulation" *(slide 2)*. The Chase-Iredell debate in *Calder v. Bull* is presented as "the ur-debate" -- natural law limits vs. text-only enforcement -- and tagged as a debate "that has never been settled" that "just keeps reappearing: substantive due process, penumbras, 'deeply rooted traditions,' 'reasoned judgment'" *(slides 3-4)*. The deck calls Calder "the genetic code of substantive due process" -- every subsequent case is a variation on whether courts may police legislation by unwritten principles *(slide 25)*. Holmes's three-paragraph dissent in *Lochner* is flagged as planting "the seed that killed" the doctrine *(slides 8, 25)*. The Same-Term Paradox slide explicitly states that the outcomes are irreconcilable "unless the Court simply values economic liberty more than bodily autonomy" *(slide 11)*. The deck's closing question on BMW is whether it is "Lochner by another name" -- Scalia's dissent says it is, and the deck frames the choice as whether Lochner was wrong about method or wrong about everything *(slides 23-25)*.

## Lecture Outline

**I. The Opening Question**
The Fourteenth Amendment says "due process" -- a procedural concept. How did it become a vehicle for protecting substance? *(slide 2)*. This framing question organizes the entire lecture.

**II. Calder v. Bull (1798) — The Ur-Debate**
The Connecticut legislature set aside a probate decree -- effectively reversing a court judgment by legislative act *(slide 3)*. The Court held the ex post facto clause applied only to criminal statutes, but two Justices used the case to stage a debate that has never ended *(slide 3)*. Justice Chase argued an "ACT of the Legislature (for I cannot call it a law)" contrary to "the great first principles of the social compact" cannot be a rightful exercise of legislative authority *(slide 4)*. Justice Iredell countered that "the ideas of natural justice are regulated by no fixed standard: the ablest and the purest men have differed upon the subject" *(slide 4)*. The debate keeps reappearing in substantive due process, penumbras, "deeply rooted traditions," and "reasoned judgment" *(slide 4)*.

**III. Lochner v. New York (1905) — Constitutionalizing Liberty of Contract**
New York's maximum-hours law for bakers was struck down as an interference with liberty of contract *(slide 5)*. The Peckham majority's framework: start with liberty of contract as part of Fourteenth-Amendment "liberty"; the police-power exception requires a "direct relation" to a legitimate end; the Court reviews independently whether baking is unhealthy enough; and Peckham sees a slippery slope -- if New York can limit bakers' hours, what prevents limiting doctors, lawyers, bank clerks? *(slide 6)*. Two dissents *(slide 7)*: Harlan argued for deference -- bakers are pale-faced, work in flour dust, seldom live past fifty -- placing the burden on the challenger, not the state. Holmes wrote that "the Fourteenth Amendment does not enact Mr. Herbert Spencer's Social Statics," that "a Constitution is not intended to embody a particular economic theory," that "general propositions do not decide concrete cases," and that "a reasonable man might think it a proper measure on the score of health" *(slide 7)*. The deck distills Holmes's dissent into three moves: the Constitution is neutral as to economic theory; abstract principles like liberty of contract cannot resolve concrete cases; and the rational-person test asks only whether no rational and fair person could think the law consistent with our traditions *(slide 8)*.

**IV. Jacobson v. Massachusetts (1905) — The Same-Term Paradox**
Henning Jacobson refused compulsory smallpox vaccination in Cambridge, Massachusetts and was fined five dollars *(slide 9)*. Harlan wrote that "the liberty secured by the Constitution does not import an absolute right in each person to be, at all times and in all circumstances, wholly freed from restraint" *(slide 9)*. The deck distills Jacobson's framework into four moves: liberty is not absolute, community self-defense permits epidemic-protection measures, a statute must bear a "real and substantial relation" to public health or safety, and even a valid police-power exercise can be struck down if "applied arbitrarily to a particular individual" -- an escape valve Harlan reserves for an undefined extreme case *(slide 10)*. The Same-Term Paradox: Lochner (April 1905) holds that liberty of contract trumps state health regulation and that the Court reviews the legislature's factual claims independently, while Jacobson (Feb 1905) upholds compulsory vaccination -- a far more invasive bodily intrusion -- with deference to the legislature; the deck reads the outcomes as irreconcilable "unless the Court simply values economic liberty more than bodily autonomy" *(slide 11)*.

**V. West Coast Hotel Co. v. Parrish (1937) — The Fall**
Elsie Parrish, a chambermaid at a hotel in Wenatchee, Washington, sued to recover the difference between her pay and the state minimum wage *(slide 12)*. The hotel relied on *Adkins v. Children's Hospital* (1923), which had struck down a minimum-wage law for women on liberty-of-contract grounds and blocked minimum-wage legislation for fourteen years -- until the Depression, the New Deal, Roosevelt's court-packing threat, and this case *(slide 12)*. Chief Justice Hughes wrote that "the Constitution does not speak of freedom of contract" and that "regulation which is reasonable in relation to its subject and is adopted in the interests of the community is due process"; the deck also flags Hughes's taxpayer argument that the community is not bound to provide "a subsidy for unconscionable employers" via poor relief *(slide 13)*. Justice Roberts voted to strike down New York's minimum-wage law in *Morehead* (1936) and ten months later voted to sustain Washington's -- a vote that made the majority and that the press called "the switch in time that saved nine" *(slide 14)*. Sutherland's dissent: "The meaning of the Constitution does not change with the ebb and flow of economic events," and an oath of office is "not a composite oath, but an individual one" -- if a judge surrenders his deliberate judgment, "he stands forsworn" *(slide 14)*. Hughes overruled *Adkins* in a single sentence; with *Adkins* gone, the constitutional foundation of Lochner-era wage regulation is destroyed, though the Court does not formally overrule *Lochner* itself *(slide 15)*.

**VI. United States v. Carolene Products (1938) — The Replacement Framework**
A case about shipping adulterated milk across state lines produced the most famous footnote in constitutional law *(slide 16)*. The text of the holding is unremarkable: regulatory legislation affecting ordinary commercial transactions is not unconstitutional unless it cannot be assumed to rest on some rational basis *(slide 16)*. Footnote 4 splits into three paragraphs *(slide 17)*. Paragraph one: there may be "narrower scope for operation of the presumption of constitutionality" when legislation appears on its face to be within a specific Bill of Rights prohibition *(slide 17)*. Paragraph two: legislation that restricts "those political processes which can ordinarily be expected to bring about repeal of undesirable legislation" may require "more exacting judicial scrutiny" *(slide 17)*. Paragraph three: "prejudice against discrete and insular minorities may be a special condition, which tends seriously to curtail the operation of those political processes ordinarily to be relied upon to protect minorities" -- the theoretical foundation for strict scrutiny, suspect classifications, and modern equal protection, all from a footnote about imitation milk *(slide 17)*.

**VII. Williamson v. Lee Optical (1955) — The Epitaph**
Oklahoma made it illegal for an optician to fit old lenses into new frames -- or to replace a broken lens with an identical copy -- without a new prescription from an ophthalmologist *(slide 18)*. Douglas wrote that "the day is gone when this Court uses the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment to strike down state laws, regulatory of business and industrial conditions, because they may be unwise, improvident, or out of harmony with a particular school of thought" *(slide 18)*. The methodological move: Douglas hypothesized that "the legislature might have concluded that the frequency of occasions when a prescription is necessary was sufficient to justify requiring one in every case," or that eye examinations were so critical for detecting latent ailments that every change in frames and every duplication of a lens should be accompanied by a prescription -- reasons the legislature never articulated *(slide 19)*. Under rational basis review, any conceivable justification will do, even one the judges make up after the fact *(slide 19)*. The deck's "burial of economic SDP" timeline reads 1905 → 1937 → 1955: Lochner held liberty of contract fundamental; West Coast Hotel made it qualified; Williamson hypothesizes legislative reasons and declares the remedy for a bad law to be an election, not a lawsuit *(slide 20)*.

**VIII. BMW of North America v. Gore (1996) — The Resurrection**
Dr. Ira Gore bought a new BMW for $40,750 and discovered nine months later that it had been partially repainted before delivery *(slide 21)*. BMW acknowledged a nationwide policy: when predelivery repair cost less than 3% of retail price, the car was repainted and sold as new without disclosure; the cost of repainting Gore's car was $601 *(slide 21)*. An Alabama jury awarded $4,000 compensatory and $4 million punitive; the state supreme court reduced punitive to $2 million, leaving a 500:1 ratio for a cosmetic repair BMW performed for free *(slide 21)*. Stevens's majority articulated three guideposts *(slide 22)*: degree of reprehensibility (BMW's nondisclosure was purely economic -- no safety risk, no vulnerable victim, no isolated malice, at the low end of the scale); the ratio of punitive to compensatory (the 500:1 ratio "must surely raise a suspicious judicial eyebrow"); and comparable civil penalties (Alabama's maximum civil penalty for deceptive trade practices was $2,000 per violation, so the punitive award was 1,000 times the maximum) *(slide 22)*. Scalia's dissent: "I do not regard the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause as a secret repository of substantive guarantees against 'unfairness'"; due process requires process -- a fair hearing, a fair tribunal -- not substantively fair outcomes, and the three guideposts are standardless invitations for judges to substitute their views of reasonable punishment *(slide 23)*. The deck explicitly poses the question: is BMW v. Gore Lochner by another name? *(slide 24)*. Both cases say a state's economic regulation is "unreasonable," both apply a standard the Court invented, and both override a democratic judgment about economic policy *(slide 24)*.

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

**The Scalia Challenge.** The deck explicitly poses the question BMW raises: Scalia says the punitive-damages cases are economic SDP under a new label *(slides 23-25)*. This page expands the response into two paths: either Lochner was wrong about everything -- both method and substance -- and BMW is also wrong, or Lochner was wrong only about which rights deserve heightened protection and BMW is distinguishable because the Due Process Clause has always governed the fairness of civil proceedings.

**The Same-Term Paradox.** If Lochner and Jacobson are both liberty cases decided the same Term, and Jacobson is obviously correct, does that tell us Lochner was decided on an unstated premise? *(slide 11)*. What is that premise? Does the modern Court avoid this problem or simply suppress it?

**Carolene Products Footnote 4 and SDP.** The footnote contemplates heightened review for discrete and insular minorities. Lochner-era cases often protected large corporations. Does Footnote 4 suggest a reformed economic SDP that would protect wage earners and the poor rather than employers? Or does the footnote contemplate only equal protection, not substantive due process?

## Key Takeaways

The Chase-Iredell debate is the genetic code of all substantive due process. Lochner constitutionalized liberty of contract and launched an era of aggressive judicial review of economic regulation; Holmes's three-paragraph dissent planted the seed that killed it. West Coast Hotel buried economic SDP by declaring "freedom of contract" absent from the constitutional text and shifting to a reasonableness standard. Carolene Products Footnote 4 created the replacement architecture: rational basis for economic laws, heightened scrutiny for political process failures and minority prejudice. Williamson wrote the epitaph with post-hoc rational basis review. BMW v. Gore resurrected the corpse in the guise of punitive-damages review; Scalia named it accurately. Whether you agree with Scalia depends on whether you think Lochner was wrong about method or wrong about everything.

## Connections

- **Calder debate** → resurfaces in Griswold's penumbra theory, in the Casey mystery-of-human-life passage, and in the Glucksberg-Casey methodological clash
- **Holmes's rational-person test** → becomes rationality review in Williamson and modern equal protection; anticipates the deferential strand in Harlan's Poe v. Ullman dissent
- **Lochner as negative template** → Douglas in Griswold explicitly distances the opinion from Lochner; Brennan invokes Lochner in Baker v. Carr context to argue that substantive judgment can be principled; every major SDP case after 1937 is written in Lochner's shadow
- **Carolene Products Footnote 4** → direct ancestor of Warren Court equal protection doctrine (Brown, one-person-one-vote), rational basis with bite, and the preferred-freedoms hierarchy
- **BMW v. Gore** → connected to State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance Co. v. Campbell (2003) 10:1 punitive ratio cap; also connected to Scalia's consistent critique of unenumerated substantive rights in cases from Casey through Lawrence
