## Professor Emphasis

### From source

The deck's title-slide framing is literal: a tiny gravitational deflection -- a 5-4 decision by the wrong litigants in the worst possible test case -- nudged the interpretation of the Privileges or Immunities Clause off course by just enough to alter everything that followed, and 150 years later we might still be on the wrong trajectory *(slide 4)*. The deck presents Slaughterhouse as bad plaintiffs making bad law: white butchers led by former Confederate Justice John A. Campbell tried to repurpose Reconstruction amendments to protect their profits against a public health measure, and the Court rightly rejected the hijacking but threw the baby out with the bathwater *(slide 14)*. The deck stages Miller's three arguments (textual, purposivist, structural) and presses what it calls the "core failure" -- Miller scrutinized only one hypothesis, when a proper analysis compares the flaws of competing theories; the deck explicitly invokes "what statisticians call the Naive Bayes approach" *(slides 17, 18)*. The absurdity slide presses the rhetorical question: "Why would anyone fight a civil war ... to protect access to seaports and the right to be rescued on the high seas?" *(slide 18)*. ## Lecture Outline

**I. Two Clauses to Keep Straight**
Article IV, Section 2 requires states to treat out-of-state citizens the same as their own regarding fundamental rights -- an anti-discrimination provision that does not create rights *(slide 3)*. The Fourteenth Amendment's Privileges or Immunities Clause is categorically different: an affirmative prohibition on states from abridging the privileges or immunities of citizens of the United States, intended to constitutionalize fundamental rights against state action *(slide 3)*. Slaughterhouse gutted the latter clause and reduced it to a near-dead letter *(slide 3)*.

**II. Act I — Corfield v. Coryell (1823) and the Catalog of Fundamental Rights**
Justice Bushrod Washington (George Washington's nephew), riding circuit, heard the case of Corfield, a Delaware citizen whose vessel was seized in New Jersey waters for harvesting oysters in violation of an 1820 statute reserving that right to New Jersey citizens *(slide 6)*. Washington confined the Article IV Privileges and Immunities Clause to "those privileges and immunities which are, in their nature, fundamental" -- rights that belong to citizens of all free governments and have been enjoyed by the citizens of the several States *(slide 7)*. His catalog included: protection by government, enjoyment of life and liberty with the right to acquire property, the right to pursue happiness, the right to pass through or reside in other states for trade and professional purposes, habeas corpus, access to courts, the right to hold property, exemption from discriminatory taxes, and the elective franchise *(slide 8)*. The case is treated with the authority of a Supreme Court opinion because of its author, despite being only a circuit court case *(slide 6)*. It contains the seeds of later controversy: Washington never explained how to identify a "fundamental" right (circular reasoning), mixed natural-rights language with positive-law tools, and excluded oyster harvesting as common property while seemingly protecting the right to pursue a trade *(slide 9)*. This ambiguity became the battleground during Reconstruction *(slide 9)*.

**III. Act II — Reconstruction and the 14th Amendment's Promise**
The Thirteenth Amendment (1865) abolished slavery; the Civil Rights Act of 1866 declared all born in the United States to be citizens with equal rights to contract, sue, and own property; the Fourteenth Amendment (1868) constitutionalized these guarantees against the states *(slide 11)*. Senator Jacob Howard explicitly quoted Corfield's list at length in presenting the Amendment to the Senate, and many Republicans understood the Privileges or Immunities Clause as making Corfield's fundamental rights enforceable against state governments *(slide 11)*. The Clause was designed to overrule Dred Scott and secure substantive rights for the newly freed *(slide 11)*.

**IV. Act III — The Slaughter-House Cases (1873): How White Butchers Hijacked the 14th Amendment**
Louisiana granted a 25-year exclusive franchise to the Crescent City Live-Stock Landing and Slaughter-House Company, requiring all butchers in New Orleans to use its facilities and pay its fees *(slide 13)*. The stated purpose was public health -- centralizing slaughtering downstream from the city's water supply *(slide 13)*. About 1,000 independent butchers organized as the Butchers' Benevolent Association to challenge the grant *(slide 13)*. They hired John A. Campbell -- a former Supreme Court Justice who had resigned and served the Confederacy -- to argue their case under the 13th, 14th P or I, due process, and equal protection clauses *(slide 13)*. The deck frames this as bad plaintiffs making bad law: these were not newly freed slaves seeking basic rights but white businessmen trying to repurpose amendments born of the Civil War to protect their profits against a public health measure *(slide 14)*. Miller's 5-4 majority correctly rejected the hijacking but threw the baby out with the bathwater *(slide 14)*. His dual-citizenship trick: the Fourteenth Amendment protects only privileges of national citizenship, defined as a thin, almost trivial list -- access to the seat of government, free access to seaports, protection on the high seas, use of navigable waters, treaty rights, and the right to become a state citizen *(slide 15)*. All fundamental rights -- the right to pursue a trade, property rights, life, liberty, free speech, religion, and everything in Corfield's list -- were classified as privileges of state citizenship, enforceable only by state governments *(slide 15)*.

Miller made three arguments *(slide 17)*. Textually: the Amendment says "citizens of the United States," not "citizens of the States," so its protections must attach only to national citizenship -- but this ignores that the Amendment was designed to make state-level rights federally enforceable *(slide 17)*. Purposivist: the pervading purpose of the Reconstruction Amendments was freeing the slaves, and butchers seeking economic liberty were clearly not the target -- but even if the butchers' case was a bad vehicle, the clause still needed to mean something for the people it was designed to protect *(slide 17)*. Structural: a broad reading would make the Court a "perpetual censor upon all legislation of the States" *(slide 16)*, a legitimate concern -- but Miller never turned the same skepticism on his own thin reading *(slide 17)*. The core failure: Miller scrutinized only one hypothesis; a proper analysis compares the flaws of competing theories *(slide 17)*. The deck presses the absurdity: why would anyone fight a civil war, pass a constitutional amendment, and fundamentally restructure the Union to protect seaport access and high-seas rescue? Did states ever abridge those rights? *(slide 18)* The deck invokes the Naive Bayes approach -- comparing the plausibility of competing hypotheses rather than merely finding fault with one *(slide 18)*. Field's dissent put the point directly: Miller's reading rendered the clause "a vain and idle enactment, which accomplished nothing" *(slide 21)*. Swayne in dissent warned the majority turned what was meant for bread into a stone *(slide 20)*.

**V. Act IV — The Colfax Massacre (Easter Sunday, April 13, 1873)**
Louisiana's disputed 1872 elections produced rival governments claiming every state office *(slide 24)*. In Grant Parish, Black Republicans occupied the courthouse to defend against Democratic seizure *(slide 24)*. On Easter Sunday 1873, approximately 150 armed white men, including ex-Confederates, the White League, and KKK, surrounded the courthouse with a cannon, set the roof on fire, and murdered those who surrendered; many were shot execution-style after they had laid down their arms *(slide 24)*. Between 62 and 153 Black men were killed; bodies were left unburied for weeks; three white attackers died *(slide 24)*. Eric Foner called it the bloodiest single instance of racial carnage in the Reconstruction era *(slide 24)*. State prosecution was impossible: local courts were controlled by those who sympathized with or participated in the massacre, and all-white juries routinely acquitted white perpetrators of racial violence *(slide 25)*. The only avenue was federal prosecution under the Enforcement Act of 1870 *(slide 26)*.

**VI. Act V — United States v. Cruikshank (1876): Closing the Doors**
Three men, including William Cruikshank, were convicted under the Enforcement Act's conspiracy provisions *(slide 26)*. Chief Justice Waite's opinion issued sweeping constitutional holdings that the badly defective indictment did not require *(slide 31)*. On the First Amendment: the right of assembly was intended to operate upon the National government alone, not to limit the action of the States *(slide 28)*. On the Second Amendment: it "means no more than that it shall not be infringed by Congress" *(slide 28)*. On the Fourteenth Amendment: it "adds nothing to the rights of one citizen as against another" -- only a guarantee against State action, not private violence *(slide 28)*. The indictment was also fatally defective for failing to allege the conspiracy was racially motivated *(slide 28)*. Justice Clifford's concurrence would have stopped at the pleading defect; Waite chose the maximally broad constitutional path *(slide 31)*. The deck frames this as the second gravitational deflection: unnecessary, maximally restrictive, and chosen when a narrow ground was available *(slide 31)*. The combined effect of Slaughterhouse and Cruikshank left the freedmen in a legal no-man's land: fundamental rights were state-level privileges, the states were controlled by the people who denied those rights, and the federal government -- which had the will to protect Black citizens -- was told it lacked the power *(slide 32)*. After Cruikshank, white supremacist groups understood they could act with impunity *(slide 32)*.

**VII. Act VI — The Compromise of 1877 and the Long-Term Consequences**
The disputed 1876 presidential election was resolved by the Compromise of 1877: Democrats accepted Hayes's election, and Republicans withdrew the last federal troops from South Carolina and Louisiana, effectively ending Reconstruction and exposing Black citizens to Redeemer rule *(slide 33)*. The White League, the KKK, and Redeemer governments dismantled every vestige of Black political power, and Jim Crow was built on the foundation these cases laid *(slide 32)*.

The textually unmoored workarounds: to apply the Bill of Rights to the states, courts run incorporation through the Due Process Clause -- a clause about procedure pressed into service for substance, with Gitlow (1925) through McDonald (2010) as the century-long workaround *(slide 35)*. For unenumerated rights, courts again use Due Process in its substantive mode -- producing the Lochner → Griswold → Roe → Obergefell → Dobbs rollercoaster *(slide 35)*. Both doctrines are textually homeless because the P or I Clause, which would have given them a proper home, was gutted in 1873 *(slide 36)*. Justice Thomas in McDonald called for reconsidering Slaughterhouse, writing that the P or I Clause "more naturally reads to protect Americans against state intrusion on enumerated and certain unenumerated rights" *(slide 36)*. But 150 years of precedent creates its own gravitational field *(slide 36)*.

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

**Miller's Naive Bayes Problem.** The deck accuses Miller of scrutinizing only one hypothesis (the broad reading) while ignoring the implausibility of his own (the narrow reading) *(slides 17, 18)*. If you were Miller's law clerk and had to compare the two hypotheses, which flaws are larger? The broad reading risks judicial censorship of all state legislation *(slide 16)*; the narrow reading produces a clause that protects seaport access but not the right to earn a living *(slides 15, 18)*. Is there a middle path that avoids both objections?

**The Campbell Problem.** Campbell was a former Confederate justice arguing for civil rights *(slide 13)*. Does the identity of the litigant matter to constitutional interpretation? If a different test case had been brought -- say, a freedman denied the right to pursue a trade in a Southern state -- could the same 5-4 majority have been assembled for the same result? *(slide 22)*

**The Cruikshank Necessity Question.** Justice Clifford's concurrence would have reversed on pleading grounds without reaching the constitutional questions, but Waite chose the broad constitutional path *(slide 31)*. Was there any legitimate judicial reason for Waite to go further? Or was this a pure power move dressed in procedural clothes? Compare to how the Court sometimes avoids constitutional questions as a virtue of judicial restraint.

**Thomas's Proposal.** Justice Thomas in McDonald (2010) argues for returning to the P or I Clause *(slide 36)*. What would change if Slaughterhouse were overruled? Would incorporation doctrine need to be rebuilt from scratch? Would SDP be affected? Is this realistic after 150 years, or is the asteroid now in an orbit too stable to redirect? *(slide 36)*

## Key Takeaways

Corfield v. Coryell provided the foundational catalog of fundamental rights—citizenship rights that attach to all free persons and should be protected against state abridgment. The Fourteenth Amendment's Privileges or Immunities Clause was designed to federalize this protection against the states. Slaughterhouse Cases gutted the Clause in a 5-4 decision driven by bad litigants in the worst possible test case—Miller's dual-citizenship trick reduced national privileges to a trivial list while leaving fundamental rights solely with state governments. Cruikshank completed the destruction by announcing the Bill of Rights does not apply to states and the Fourteenth Amendment cannot reach private violence. The Colfax Massacre illustrated the human cost: federal prosecution failed, state prosecution was impossible, and the perpetrators of mass murder went free. The constitutional legacy is two generations of textually unmoored workarounds—incorporation running through Due Process, unenumerated rights through SDP—all because the correct textual home was destroyed in 1873. Justice Thomas's call to reconsider Slaughterhouse remains the only path back to textual moorings, but faces 150 years of precedent.
