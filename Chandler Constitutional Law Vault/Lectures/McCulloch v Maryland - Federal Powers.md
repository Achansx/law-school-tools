## Professor Emphasis

### From source

The deck opens by labeling McCulloch "a dominant case" -- the case the Supreme Court itself cites most often and the "eigencase" *(slide 2)*. The facts are skeletal: 1816 National Bank created (again); 1817 Maryland taxes the national bank; McCulloch says no *(slide 3)*. The deck poses the constitutional question -- where does Congress get power to establish a bank, explicitly or implicitly -- and flags that the Necessary and Proper Clause is "adverbial" *(slide 4)*. A pros-and-cons slide weighs implicit power: pros (unrealistic to operate without it; consistent with framers' intent; makes the Constitution adaptable) versus cons (undercuts the 10th Amendment; open to interpretation and usurpation; the same adaptability cuts both ways) *(slide 5)*. Marshall draws the line between "necessary means convenient" (linguistic + practicality + the if-A-or-B-or-C-could-help-then-none-is-necessary argument) and "necessary means truly necessary" (linguistic + 10th Amendment) *(slide 6)*.

The deck quotes Marshall's key passage in full: "Let the end be legitimate, let it be within the scope of the Constitution, and all means which are appropriate, which are plainly adapted to that end, which are not prohibited, but consist with the letter and spirit of the Constitution, are Constitutional" *(slide 7)*. The "Adaptive Theme" slide reproduces Marshall's "Constitution intended to endure for ages to come, and consequently to be adapted to the various crises of human affairs" passage in full, ending with "we must never forget that it is a constitution we are expounding" *(slide 8)*. The pretext passage is reproduced verbatim and flagged with two cross-references: "See if this holds up in Dagenhart; see if this holds up in Darby; ask if this passage still has life" *(slide 9)*.

The deck enumerates Marshall's arguments of interpretation: course of dealings / tradition; reliance; extensive deliberations by legislature; lower scrutiny for power issues than liberty issues; deletion of "expressly" from the 10th Amendment matters *(slide 10)*. Course of dealings: "we've had a bank before"; Marshall says a doubtful question in which the great principles of liberty are not concerned ought to receive a considerable impression from the practice of the Government, with the deck asking whether Congress (or states) can deny newly conceived rights on this theory *(slide 11)*. Reliance: "An exposition of the constitution, deliberately established by legislative acts, on the faith of which an immense property has been advanced, ought not to be lightly disregarded," with the deck flagging a link to Dobbs and the modern stare-decisis reliance-interest analogue *(slide 12)*. Multiple-choice prompts on tradition's weight ("a good amount (the Marshall answer & the current answer)") and the "Stevens theme" of countervailing-interest weighting *(slide 13)*. Process matters too: Marshall's "the bill for incorporating the Bank of the United States did not steal upon an unsuspecting legislature and pass unobserved" passage; plus 9 days of argument in the Supreme Court when big cases today get 2 hours *(slide 14)*. A multiple-choice on quality-of-debate weight *(slide 15)*.

Marshall downplays the 10th Amendment because the framers omitted "expressly," leaving the delegation/prohibition question to "depend on a fair construction of the whole instrument" -- the deck asks whether it is appropriate to question the motives of the three-fourths of states that signed off on the 10th Amendment *(slide 16)*. The deck flags that "proper" gets little judicial attention until NFIB v. Sebelius (2012) *(slide 17)*. The tax-immunity holding is reproduced: if states may tax one instrument they may tax the mail, mint, patent rights, custom-house papers, judicial process -- to an excess that would defeat all ends of Government *(slide 18)*. The deck adds a ChatGPT verse rendition of the Adaptive Theme *(slide 19)*.

McCulloch's aftermath: Madison criticized the opinion in a letter to Spencer Roane -- overbreadth, weakness of the pretext passage, "I was there: this isn't what we meant" -- ending with the "reasonable medium between expounding the Constitution with the strictness of a penal law … and expounding it with a laxity which may vary its essential character" passage *(slide 20)*. The deck lists residual matters: again the origin myth; "why it matters: Term Limits v. Thornton"; "Necessary and proper"; intergovernmental tax immunities; aftermath *(slide 21)*. The origin-myth slide draws an explicit parallel to Martin v. Hunter's Lessee, "the case from which Marshall recused himself" *(slide 22)*. The deck asks how the student would write a dissent from McCulloch *(slide 23)* and reproduces the "no political dreamer was ever wild enough to think of breaking down the lines which separate the States" passage that frames the people-acting-in-conventions argument *(slide 24)*. U.S. Term Limits v. Thornton (1995) is presented as the modern echo: Stevens for the Court, with Story's "the states can exercise no powers whatsoever, which exclusively spring out of the existence of the national government" and Marshall's "government of the union … is, emphatically, and truly, a government of the people" passages reproduced *(slide 25)*. The deck closes by asking whether states should be able to bar Donald Trump from the 2024 ballot *(slide 26)*.

### Editor's framing

This page treats McCulloch as the doctrinal and rhetorical architecture of an expansive, adaptive federal government -- an editorial gloss the deck supports through its "dominant case" framing *(slide 2)* and the explicit "Adaptive Theme" *(slide 8)*. The framing of the pretext passage as a "limiting principle that outside observers often overlook" is editorial sharpening of the deck's open-ended "ask if this passage still has life" prompt *(slide 9)*. Third, the pretext passage. The deck reproduces Marshall's concession: "Should Congress, in the execution of its powers, adopt measures which are prohibited by the Constitution, or should Congress, under the pretext of executing its powers, pass laws for the accomplishment of objects not intrusted to the Government, it would become the painful duty of this tribunal … to say that such an act was not the law of the land" *(slide 9)*. The deck cross-references Dagenhart and Darby to ask whether the passage still has life *(slide 9)*.

Reliance: Marshall's "exposition of the constitution, deliberately established by legislative acts, on the faith of which an immense property has been advanced, ought not to be lightly disregarded" passage, with a Dobbs cross-reference for the modern reliance-interest analogue *(slide 12)*. Multiple-choice prompts on how much weight courts should give tradition (the deck labels "a good amount" the Marshall and current answer) and on the "Stevens theme" of weighing the countervailing interest *(slide 13)*. Fourth, the 10th Amendment: Marshall downplays it because the framers omitted "expressly," leaving delegation/prohibition to "a fair construction of the whole instrument" *(slide 16)*. The deck flags that "proper" gets little attention until NFIB v. Sebelius (2012) *(slide 17)*.

The lecture closes with U.S. Term Limits v Thornton (1995) as the modern echo. ## Key Takeaways

- The Constitution was ratified by the people, not the states, which forecloses the compact theory and supports a national government acting directly on individuals.
- "Necessary and proper" means "convenient, useful, or conducive to" an enumerated end, not "absolutely indispensable."
- The pretext principle operates as a limit in theory even if rarely triggered in practice.
- States cannot tax the operations of federal instrumentalities acting within their legitimate sphere.
- McCulloch's method is the template for every later expansion of federal power, from Gibbons v Ogden (1824) to Wickard v Filburn (1942) to Gonzales v Raich (2005).
