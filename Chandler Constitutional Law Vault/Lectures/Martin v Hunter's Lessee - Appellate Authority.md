## Professor Emphasis

### From source

The deck spends substantial time on the Northern Neck land dispute -- King Charles II's grant after the War of the Three Kingdoms ending up in the 5th and 6th Lord Fairfax's hands, with the estate run like a government with "quitrents" as property taxes, complicated by surveying difficulties and conflicting Virginia laws *(slide 2)*. The estimated value of the land is flagged at roughly $1 trillion in today's dollars *(slide 3)*. The two sets of interests are spelled out: those deriving title from the Fairfax line (including British nephew Denny Martin/Fairfax and "an enterprising young barrister named John Marshall who leverages family money to buy a 200,000 acre tract") versus those deriving title from Virginia (including David Hunter) *(slide 4)*. The deck flags the common-law alien-takes-no-title rule confirmed by *Dawson's Lessee v. Godfrey* (1808) *(slide 5)*, with the Treaty of Paris 1783-84 and Jay's Treaty 1794 as cutting the other way *(slide 6)*. The deck describes Marshall as "right in the middle both as attorney for the Fairfax interests, owner of considerable chunks of the Fairfax lands … and as politican and then judge" *(slide 7)*. *Fairfax's Devisee v. Hunter's Lessee* (1812/1813) is presented as a Story opinion finding Virginia courts erred in their understanding of Virginia laws -- the deck flags this as a "VERY RARE" move citing Mullaney v. Wilbur footnote 9 -- and notes Marshall's recusal "because his fortune is tied up in the resolution of the case" *(slide 8)*. Then Virginia courts REFUSE to obey the Supreme Court mandate, with the deck reproducing the argument that Congress can authorize removal but cannot grant appellate jurisdiction over final state-court judgments *(slide 9)*.

The deck steps back to a "Big Picture Problem" slide: states can oppress people by violating natural law rights, state positive rights, or federal rights (Constitution, treaties, federal statutes, regulations) *(slide 10)*. It walks through an "overlapping menu" of how to protect federal rights against state incursion -- complete Article III arising-under jurisdiction, expansive removal, Motleyesque jurisdiction, review by some federal court of state-court federal rulings, Supreme Court review only, or none -- and notes the closely related issue of binding-authority doctrine (res judicata, collateral estoppel, stare decisis) *(slide 12)*. The Judiciary Act of 1789 did not vest complete arising-under jurisdiction in federal courts; Sections 9-13, 22, 25 give jurisdiction *(slide 13)*. The deck reproduces Section 25 in detail: it covers final judgments or decrees from the highest state court of law or equity in which a decision could be had *(slide 15)*, for three specific case categories where federal validity or construction is drawn in question *(slide 16)*, with the consequence being that the case "may be re-examined and reversed or affirmed in the Supreme Court of the United States upon a writ of error" *(slide 17)*.

The case posture is then set up: writ of error from the Virginia Court of Appeals; the Supreme Court allegedly had no jurisdiction in Fairfax's devisee because Section 25 of the Judiciary Act of 1789 was unconstitutional -- "just like section 13 in Marbury" *(slide 18)*. The deck flags that Story's opinion is "less convoluted and strange when seen as a response to Virginia with Cabell and Roane" and notes "an unbelievable amount of money tied up in its resolution" *(slide 18)*.

The Cabell/Roane view is presented in three slides. First, the compact theory: an instead-of-requisition government of power operating on individuals; symmetry that states can't void federal mistakes and so the federal government can't object if states encroach on federal jurisdiction *(slide 19)*. Second, the "Apocalypse Theme" passage: the Constitution contemplates the independence of both governments and provides "no umpire" -- giving the general government direct controlling operation upon state departments "as such" would extinguish state-authority independence and "would, sooner or later terminate in an entire consolidation of the states into one complete national sovereignty"; the deck notes this language is "very similar to that used by the Anti-Federalists" *(slide 20)*. Third, Cabell's Supremacy-Clause rejoinder: subjection to the Constitution does not mean subjection to the Federal Courts -- state judges decide what the Constitution and laws and treaties are, "according to their own judgments, and upon their own responsibility" *(slide 21)*. Roane's dismissal of the Federalist Papers as "a mere newspaper publication, written in the heat and hurry of the battle" follows *(slide 22)*, along with Roane sounding the 10th Amendment theme via Vattel's federal-republic maxim *(slide 23)*. The deck then quotes Roane's "amendment theme" answer to the uniformity-of-federal-law objection: "the defects of our system of government must be remedied, not by the judiciary, but by the sovereign power of the people" *(slide 24)*.

The deck then turns to the Story Creation Story: the Constitution was "ordained and established not by the States in their sovereign capacities, but emphatically, as the preamble of the Constitution declares, by 'the people of the United States,'" and the powers of state governments depend on their own Constitutions, remaining unaltered except as granted to the Government of the United States *(slide 26)*. The "Adaptive Theme" follows: the Constitution unavoidably deals in general language and the framers left the legislature to "mould and model the exercise of its powers as its own wisdom and the public interests, should require" *(slide 27)*. Story's arguments to support Section 25's constitutionality are catalogued: appellate power not limited to federal courts; plain meaning of Article III; Congress need not create lower federal courts (yet the Constitution contemplates appellate jurisdiction); striking Section 25 would leave federal rights vulnerable; removal is an imperfect solution; the Constitution does act on states in big ways; state oath is great but prejudices persist; uniformity is needed *(slide 28)*. The settled doctrine: Congress may constitutionally grant federal courts appellate jurisdiction over state-court decisions involving constitutional law in civil cases (Martin) and in criminal cases (Cohens v. Virginia) -- the deck flags Cohens's lottery-control facts as a fascinating case about whether Virginia's anti-competing-lottery statute obstructs federal policy *(slide 29)*.

## Lecture Outline

The lecture opens with the Northern Neck land dispute, which traces back to King Charles II's grant during the War of the Three Kingdoms and ended up in the 5th and 6th Lord Fairfax's hands, with the estate run almost as a government with "quitrents" serving as property taxes *(slide 2)*. The deck flags the estimated land value at roughly $1 trillion in today's dollars *(slide 3)*. Two sets of interests emerge: those deriving title from the Fairfax line (including British nephew Denny Martin/Fairfax and "an enterprising young barrister named John Marshall who leverages family money to buy a 200,000 acre tract from the Fairfax line") versus those deriving title from Virginia (including David Hunter) *(slide 4)*. Virginia was unreceptive to property claims of aliens like Denny Martin; the common-law alien-takes-no-title rule was confirmed in *Dawson's Lessee v. Godfrey* (1808) *(slide 5)*. But the Treaty of Paris 1783-84 and Jay's Treaty 1794 cut the other way *(slide 6)*, and the deck describes Marshall as "right in the middle both as attorney for the Fairfax interests, owner of considerable chunks of the Fairfax lands … and as politican and then judge" *(slide 7)*.

The case's first Supreme Court appearance was *Fairfax's Devisee v Hunter's Lessee* (1812/1813), where Justice Story found that the Virginia courts had erred in their understanding of Virginia laws -- the deck flags this as a "VERY RARE" move and cites Mullaney v. Wilbur, 421 U.S. 684, 691 n.9 (1975) as a citation if one ever needs it *(slide 8)*. Marshall recused "because his fortune is tied up in the resolution of the case," and Jay's Treaty protected the Fairfax interests; the deck reproduces the remand order directing the Virginia Court of Appeals to enter judgment for Philip Martin *(slide 8)*. The Virginia courts then refused to obey the Supreme Court mandate, with the deck reproducing the argument that Congress can authorize removal of pending federal-question cases but cannot grant appellate jurisdiction over final state-court judgments *(slide 9)*. The Virginia Court of Appeals (the Cabell/Roane view) held that Section 25 of the Judiciary Act of 1789, which authorized Supreme Court review of state-court judgments on federal-law questions, was unconstitutional -- "just like section 13 in Marbury" *(slide 18)*.

The Cabell/Roane compact theory is presented in three steps. First, an "instead-of-requisition government of power" operating on individuals, with symmetry such that the federal government cannot object if states encroach on federal jurisdiction *(slide 19)*. Second, the "Apocalypse Theme" passage that the Constitution contemplates the independence of both governments and provides "no umpire," and that giving the general government a "direct and controlling operation upon the state departments, as such, would be to change at once, the whole character of our system" -- with the deck noting that this language is "very similar to that used by the Anti-Federalists" *(slide 20)*. Third, Cabell's Supremacy-Clause rejoinder that subjection to the Constitution does not mean subjection to the Federal Courts: what the Constitution, laws, and treaties are "must, in cases coming before the State Courts, be decided by the State Judges, according to their own judgments, and upon their own responsibility" *(slide 21)*. Roane dismisses the Federalist Papers as "a mere newspaper publication, written in the heat and hurry of the battle" *(slide 22)*; sounds a 10th-Amendment theme via Vattel's federal-republic maxim *(slide 23)*; and accepts the uniformity-of-federal-law objection only to answer that "the defects of our system of government must be remedied, not by the judiciary, but by the sovereign power of the people" *(slide 24)*.

The Supreme Court took the case on a second writ of error. Justice Story wrote for the Court; Marshall was recused. Story's argument proceeds structurally through the "Story Creation Story": the Constitution was "ordained and established not by the States in their sovereign capacities, but emphatically, as the preamble of the Constitution declares, by 'the people of the United States,'" and state-government powers depend on their own Constitutions, remaining unaltered except as granted to the Government of the United States *(slide 26)*. The "Adaptive Theme" follows: the Constitution unavoidably deals in general language, leaving the legislature to "mould and model the exercise of its powers" as the public interest requires *(slide 27)*. Story's seven-point catalogue supporting Section 25's constitutionality: appellate power not limited to federal courts; plain meaning of Article III; Congress need not create lower federal courts yet the Constitution contemplates appellate jurisdiction; striking Section 25 would leave federal rights vulnerable; removal is an imperfect solution; the Constitution does act on states in big ways; the state oath is great but prejudices persist; uniformity is needed *(slide 28)*.

The lecture closes with doctrinal projection. The deck flags two settled propositions: Congress may constitutionally grant federal courts appellate jurisdiction over state-court decisions involving constitutional law in civil cases (Martin) and in criminal cases (Cohens v. Virginia, with the Cohen brothers selling federal lottery tickets in Virginia despite a Virginia anti-competing-lottery statute) *(slide 29)*. McCulloch v Maryland (1819) is the Marshall-era federalism companion. The incorporation doctrine, every preemption case, and every dormant-commerce ruling rests on Martin's premise that the Supreme Court is the final word on federal law regardless of which court below ruled first. The modern adequate-and-independent-state-ground doctrine is the limiting principle that preserves state-law autonomy while leaving Martin's federal-question holding intact.

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

**Hypothetical 1 (Core Martin territory): State court departs from federal precedent on federal-law question.** A state supreme court holds that a federal environmental statute does not preempt a particular state regulation, on an interpretation of the federal statute that squarely contradicts recent Supreme Court precedent. The losing party petitions for certiorari. Reasoning: Martin squarely authorizes Supreme Court review. The case falls within Article III because it arises under a law of the United States; Story's case-not-court principle means the state court of origin does not insulate the federal question from federal appellate review. Granting cert and reversing would be the routine application of Martin. The hypo clarifies that Martin's main work is daily and mundane: most Supreme Court review of state-court federal rulings is uncontroversial because Martin has been settled law for two centuries.

**Hypothetical 2 (Adequate-and-independent state ground): State court rests on federal and state grounds.** A state supreme court reverses a criminal conviction on two independent grounds: first, under a state-constitutional right against unreasonable searches that it says is broader than the Fourth Amendment, and second, under the Fourth Amendment itself. The state seeks Supreme Court review. Reasoning: the modern adequate-and-independent-state-ground doctrine (Michigan v Long, 1983) limits Martin at its edges. If the state-constitutional ground is genuinely adequate (sufficient on its own to sustain the judgment) and independent (not interwoven with the federal analysis), the Supreme Court will decline review because any ruling on the federal question would be an advisory opinion. The hypo shows that Martin's reach has been refined rather than narrowed: the Court reviews state-court federal rulings when doing so would change the outcome, not when the state ground is doing all the work.

**Hypothetical 3 (Outside Martin): Pure state-law dispute.** A state supreme court decides a state-law contract dispute with no federal dimension and rejects a losing party's effort to invoke the Supreme Court's appellate jurisdiction. Reasoning: Martin's jurisdictional grant is limited to cases arising under the Constitution, laws, or treaties of the United States, which is the Article III category Section 25 tracks. A pure state-law contract question is not such a case. The hypo makes the textual limit of Martin visible: it is not a universal federal appellate power, it is a federal-question-specific power. This is what preserves state-law autonomy in the post-Martin system.

**Hypothetical 4 (Modern revival of Roane's compact theory): State legislative resolution purporting to bar SCOTUS review.** A state legislature passes a resolution declaring that the state will not recognize any Supreme Court ruling reversing state courts on the interpretation of a particular federal regulation. A state court, following the resolution, refuses to accept the Supreme Court's mandate. Reasoning: this is Martin's facts again at two centuries' remove. Judge Roane's compact theory has been definitively rejected; the resolution has no legal force, and federal marshals can enforce the Supreme Court's mandate directly. The hypo exists so students see that Martin's holding is not historical trivia but live law: the modern equivalents of state-sovereignty resistance to federal appellate authority arise periodically (most famously Cooper v Aaron in 1958), and Martin supplies the doctrinal premise that closes them down.

**Hypothetical 5 (Fence-sitter, Marbury comparison): Federal statute directing state-court procedure.** Congress passes a statute directing state courts to use a specific evidentiary rule in any state proceeding adjudicating a federal claim. A state supreme court refuses to apply the rule on federalism grounds. Reasoning: this mixes Martin's federal-question-review holding with a separate anti-commandeering question. Martin authorizes Supreme Court review of the state court's federal-law ruling; it does not settle whether Congress has the power to commandeer state judicial procedure in the first place. The hypo separates two questions that are easy to conflate: Martin is about review, not about the scope of Congress's affirmative power to direct state courts. This doctrinal separation is one of the most useful teaching points the case offers.

## Key Takeaways

- Section 25 of the Judiciary Act of 1789, authorizing Supreme Court review of state-court judgments on federal-law questions, is constitutional.
- Story's case-not-court principle: Article III's judicial power extends to categories of cases, not to particular tribunals. Once a case falls within Article III, the tribunal of origin does not remove it from federal appellate reach.
- The Supremacy Clause and the need for uniformity of federal law together require Supreme Court review of state-court federal rulings.
- Rejects the compact theory of state-court coequality, which Judge Spencer Roane pressed for Virginia.
- Marshall's recusal underscores that Marshall Court federalism is an institutional project, not a personal one. Story's independent authorship has the same outcome.
- Martin is the structural predicate for every incorporation, preemption, and dormant-commerce case. Without it, Marbury reaches only federal-court judgments.
- The modern adequate-and-independent-state-ground doctrine is the limiting principle that preserves state-law autonomy while leaving Martin's holding intact.

Every incorporation decision presupposes Martin: the Supreme Court can bind state courts on federal constitutional questions, which is exactly what incorporation does when it applies a Bill of Rights guarantee to the states.

**Doctrinal placement.** Marbury and Martin are the twin foundations of the Marshall Court's judicial-power architecture, and they are often mistakenly described as though they do the same work. They do not. Marbury is vertical within the federal system: it establishes the Supreme Court's authority to review acts of Congress against the Constitution. Martin is horizontal between the federal and state systems: it establishes the Supreme Court's authority to review state-court judgments on federal-law questions against federal law. The two together are what make the United States Constitution a single legal instrument with a single authoritative expositor, rather than fifty different bodies of federal constitutional law. Every later structural ruling (McCulloch's federalism, the Incorporation Doctrine, every preemption case, every dormant-commerce case, every Bush v Gore) rests on the combined premise. Judge Spencer Roane's compact theory of state-court coequality is the path not taken, and it resurfaces periodically in nullification, secession, and modern states-rights arguments. Martin is the case that closes off that path.
