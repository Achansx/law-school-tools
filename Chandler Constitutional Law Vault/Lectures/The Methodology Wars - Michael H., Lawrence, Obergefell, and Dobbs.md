### From source

The deck's central thesis is that methodology is outcome-determinative: how we identify fundamental rights determines which rights exist *(slide 19)*. The level at which a Justice describes the right -- specifically (Footnote 6) or broadly (Lawrence) -- determines whether tradition supports or condemns it *(slides 3, 7)*. Footnote 6 is the methodological core of Michael H., but Scalia kept it and accepted only a plurality rather than drop it to secure a majority, because he believed determinacy was worth more than a fifth vote *(slide 4)*. Brennan's response is the circularity problem: if the existence of a tradition denying a right is the basis for concluding the right doesn't exist, then oppression becomes self-justifying *(slide 5)*. Dobbs restores Glucksberg, returning to the most-specific-tradition Footnote 6 approach without citing Footnote 6 *(slide 13)*. ## Lecture Outline

**I. The Level-of-Generality Problem Made Explicit — Michael H. v. Gerald D. (1989)**
Michael H. is the biological father of Victoria D.; blood tests confirm 98.07% certainty of paternity *(slide 2)*. But Victoria was born to Carole D., who was married to Gerald D. *(slide 2)*. California law imposed a conclusive presumption that a child born to a married woman living with her husband is the husband's child, unrebutted by blood-test evidence after two years *(slide 2)*. Michael lived with Victoria, held himself out as her father, and sought parental rights *(slide 2)*. The Court voted 5-4 to deny Michael's claim *(slide 2)*.

Scalia wrote for the plurality and only two Justices (Scalia + Rehnquist) joined Footnote 6 -- the most important methodological statement in modern SDP *(slide 2)*. O'Connor and Stevens concurred in the judgment but refused to join the footnote, which left the holding subject to the Marks rule *(slide 2)*.

**Footnote 6** (Scalia, joined only by Rehnquist): Courts must "refer to the most specific level at which a relevant tradition protecting, or denying protection to, the asserted right can be identified" *(slide 3)*. Don't ask whether parenthood is protected; ask whether there is a tradition of protecting biological fathers of children adulterously conceived *(slide 3)*. Without this specificity rule, Scalia argues, tradition-based analysis is indeterminate -- different Justices pick different levels of generality and reach opposite results *(slide 3)*.

Scalia's strategic sacrifice: he could have secured a majority by dropping Footnote 6, but he kept it and accepted a plurality because he believed the methodological principle was more important than the vote count *(slide 4)*. O'Connor refused to join Footnote 6 because it "sketches a mode of historical analysis that may be somewhat inconsistent with our past decisions" -- too rigid *(slide 4)*.

Brennan's response (the circularity problem): if the existence of a tradition denying a right is the basis for concluding the right doesn't exist, then almost every liberty interest the Court has recognized under the Fourteenth Amendment would fail scrutiny *(slide 5)*. Brennan provides three examples: Griswold (Connecticut criminalized contraceptive use), Eisenstadt (laws banned unmarried access to contraception), Stanley v. Illinois (no tradition of hearing rights for unwed fathers) *(slide 5)*. The paradox: the more successfully a state has repressed a right, the less constitutional protection it receives -- oppression becomes self-justifying *(slide 5)*.

**The Marks Rule Applied.** Under Marks v. United States (1977), when no single rationale enjoys five-Justice assent, the holding is the position taken on the narrowest grounds *(slide 6)*. The outcome (Michael loses) is settled law; the methodology (Footnote 6's specificity rule) is NOT binding precedent *(slide 6)*. If it were binding, every SDP claim would be evaluated at the most specific tradition and most claims would fail; because it is not binding, the level-of-generality question remains open -- the engine of the methodology wars that follow *(slide 6)*.

**II. The Level-of-Generality Move — Lawrence v. Texas (2003)**
*Bowers v. Hardwick* (1986) had asked "whether the Federal Constitution confers a fundamental right upon homosexuals to engage in sodomy" *(slide 7)*. At that specific level, the tradition of condemnation was ancient and universal -- all 50 states had sodomy laws until 1961, and the claim was "at best, facetious" *(slide 7)*.

Kennedy's *Lawrence* reframes the question: whether the petitioners were free as adults to engage in private conduct in the exercise of their liberty under the Due Process Clause *(slide 7)*. At this broader level of generality, the tradition of personal autonomy in intimate matters is robust *(slide 7)*. Kennedy explicitly rejects Bowers' narrow framing as demeaning the claim *(slide 7)*.

The Kennedy ambiguity: Lawrence never identifies whether the right is "fundamental," never names the tier of scrutiny, and never definitively roots the analysis in Due Process or Equal Protection (O'Connor concurred solely on EP grounds) *(slide 8)*. The strategic case for ambiguity: calling the right "fundamental" would trigger strict scrutiny and threaten every morals-based law, while applying rational basis would require explaining why moral disapproval fails the test *(slide 8)*. His solution: articulate a robust liberty interest, then declare the Texas statute unconstitutional without slotting the analysis into any established tier *(slide 8)*.

The emerging-awareness methodology: Kennedy looks forward, tracking an emerging awareness that liberty gives protection to adults in deciding how to conduct their private lives *(slide 9)*. History as a floor, not a ceiling: sodomy laws had been repealed in most states; the Model Penal Code recommended repeal in 1955; the European Court of Human Rights struck down such laws *(slide 9)*. As Kennedy put it, times can blind us to certain truths, and later generations can see that laws once thought necessary and proper in fact serve only to oppress *(slide 9)*.

Scalia's dissent: this is rational basis review, and under rational basis the law wins -- Kennedy just won't say so *(slide 8)*.

**III. Four Principles Displacing Glucksberg — Obergefell v. Hodges (2015)**
Kennedy's majority declined to apply Glucksberg's deeply-rooted-plus-careful-description test directly. Instead, he identified four principles that explain why marriage is a fundamental right *(slide 10)*:
1. Individual autonomy -- the right to personal choice regarding marriage is inherent in individual autonomy *(slide 10)*.
2. Two-person union -- marriage supports a two-person union unlike any other *(slide 10)*.
3. Safeguarding children and families -- without marriage recognition, children of same-sex couples suffer stigma *(slide 10)*.
4. Social order keystone -- marriage is a keystone of social order *(slide 10)*.

The methodological move: rather than asking whether same-sex marriage is "deeply rooted" (it obviously isn't), Kennedy asked whether the reasons we protect marriage apply equally to same-sex couples *(slide 10)*.

The DP-EP synergy: Kennedy lets Due Process and Equal Protection reinforce each other, avoiding the narrow historical inquiry Glucksberg demands for stand-alone DP claims *(slide 11)*. Kennedy: the two clauses "are connected in a profound way" *(slide 11)*. The limiting-principle question: if Glucksberg's limiting principle is displaced, what constrains the recognition of new fundamental rights? Kennedy's answer is "reasoned judgment" guided by tradition as a living foundation, not a ceiling *(slide 11)*.

Roberts's dissent (the Lochner objection): without Glucksberg's limiting principle -- rights must be "objectively, deeply rooted" and "implicit in the concept of ordered liberty" -- nothing constrains judicial imposition of the majority's preferences *(slide 12)*. The four-principle framework is sentiment dressed up as constitutional analysis *(slide 12)*. Scalia sharpened the critique, mocking the "ask the nearest hippie" implication of rights rising from "a better informed understanding" *(slide 12)*.

**IV. Return to Glucksberg — Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization (2022)**
Alito's majority returns to Glucksberg's standard: unenumerated rights must be "deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition" and "implicit in the concept of ordered liberty" *(slide 13)*. Applied to abortion: the Constitution makes no reference to abortion, and at the Founding and for most of American history, abortion was unlawful *(slide 13)*. This is the Footnote 6 approach without citing Footnote 6 -- describe the right narrowly, then check history *(slide 13)*.

Five factors justify overruling *Roe v. Wade* and *Planned Parenthood v. Casey* *(slide 13)*: (1) Nature of the error -- Roe invented a right with no textual, historical, or traditional foundation; (2) Quality of reasoning -- Casey abandoned Roe's trimester framework while keeping its "central holding"; (3) Workability -- the undue burden test is "inherently standardless"; (4) Disruptive effect on constitutional doctrine; (5) Absence of concrete reliance -- women's reliance was "speculative" and not "sufficiently concrete."

The abortion-specific distinction: what sharply distinguishes the abortion right from other recognized rights is that abortion destroys what Roe termed "potential life" *(slide 14)*. Contraception prevents conception; marriage and intimacy involve choices of consenting adults; neither involves the destruction of potential human life *(slide 14)*. This is how the majority tries to cabin the decision *(slide 14)*. With abortion not a fundamental right, regulations need only satisfy rational-basis review, the most deferential standard -- a methodological earthquake from strict scrutiny to rational basis *(slide 14)*.

The three reactions *(slide 15)*: Thomas (concurrence) would go further -- SDP is "an oxymoron that lacks any basis in the Constitution" and the Court should reconsider all SDP precedents, including Griswold, Lawrence, and Obergefell, relocating protected rights to the Privileges or Immunities Clause. Roberts (concurrence) would not go so far -- uphold the 15-week ban without overruling Roe/Casey entirely; "a more measured course" reflecting stare decisis concerns. The joint dissent (Breyer, Sotomayor, Kagan) argues Thomas proves the majority's assurances are empty.

Post-Dobbs terrain *(slide 18)*: shield laws and Full Faith & Credit, right-to-travel challenges to "abortion trafficking" statutes, EMTALA preemption, medication abortion and the Comstock Act, equal protection and vagueness challenges, and First Amendment restrictions on abortion-related speech all now constitute the active constitutional frontier.

**V. The Methodology Map**
The deck's summary table *(slide 19)*:
- Michael H. (1989): Most-specific tradition (Footnote 6). Plurality only.
- Glucksberg (1997): "Deeply rooted" + "careful description." Majority.
- Lawrence (2003): "Emerging awareness." No tier named. Strategic ambiguity.
- Obergefell (2015): Four principles. DP-EP synergy. Glucksberg displaced.
- Dobbs (2022): Back to Glucksberg. Narrow framing. History as ceiling.

The deck's central question: is history a ceiling (you cannot have rights your ancestors did not recognize) or a floor (a foundation from which deeper understandings may emerge)? *(slide 19)* The methodology you choose is outcome-determinative: Glucksberg's narrow framing kills Lawrence and Obergefell; Kennedy's broad framing sustains them; Dobbs's return to Glucksberg raises the question whether Lawrence and Obergefell are living on borrowed time *(slide 19)*. The deck also catalogs which SDP cases are safe after Dobbs and which are uncertain -- Lawrence and Obergefell are flagged as UNCERTAIN; Loving and Cruzan as SAFE; Griswold as PROBABLY SAFE *(slide 17)*.

**VI. Performance Task — Kowalski v. State of Franklin**
The performance task applies all five cases to a fact pattern *(slides 20-31)*. David and Marco Kowalski are a same-sex couple married in 2018 post-Obergefell who adopted M.S. approximately three years ago when she was 14, after serving as her foster parents for nine months *(slide 21)*. M.S. is 17, turns 18 in six months, was in foster care from ages 11-13 after her biological mother died of cancer and her biological father was incarcerated *(slide 21)*. M.S. has aggressive AML leukemia and wants to refuse standard chemotherapy (60-70% five-year survival but 70-85% infertility, cardiomyopathy, secondary cancers) in favor of a less toxic immunotherapy trial (35-50% survival) or palliative care *(slide 21)*. Dr. Chatterjee testified that he had never had a patient more thoroughly informed and that her reading of the data was within the range of reasonable interpretation *(slide 22)*. The Franklin Mature Minor Medical Decisions Act authorizes a court to override parental medical decisions on clear-and-convincing evidence of maturity, with substantial-weight-but-not-dispositive treatment of parental views *(slide 23)*. Judge Barrera so found *(slide 23)*.

Two competing SDP claims *(slide 25)*: (1) The Kowalskis' parental rights (fundamental -- Troxel, Parham, Meyer/Pierce; strict scrutiny applies; Troxel presumption that fit parents act in their children's best interests; complicated by Henderson's "duration and depth" language for a three-year adoptive relationship *(slides 24, 26)*). (2) M.S.'s liberty interest in refusing unwanted treatment (Cruzan recognized a competent person's right; Rivera identifies a "developing liberty interest" for minors *(slide 24)*; the level-of-generality problem determines whether the claim survives Glucksberg: narrowly framed as the right of a minor to refuse life-saving treatment over parental objection, the claim fails; broadly framed as the right to bodily integrity and to refuse unwanted medical intervention, the pedigree is robust *(slide 28)*).

The Obergefell irony *(slide 30)*: the Kowalskis' family exists because Obergefell read "liberty" broadly, rejecting Glucksberg's narrow methodology. Now they invoke SDP to protect their parental authority while M.S. invokes the same broad doctrinal framework against them. The same broad SDP methodology that recognized their right to marry supports M.S.'s claim to autonomous medical decision-making. The deck frames this as "the methodology war made personal" *(slide 30)*.

## Cases Discussed

### [[Cases/Michael H v Gerald D (1989)|Michael H v Gerald D (1989)]]
Biological father denied parental rights when child born during mother's marriage. Scalia's Footnote 6 (plurality only): courts must use the most specific level of tradition. Circularity objection from Brennan. Marks rule leaves Footnote 6 as non-binding precedent. Engine of the methodology wars.

### [[Cases/Washington v Glucksberg (1997)|Washington v Glucksberg (1997)]]
"Deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition" and "implicit in the concept of ordered liberty" are required for unenumerated fundamental rights. "Careful description" of the right at stake. The standard that Dobbs restores and that Kennedy displaces in Lawrence and Obergefell.

### [[Cases/Lawrence v Texas (2003)|Lawrence v Texas (2003)]]
Overruled Bowers v. Hardwick. Reframed question from "right to homosexual sodomy" to "liberty of adults to engage in private conduct." "Emerging awareness" replaces history-as-ceiling. No tier of scrutiny named; strategic ambiguity. History as floor, not ceiling.

### [[Cases/Obergefell v Hodges (2015)|Obergefell v Hodges (2015)]]
Fundamental right to marry extended to same-sex couples. Four-principles framework displaces Glucksberg's historical inquiry. DP-EP synergy avoids narrow historical test. Roberts dissent: the Lochner objection; no limiting principle without Glucksberg.

### [[Cases/Dobbs v Jackson Women's Health Organization (2022)|Dobbs v Jackson Women's Health Organization (2022)]]
Roe v. Wade and Planned Parenthood v. Casey overruled. Restored Glucksberg. Abortion "deeply rooted"? No. From strict scrutiny to rational basis. Abortion distinct because it involves "potential life." Thomas concurrence: reconsider all SDP precedents. Roberts: more measured course. Dissent: Thomas proves majority's assurances are empty.

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

**Is Kennedy's Lawrence Ambiguity Genius or Abdication?** *(slide 32)* The strategic ambiguity in Lawrence -- no named tier of scrutiny, no clear DP vs. EP grounding *(slide 8)* -- allowed the majority to avoid threatening all morals-based legislation while still invalidating the Texas sodomy statute. Is this a stroke of judicial genius enabling incremental progress, or an abdication of the Court's duty to provide a clear legal framework that lower courts and legislatures can follow?

**Can Dobbs Credibly Cabin Itself?** *(slide 32)* The majority insists Dobbs does not threaten Lawrence and Obergefell because abortion uniquely involves "potential life" *(slide 14)*. But the methodology it adopts -- Glucksberg's narrow historical test -- would invalidate Lawrence and Obergefell if applied consistently *(slide 17)*. Are the majority's protestations persuasive? Does Thomas's concurrence (urging reconsideration of all SDP precedents) prove the assurances are illusory? *(slide 15)*

**Thomas's Intellectual Honesty.** Thomas calls for reconsidering all SDP precedents and relocating the analysis to the Privileges or Immunities Clause *(slide 15)*. Is he the only intellectually honest Justice in the debate -- the one willing to follow his methodology to its logical conclusion? *(slide 32)*

**The Kowalski Obergefell Irony.** Can the Kowalskis consistently invoke broad SDP when it constitutes their family (Obergefell) and narrow SDP when it threatens their authority over M.S.? *(slide 32)* Is methodological consistency required across different rights? Or can broad SDP and narrow SDP each apply in their own domains?

**The Age Line.** If M.S. were 18, this would not be a case -- she would be legally free to refuse treatment *(slide 29)*. Should six months really determine whether the Constitution protects her decision about her own body? *(slide 32)* Is the age-of-majority line a principled bright-line rule or an arbitrary cutoff that overrides demonstrated competence?

## Key Takeaways

1. **Methodology is outcome-determinative.** The level of generality at which you describe a claimed right determines whether it has traditional pedigree. Describe the right specifically (homosexual sodomy), and tradition condemns it. Describe it broadly (liberty in private intimate conduct), and tradition supports it. There is no neutral methodological choice.

2. **Footnote 6 is not binding precedent.** Only Scalia and Rehnquist joined it in Michael H. The specificity rule has enormous implications—if binding, it would defeat most SDP claims—but the Marks rule prevents it from controlling. The methodology wars persist precisely because no approach has commanded a stable majority.

3. **Kennedy's emerging-awareness approach is a historical methodology, not an abandonment of history.** Lawrence and Obergefell do not reject history; they read it differently. History as a floor (a foundation for expanding understanding) rather than a ceiling (a bar against recognizing unarticulated rights). The doctrinal question after Dobbs is which reading of history the Court will use.

4. **Dobbs restores Glucksberg but its cabining argument is contested.** The "potential life" distinction is the majority's attempt to preserve Lawrence and Obergefell while returning to Glucksberg. Whether that distinction is principled or tactical is the central question for SDP going forward.

5. **The DP-EP synergy is a powerful but under-theorized tool.** Kennedy's Obergefell methodology lets the two clauses reinforce each other, avoiding the narrow historical inquiry of a pure DP analysis. The synergy's scope and limits remain underdeveloped in subsequent doctrine.

6. **The performance task illustrates that SDP can generate competing fundamental rights claims.** Kowalski is not a case of one fundamental right against the state; it is two fundamental rights in collision—parental authority under Troxel and bodily integrity under Cruzan. The same doctrinal framework generates both claims, and the methodology for resolving them is unsettled.
