### From source

The deck pairs Youngstown and Dames and Moore as the same framework applied across 29 years and a different crisis (slide 12). For Youngstown the deck stages the Korean War steel crisis, Truman's Executive Order 10340 issued April 8, 1952, and the critical fact that Congress had considered and rejected seizure authority when it passed Taft-Hartley (slide 3). Black's 6-3 majority is formalist: presidential power must stem from "an act of Congress or from the Constitution itself," and there is no statute authorizing seizure (slide 4). Frankfurter's "gloss" theory treats a "systematic, unbroken, executive practice, long pursued to the knowledge of the Congress and never before questioned" as a gloss on Article II § 1, but warns that Taft-Hartley's express rejection is the opposite of acquiescence (slide 5). Frankfurter also warns about creeping power: "The accretion of dangerous power does not come in a day. It does come, however slowly, from the generative force of unchecked disregard of the restrictions that fence in even the most disinterested assertion of authority" (slide 6). Jackson's three-category concurrence -- maximum power with congressional authorization, the zone of twilight when Congress is silent, and the lowest ebb when the President acts against Congress's expressed or implied will -- is identified as "the most cited concurrence in Supreme Court history," and the steel seizure falls in Category 3 because Taft-Hartley rejected seizure (slide 7). The deck stresses Jackson's biographical credibility (FDR's SG and AG, Nuremberg chief prosecutor) and his enduring warning that "Emergency powers tend to kindle emergencies" (slide 8). Vinson's dissent catalogs Washington's Neutrality Proclamation, Jefferson's Louisiana Purchase, Lincoln's Emancipation Proclamation, and FDR's pre-Pearl Harbor plant seizures, framing them as wartime precedent for unilateral executive action (slide 9). The Youngstown takeaways slide identifies Jackson's concurrence as the canonical framework even though technically a concurrence, treats congressional silence as genuinely ambiguous, insists emergency does not create constitutional authority, and previews that Rehnquist will transform Jackson's "categories" into a "spectrum" in Dames and Moore (slide 11).

For Dames and Moore the deck dates the Iran hostage crisis to November 4, 1979, with 52 hostages held for 444 days (slide 14). Carter declared a national emergency under IEEPA on November 14, 1979, freezing approximately $12 billion in Iranian assets, and Dames and Moore -- an engineering firm owed $3.4 million for work on a nuclear plant in Bushehr -- obtained prejudgment attachments under revocable Treasury licenses (slide 15). The Algiers Accords (signed January 19, 1981, hostages released January 20, 1981) require terminating litigation, nullifying attachments, transferring assets, and creating a Hague claims tribunal (slide 16). The fait accompli is structural: the Court heard argument June 24 and decided July 2 -- eight days -- because the executive had created irreversible facts on the ground (slide 17). Rehnquist's two-part analysis treats the asset transfer and nullification as Category 1 under IEEPA's "direct and compel" and "nullify" language, with attachments "in every sense subordinate to the President's power under the IEEPA," while suspension of claims requires moving beyond statutory text (slide 18). The "pigeonholes / spectrum" reformulation is quoted directly: "Executive action in any particular instance falls, not neatly in one of three pigeonholes, but rather at some point along a spectrum running from explicit congressional authorization to explicit congressional prohibition" (slide 19). The Dames factors are listed as character of related legislation, history of acquiescence, length and numerosity, congressional discussion without disapproval, and the unspoken chaos factor (slide 20). The deck closes with the 2025-26 IEEPA tariff dispute, noting that the spectrum runs in both directions -- and that "the daunting prospect of refunding tariffs does add a fait accompli flavor to the case" (slide 22).

## Lecture Outline

### Youngstown Sheet and Tube Co v Sawyer (1952)

**Background.** The Korean War creates a steel supply crisis (slide 3). The United Steelworkers threaten a nationwide strike (slide 3). Truman believes a steel shutdown will cripple war production and issues Executive Order 10340 directing the Secretary of Commerce to seize the nation's steel mills (slide 3). Critically, Congress had considered and rejected giving the President seizure power when it passed the Taft-Hartley Act of 1947 -- choosing instead an 80-day cooling-off injunction, a path Truman refused to use (slide 3).

**Justice Black's majority (6-3).** Formalist and categorical: the President's power "must stem either from an act of Congress or from the Constitution itself" (slide 4). The Commander-in-Chief power does not extend to seizing domestic industry, and Congress expressly rejected seizure authority in Taft-Hartley (slide 4). Five concurring Justices each wrote separately, suggesting the majority opinion was too rigid for them (slide 4).

**Justice Frankfurter's concurrence: the "gloss" of practice.** A "systematic, unbroken, executive practice, long pursued to the knowledge of the Congress and never before questioned" may be treated as a gloss on Executive Power under Article II § 1 (slide 5). But this has to be genuine acquiescence, not constructive silence -- and Congress's express rejection of seizure in Taft-Hartley is the opposite of acquiescence (slide 5). Frankfurter also warns against incremental power expansion: "The accretion of dangerous power does not come in a day. It does come, however slowly, from the generative force of unchecked disregard of the restrictions that fence in even the most disinterested assertion of authority" (slide 6).

**Justice Jackson's three-category concurrence (the governing law).** Jackson organizes presidential power into three zones based on the relationship between presidential and congressional authority (slide 7):

- Category 1 (maximum power): President acts with express or implied congressional authorization. Power is at its maximum, combining his own authority plus all Congress can delegate (slide 7).
- Category 2 (zone of twilight): Congress is silent. The test turns on "the imperatives of events and contemporary imponderables" (slide 7). Presidential power is uncertain; outcomes depend on context.
- Category 3 (lowest ebb): President acts against the expressed or implied will of Congress. Power is at its minimum; courts can sustain the action only if Congress lacks authority over the matter (slide 7).

The steel seizure falls in Category 3 because Taft-Hartley expressly rejected seizure authority (slide 7). Jackson also warns: "Emergency powers tend to kindle emergencies" (slide 8). Historical emergency claims are the oldest justification for tyranny (slide 8). Jackson places responsibility on Congress to check the executive by acting affirmatively and not leaving dangerous ambiguities (slide 8).

**Chief Justice Vinson's dissent.** This is a genuine wartime crisis -- Americans are fighting and dying in Korea, and steel is the sinew of modern warfare (slide 9). He catalogs a long history of unilateral executive action: Washington's Neutrality Proclamation, Jefferson's Louisiana Purchase, Lincoln's Emancipation Proclamation, and FDR's pre-Pearl Harbor plant seizures defended by then-AG Jackson himself (slide 9). On this page's reading, the dissent itself provides raw material Rehnquist will use 29 years later in Dames and Moore (slide 9).

### From Youngstown to Dames and Moore: 29 Years

Rehnquist had clerked for Jackson (slide 12). When the Iran hostage crisis produced a constitutional question in 1981, Rehnquist took his former boss's categories and reshaped them (slide 12).

### Dames and Moore v Regan (1981)

**Background.** November 4, 1979: Iranian militants stormed the U.S. Embassy in Tehran, taking 66 American diplomats and citizens hostage; 52 would remain captive for 444 days (slide 14). On November 14, 1979, Carter declared a national emergency under IEEPA and froze approximately $12 billion in Iranian assets held in U.S. banks and their overseas branches (slide 15). American companies -- including Dames and Moore, an engineering firm owed $3.4 million for work on a nuclear power plant in Bushehr -- obtained prejudgment attachments under revocable Treasury licenses (slide 15). The Algiers Accords, signed January 19, 1981 the day before Reagan's inauguration, terminated all U.S. litigation against Iran, nullified all prejudgment attachments, required transferring the frozen $12 billion out of the United States, and created the Iran-United States Claims Tribunal at The Hague (slide 16).

**The fait accompli problem.** By July 1981, the hostages were home, the assets were moving, and the Accords were executed (slide 17). The Court heard argument June 24 and decided July 2 -- eight days (slide 17). Invalidating the executive orders would have required the United States to repudiate a binding international commitment under which Iran had already released the hostages, destroying American credibility in future negotiations (slide 17).

**Rehnquist's two-part analysis.**

Part 1 (asset transfer and nullification of attachments): IEEPA expressly authorized this -- the statute says the President may "direct and compel" the "transfer" of foreign property and "nullify" rights acquired in it (slide 18). The attachments were obtained under revocable Treasury licenses and were "in every sense subordinate to the President's power under the IEEPA" (slide 18). This is Category 1: maximum power, supported by the strongest of presumptions (slide 18).

Part 2 (suspension of claims): IEEPA does NOT authorize this -- claims are "not in themselves transactions involving Iranian property" (slide 18). The Hostage Act does not help directly either, but IEEPA and the Hostage Act are "highly relevant in the looser sense of indicating congressional acceptance of a broad scope for executive action" (slide 18). Rehnquist therefore turns to congressional acquiescence and the Youngstown "spectrum" (slide 18).

**The spectrum.** Rehnquist reformulates Jackson's framework: "Executive action in any particular instance falls, not neatly in one of three pigeonholes, but rather at some point along a spectrum running from explicit congressional authorization to explicit congressional prohibition" (slide 19). The claims suspension sits somewhere on this spectrum -- not Category 1 because there was no express authorization, not Category 2 because Congress had legislated in adjacent areas rather than been silent, and not Category 3 because Congress had never prohibited it (slide 19). The spectrum let Rehnquist treat acquiescence plus related legislation as near-authorization (slide 19).

**The Dames factors (what moves the dial toward authorization) (slide 20):**

- Character of related legislation: IEEPA and the Hostage Act show Congress accepts broad executive action in international crises, even if they do not specifically authorize claims settlement (slide 20).
- History of acquiescence: From the Jay Treaty (1794) through the twentieth century, Presidents have settled claims by executive agreement (slide 20).
- Length and numerosity of instances: The longer and more numerous the precedents, the stronger the inference of acquiescence (slide 20).
- Congressional discussion without disapproval: Congress created the International Claims Settlement Act, which presupposes the validity of executive claims settlement (slide 20).
- Unspoken factor (chaos): Would invalidation produce catastrophic consequences? Rehnquist never says this openly, but the fait accompli is the elephant in the room (slide 20).

**Powell's partial dissent.** Agreed on the IEEPA question but dissented on claims suspension, arguing the Court should not defer the takings question -- the government was extinguishing vested property rights (the attachments, the judgment) and should have to show it was providing just compensation now, not in some future proceeding (slide 21).

### IEEPA Today: The Trump Tariff Dispute (2025-26)

The same statute at the heart of Dames and Moore is now at the center of the biggest trade case in decades (slide 22). In 2025, Trump invoked IEEPA to impose sweeping tariffs on imports worldwide, claiming IEEPA's "regulate ... importation" language covers tariff imposition; the CIT and Federal Circuit (7-4 en banc) said no, and the Supreme Court heard argument November 5, 2025 (slide 22). Unlike 1981, there is no history of IEEPA tariffs (no acquiescence), Congress has enacted specific, limited tariff statutes (Sections 122, 201, 232, 301) -- suggesting IEEPA was not meant as a backdoor -- and the major questions doctrine demands clear authorization for actions of "vast economic significance" (slide 22). The irony of Dames and Moore's legacy: the very flexibility Rehnquist built -- the spectrum, the acquiescence theory -- now provides tools for limiting executive power, with the daunting prospect of refunding tariffs adding a fait accompli flavor to the case (slide 22).

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

The lecture uses the IEEPA tariff dispute (2025-26) as a live hypothetical: Can the President impose broad tariffs under IEEPA's "regulate importation" language? The class is asked to apply the Dames spectrum. In favor of executive power: IEEPA's text, past emergency economic orders. Against: Congress has enacted specific tariff statutes (sections 122, 201, 232, 301) suggesting a deliberate structure; no history of IEEPA tariffs (no acquiescence); major questions doctrine demands a clear statement.

Professor Chandler also flags the DACA and border wall questions as additional applications of the Youngstown framework: presidential action in a legislative vacuum (Category 2 ambiguity) versus presidential action after Congress has considered and rejected specific measures (Category 3).

## Key Takeaways

- Jackson's concurrence is the canonical framework for executive power analysis, though it was never the majority opinion.
- Congressional silence is genuinely ambiguous: silence in a field Congress has never addressed is different from silence after Congress has expressly considered and rejected a power.
- Emergency does not create constitutional authority. "Emergency powers tend to kindle emergencies."
- Rehnquist's spectrum allows courts to treat congressional acquiescence plus adjacent legislation as near-authorization, but the same framework can limit executive power when Congress has legislated in the area.
- The fait accompli problem: executive speed and irreversibility can constrain judicial review in ways that are constitutionally troubling.
- The "gloss" doctrine (Frankfurter): long-standing uncontested executive practice can inform the scope of Article II power, but only where genuine acquiescence exists, not mere silence.
