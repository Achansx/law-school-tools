## Lecture Outline

The deck is relatively sparse, but the framing is consistent: the Civil War "essentially creates a second constitution" with choices made at this time still controlling today *(slide 2)*. The Thirteenth Amendment (1865) abolished slavery and involuntary servitude, and empowered Congress to enforce. The Fourteenth Amendment (1868) created national citizenship, forbade states from abridging the privileges or immunities of that citizenship, from depriving any person of life, liberty, or property without due process of law, or from denying the equal protection of the laws, and empowered Congress to enforce. The Fifteenth Amendment (1870) prohibited race-based restrictions on voting and empowered Congress to enforce. Congress used that new enforcement authority aggressively in the Civil Rights Act of 1866, the Reconstruction Act of 1867, the First, Second, and Third Enforcement Acts of 1870-1871, and the Civil Rights Act of 1875 -- the deck's tabular timeline categorizes each statute by what it addressed *(slide 8)*.

The lecture then turns to the reception of this statutory framework in the federal courts. The Slaughter-House Cases (1873) held that the Fourteenth Amendment's Privileges or Immunities Clause protects only a narrow set of rights tied to national citizenship (access to federal seaports, protection on the high seas, etc.), not the broad set of fundamental rights that Republicans in 1868 had thought they were codifying. The Clause was effectively dead-lettered for more than a century, until Saenz v Roe (1999) and arguably McDonald v Chicago (2010) began to revive it.

The Colfax Massacre is flagged in the deck's statutory timeline as the doctrinal break point *(slide 8)*. The Massacre (April 13, 1873) was one of the worst acts of racial violence in American history: a white militia killed dozens of Black state militiamen and freed men defending the Grant Parish courthouse in Louisiana. Federal prosecutors indicted the perpetrators under the Enforcement Acts. In United States v Cruikshank (1876), the Supreme Court reversed the convictions, holding that the Fourteenth Amendment's protections ran only against state action, not private violence, and that Congress's Section 5 power did not reach the private conspiracy. The practical effect was to immunize the Klan and its successors from federal prosecution and to leave Black Southerners to the mercy of state courts and sheriffs who were often complicit in the violence.

The Civil Rights Cases (1883) struck down the public-accommodations provisions of the Civil Rights Act of 1875, again on state-action grounds. Justice Bradley's opinion insisted that the Fourteenth Amendment reached only state discrimination, not private discrimination, and that Section 5 authority was confined to "remedial" legislation targeting state violations. The dissent by Justice Harlan warned that this cramped reading would leave the freedmen in a "condition of legal inferiority."

Ex parte McCardle (1869) illustrated the Reconstruction-era Congress's willingness to strip the Supreme Court of appellate jurisdiction to prevent a challenge to Military Reconstruction. The Court acquiesced, holding that Article III allowed Congress to make exceptions to its appellate jurisdiction. The lecture notes that this remains a tool in Congress's arsenal, though it has rarely been used since.

## Key Takeaways

- The Reconstruction Amendments were a structural transformation, not a clarification; they created national citizenship and gave Congress enforcement power to protect it.
- The Supreme Court in the 1870s and 1880s systematically narrowed Congress's enforcement authority and the substantive scope of the Fourteenth Amendment.
- The state-action doctrine was established in Cruikshank and the Civil Rights Cases as a limit on both Section 5 and the Amendment itself.
- The long-dormant Privileges or Immunities Clause began to show signs of revival in Saenz v Roe (1999) and the Thomas and Alito opinions in McDonald v Chicago (2010).
- Ex parte McCardle stands as a precedent for congressional jurisdiction-stripping, though rarely invoked.
- Exam framing: be able to articulate why the Slaughter-House, Cruikshank, and Civil Rights Cases trilogy "broke" Reconstruction as a legal project and how modern cases have responded.
