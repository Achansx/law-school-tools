# Marbury v Madison - Judicial Review

## Professor Emphasis

### From source

Marbury matters nearly as much for its dicta -- the right-remedy alignment, the political question doctrine, and the seeds of executive privilege -- as for its core holding on judicial review *(slide 8)*. Students should watch for Marbury "exceptions" throughout the semester: later courts that nominally honor Marbury while undercutting its ideas, limiting its holding, or acting inconsistently with its notion of the Rule of Law *(slide 18)*. The lecture also surfaces present-day threats to judicial independence -- violence and intimidation against judges, disinformation, and defiance of judgments -- via Chief Justice Roberts's admonition *(slide 16)*. And it asks why this particular "little case" became the vehicle for judicial review, given that Hamilton (Federalist 78), *Ware v. Hylton*, and Iredell's *Calder v. Bull* concurrence had already hinted at the doctrine *(slide 17)*.

### Editor's framing

This page treats Marbury as simultaneously foundational and historically contingent -- a case that established judicial review not through a logically necessary argument, but through Marshall's political tactics in choosing a confrontation he could not lose. The "manufacturing a confrontation" gloss is editorial packaging of the deck's case-selection brilliance theme, not language Chandler uses. The framing of later cases as "quietly undercutting Marbury's rule-of-law premise" likewise sharpens the deck's open-ended "do they undercut its ideas?" prompt into a stronger editorial claim.

## Lecture Outline

### Historical Setting

The background to Marbury is a story of partisan politics, logistical fumbles, and constitutional hardball. After losing the 1800 election to Jefferson, the Federalist-controlled Congress passed the Judiciary Act of 1801, creating sixteen new circuit court judgeships. President Adams scrambled to fill these "midnight judge" positions before Jefferson's inauguration. The commissions were signed and sealed but never all delivered -- Secretary of State John Marshall (who would soon become Chief Justice) left several sitting on his desk. Jefferson, once inaugurated, directed his new Secretary of State Madison not to deliver them.

William Marbury, one of the snubbed appointees, filed directly in the Supreme Court seeking a writ of mandamus ordering Madison to issue his commission *(slide 4)*. He relied on Section 13 of the Judiciary Act of 1789, which appeared to grant the Supreme Court original jurisdiction over mandamus actions against federal officers *(slide 6)*.

### The Three-Question Structure

Marshall parsed the case into three questions: (1) Did Marbury have a right to the commission? (2) If so, was there a legal remedy? (3) Was the appropriate remedy a mandamus from the Supreme Court? His answers -- yes, yes, and no -- allowed Marshall to lecture Jefferson and Madison on their lawlessness while ruling against Marbury and thus depriving Jefferson of any occasion to defy the Court.

### The Right-Remedy Alignment

The lecture treats the right-remedy principle as one of Marbury's most important contributions beyond judicial review *(slide 8)*. Marshall declared that "the very essence of civil liberty consists in the right of every individual to claim the protection of the laws whenever he receives an injury," and invoked Blackstone's principle that "where there is a legal right, there is also a legal remedy by suit or action at law whenever that right is invaded" *(slide 7)*. The principle is flagged as a recurring touchstone throughout the course -- watch for whether later cases honor it or quietly abandon it *(slides 7-8)*.

### The Political Question Doctrine

Marshall used Marbury to plant the seed of the political question doctrine *(slide 10)*. He drew a distinction between executive acts that are discretionary and political in character -- for which the courts provide no review -- and acts that involve specific legal duties tied to individual rights, which are justiciable *(slides 10-11)*. When the heads of departments merely execute the will of the President in matters entrusted to executive discretion, their conduct is "only politically examinable" *(slide 10)*. But when a specific legal duty is assigned and individual rights depend on it, the injured party has recourse in the courts *(slide 10)*. The deck explicitly invites students to "see echoes of this debate in Trump v. United States (2024)" *(slide 10)*.

The lecture notes the obvious tension: Marshall used this distinction to justify lecturing Jefferson about Marbury's legal right while still declining to provide a remedy. It raises the question of whether the political question doctrine is being used here as a principled limit on judicial power or as a tactical evasion.

### Seeds of Executive Privilege

Marshall acknowledged that "the intimate political relation" between the President and heads of departments makes legal investigation of their acts "peculiarly irksome," planting the seed of what would later be called executive privilege *(slide 12)*. The deck flags this as a "corollary notion" alongside the right-remedy and political-question dicta and asks whether it "has outgrown its bounds" *(slide 8)*. A separate passage acknowledges potential executive immunity for illegal acts under color of office, and the deck flags this as material to revisit when reaching the Eleventh Amendment, official immunity, and sovereign immunity cases *(slide 13)*.

### The Core Holding: Judicial Review

The central argument for judicial review proceeds from the structure of a written constitution. If the Constitution is paramount law, courts cannot simply ignore it when it conflicts with an ordinary statute. Closing one's eyes to the Constitution and seeing only the statute would, in Marshall's memorable phrase, give the Legislature "a practical and real omnipotence" while professing to restrict its powers -- an absurdity that reduces to nothing the concept of a written constitution *(slide 14)*. Since cases arising under the Constitution must be decided with reference to it, the conclusion that courts can declare statutes unconstitutional follows *(slide 14)*.

The lecture emphasizes that this argument was not inevitable: Hamilton had anticipated it in Federalist 78, *Ware v. Hylton* hinted at it in the context of federal supremacy over state law, and Iredell's *Calder v. Bull* concurrence anticipated review of federal statutes *(slide 17)*.

### Why Marbury Is Brilliant, Not Just Important

Marshall's genius was tactical as well as logical. He found a case where: (a) he could assert the supremacy of constitutional law without risk, (b) the act he struck down was one that paradoxically helped the Federalist party (expanding Supreme Court jurisdiction), and (c) Jefferson had no basis to defy the ruling because the decision ordered nothing against the administration. The professor asks why Marshall chose this particular case -- the answer reveals much about how judicial power is built incrementally through careful selection of battlegrounds.

### Stuart v. Laird and the Forgotten Companion

Decided the same week as Marbury, *Stuart v. Laird* was meant to address the constitutionality of the 1802 Judiciary Act, which abolished the courts created by the 1801 Act *(slide 22)*. Because none of the fired judges challenged the Act, the issue narrowed to whether Supreme Court justices could ride circuit and hear cases the 1801 Act had assigned to the "midnight" judges; the Court said yes *(slide 22)*. The deck calls it "the forgotten companion case" *(slide 22)*. This page reads the companion case as evidence that Marbury's judicial supremacy was immediately bounded by political realities -- a doctrinal gloss beyond what the slide itself states.

### The Statutory Interpretation Subtext

The lecture also raises an interpretive puzzle: did Section 13 of the 1789 Judiciary Act actually grant original jurisdiction over mandamus? A careful reading suggests the statute may have been about mandamus in the context of appellate jurisdiction over lower courts -- not as a freestanding grant of original jurisdiction. If so, Marshall may have misread or deliberately distorted the statute to create the constitutional conflict he wanted to resolve. This cuts against the idea that Marbury involved a genuinely necessary constitutional decision.

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

The lecture poses a key pedagogical question: write the most parsimonious opinion you can to resolve Marbury's complaint, resolving only those issues necessary to a disposition of the case *(slide 5)*. The deck asks both whether parsimony is a good thing in judicial opinions and why Marshall did not write an efficient opinion *(slide 5)*.

This page's gloss: Marshall's Marbury is decidedly anti-minimalist. The dicta about right-remedy alignment, political questions, and executive privilege all exceed what was necessary to resolve whether Marbury was entitled to a mandamus.

## Key Takeaways

- Marbury is the foundational source of judicial review, but the argument had been anticipated by Hamilton (Federalist 78) and earlier circuit courts.
- The right-remedy alignment principle -- where there is a legal right, there is a legal remedy -- is a recurring touchstone throughout Con Law I.
- The political question doctrine, first seeded in Marbury, limits justiciability for executive actions that are discretionary or "purely political."
- Marshall's tactical genius: he chose a case where he could assert judicial supremacy at no institutional cost, because the ruling went against the Federalist plaintiff and ordered nothing against Jefferson.
- Watch for Marbury "exceptions" throughout the course -- courts that nominally respect Marbury while narrowing, limiting, or quietly defying its principles.
- The companion case, Stuart v. Laird, immediately qualified Marbury's assertiveness through capitulation on circuit riding.
