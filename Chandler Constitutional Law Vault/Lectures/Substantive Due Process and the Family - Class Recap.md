## Lecture Outline

### Meyer v Nebraska (1923) and Pierce v Society of Sisters (1925)

**Meyer v Nebraska.** Nebraska banned teaching in any language other than English, targeting German amid post-WWI hysteria *(slide 2)*. The Court struck it down 7-2, holding that Fourteenth Amendment "liberty" protects the right of teachers to pursue their calling and parents to direct their children's education *(slide 2)*. The Court's key dicta extended liberty to encompass the right "to marry, establish a home and bring up children" — language that would anchor family-rights doctrine for decades *(slide 2)*.

**Pierce v Society of Sisters.** Oregon required all children to attend public schools — a law backed by the KKK aiming to destroy Catholic parochial education *(slide 2)*. The Court struck it down unanimously, holding "The child is not the mere creature of the State" *(slide 2)*. Because the corporate plaintiffs (parochial schools) could not claim "liberty," the Court protected parental rights through the schools' property interests — a standing workaround that later became doctrinally awkward *(slide 2)*. The anti-standardization principle: "The fundamental theory of liberty . . . excludes any general power of the State to standardize its children" *(slide 2)*.

### Buck v Bell (1927): The Doctrine's Failure

Virginia's Sterilization Act (1924) authorized forced sterilization of "feebleminded" persons in state institutions as part of the eugenics movement sweeping America *(slide 3)*. Carrie Buck was not feebleminded — she was a foster child committed after being raped by her foster family's nephew, and her daughter Vivian later made the school honor roll *(slide 3)*.

The proceeding was a sham: her appointed lawyer was a friend of the superintendent, conducted no investigation, and conceded key facts — a test case designed to rubber-stamp the statute *(slide 3)*. Holmes equated sterilization to vaccination (Jacobson) and wrote: "Three generations of imbeciles are enough" — but not one of the three was an imbecile *(slide 3)*.

The consequences: over 60,000 Americans were forcibly sterilized under state eugenics laws; Nazi Germany cited Buck v Bell as precedent for its 1933 sterilization law; the case has never been explicitly overruled, only distinguished by Skinner v Oklahoma (1942) on equal protection grounds *(slide 3)*.

### The Ninth Amendment Dead End

Madison's Ninth Amendment was designed to negate expressio unius — the fear that enumerating some rights would exclude others *(slide 4)*. In United Public Workers v Mitchell (1947), the Court gutted it: "If a granted power is found, necessarily the objection of invasion of those rights reserved by the 9th and 10th Amendments must fail" *(slide 4)*. The Ninth Amendment became an "inkblot" — unintelligible and unenforceable — and future unenumerated-rights claims had to travel through Due Process instead *(slide 4)*.

### Griswold v Connecticut (1965)

Connecticut criminalized the use of contraceptives even by married couples — a deliberate test case challenged the law *(slide 4)*. Douglas found a right to marital privacy in "penumbras, formed by emanations" from the First, Third, Fourth, Fifth, and Ninth Amendments — widely seen as inventive but strained reasoning *(slide 4)*. Goldberg's concurrence tried to revive the Ninth Amendment with independent doctrinal force — his last try before leaving the Court — but it did not take hold *(slide 4)*. The key weakness: the opinion protects only marital privacy (later extended in Eisenstadt v Baird) *(slide 4)*. Douglas's signature passage: "Would we allow the police to search the sacred precincts of marital bedrooms for telltale signs of the use of contraceptives?" *(slide 4)*.

### Loving v Virginia (1967): The Dual Holding

Virginia's Racial Integrity Act prohibited interracial marriage and included a "Pocahontas Exception" for elite families claiming Native ancestry *(slide 5)*. The Lovings married in D.C. and returned to Virginia; a sheriff raided their bedroom at 2 AM *(slide 5)*. Chief Justice Warren's opinion rested on two independent constitutional legs: Equal Protection (racial classification) and Due Process (marriage as a fundamental right) *(slide 5)*. The dual structural grounding makes Loving resilient to doctrinal challenge from either direction.

### Moore v City of East Cleveland (1977): Extended Family

East Cleveland's zoning ordinance defined "family" so narrowly that a grandmother living with two grandsons who were cousins was criminalized *(slide 5)*. The plurality held the extended family tradition is "equally venerable" — the state cannot standardize family structure *(slide 5)*. Brennan's concurrence highlighted the racial dimension: extended family arrangements are survival mechanisms for some communities *(slide 5)*.

### The Glucksberg Paradox

If fundamental rights must be "deeply rooted in history and tradition" (Glucksberg), how do we explain Loving? *(slide 5)*. Anti-miscegenation laws were the history and tradition — 41 states had them at various points *(slide 5)*. Yet Loving is universally accepted as correct *(slide 5)*. The deck flags this as the strongest case against a purely backward-looking approach to substantive due process, to be continued the following session with Roe, Casey, Dobbs, and Glucksberg methodology *(slide 5)*.

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

**Buck v Bell and the Sham Proceeding.** The constitutional significance of Buck v Bell is not only its holding but its procedural pathology *(slide 3)*. A test case designed to produce a predetermined result, with a lawyer who made no effort to investigate or advocate, raises questions about whether any procedural due process requirement was satisfied at all. Buck v Bell is often paired with Skinner v Oklahoma (1942), which struck down a sterilization law for habitual criminals on equal protection grounds (white-collar criminals were exempt). Skinner avoids overruling Buck but makes its continued validity untenable as a moral matter *(slide 3)*.

**The Glucksberg Paradox.** If Loving is correct — and it universally is accepted as such — then the history-and-tradition test cannot be the exclusive methodology for SDP *(slide 5)*. Anti-miscegenation was not merely a recent legislative preference; it was a deeply embedded tradition. The Paradox forces any student defending Glucksberg's backward-looking methodology to explain why Loving was correctly decided. Candidates include: (1) Loving rested primarily on Equal Protection, making the SDP leg technically unnecessary; (2) even under Glucksberg, the tradition of marriage as a fundamental right is more deeply rooted than the tradition of racial exclusion from it; (3) Loving represents a case where the tradition was itself unconstitutionally infected by racial animus and therefore cannot serve as the benchmark. None of these answers is wholly satisfying.

## Key Takeaways

- Meyer and Pierce establish parental rights as a strand of SDP liberty that survived Lochner's demise -- confirmed in Troxel (2000) and central to the Kowalski hypothetical.
- Buck v Bell demonstrates that procedural and substantive due process can both fail simultaneously, producing catastrophic injustice; it remains nominally valid precedent.
- The Ninth Amendment is doctrinally inert as an independent source of unenumerated rights; the action is in the Due Process Clause.
- Griswold's penumbra reasoning is analytically contested but outcomes-wise foundational -- contraceptive privacy generates the privacy tradition that feeds Roe, Lawrence, and Obergefell.
- The Glucksberg Paradox -- Loving's universal acceptance despite anti-miscegenation history -- is the strongest argument against purely backward-looking SDP methodology and recurs throughout the methodology wars.
