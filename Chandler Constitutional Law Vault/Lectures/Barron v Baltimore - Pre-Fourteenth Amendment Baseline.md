## Lecture Outline

The deck opens with the factual setup: a public-works project diverts streams, silting up the area adjacent to Barron's profitable wharf so that the water is no longer deep enough (slide 2). The deck flags that what happened to Barron would likely be a taking under current doctrine — a question students will study in property class (slide 2). Barron sued the City for damages under the Fifth Amendment's Takings Clause.

The legal question is whether the Fifth Amendment applies to the states and their political subdivisions. Marshall, writing for a unanimous Court, holds that it does not. The Bill of Rights does not constrain state governments (slide 3). The deck's reasoning rests on two points (slide 4): the fear at the founding was of the general government, not the states; and the states would not have wanted to rely on the "unwieldy and cumbrous machinery" of constitutional amendment when they could amend their own constitutions through a state convention (slide 4).

The deck spells out the consequences (slide 3). States are still subject to the Article I § 10 limits (no ex post facto laws, no titles of nobility, no religious tests for office), and protections may still flow from state constitutions and state laws. But under Barron, a state may presumably establish religion, restrain free speech, engage in cruel and unusual punishment, and conduct unreasonable searches and seizures (slide 3). And it is hard to see a basis for unenumerated rights — privacy, the right to raise your children, the right to learn German (slide 3).

The deck asks the structural question about how to reverse Barron via a "13th amendment" — listing three textual options: an Article I § 10 addendum extending the Bill of Rights to states, a Privileges or Immunities clause, or a Due Process clause (slide 5). Barron is settled law from 1833 to 1868. The Fourteenth Amendment introduces new text (Privileges or Immunities, Due Process, Equal Protection) that makes possible the question Barron had foreclosed. The Slaughter-House Cases (1873) read the Privileges or Immunities Clause narrowly; incorporation then routes through the Due Process Clause, from Palko (1937) through Duncan (1968) to McDonald (2010) and Timbs (2019). The deck closes by asking what protects against discriminatory state treatment, voting rights in state elections, free speech from state censorship, and religious oppression — noting that New Hampshire, Connecticut, and Massachusetts all had state religions in the early 1800s — and projecting forward to "what 14th amendment does (and does not do)" (slide 6).

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

**Hypothetical 1 (Same side as Barron): State criminal trial, 1840.** A defendant prosecuted in an Alabama state court for an ordinary state-law offense demands a grand jury indictment under the Fifth Amendment and objects to the absence of a twelve-member jury under the Sixth Amendment. Reasoning: Barron squarely forecloses the claim. In 1840 the Bill of Rights restrains only the federal government, and the Fourteenth Amendment does not yet exist to supply an alternative textual hook. The defendant's only recourse is Alabama's own constitution and the protections it chooses to extend; the federal Bill of Rights is simply unavailable as a check on state criminal process. This is the paradigmatic pre-Reconstruction outcome the lecture uses to illustrate Barron's baseline.

**Hypothetical 2 (Same side as Barron, harder facts): State takings claim, 1860.** A Georgia landowner's property is destroyed by a state railroad project authorized by the legislature with no provision for compensation. The owner sues under the Fifth Amendment's Takings Clause. Reasoning: even on facts functionally identical to Barron himself, the Fifth Amendment claim fails because the Amendment does not reach state action in 1860. The obvious injustice of the outcome is precisely why the post-Civil War amendments were thought necessary; the answer changes only in 1868 with ratification of the Fourteenth Amendment and eventually in Chicago, Burlington and Quincy Railroad v Chicago (1897), which incorporates the Takings Clause. The hypo makes Barron's structural holding salient by showing that unfair outcomes under state law are what the original Constitution left to state constitutional process.

**Hypothetical 3 (Opposite side, counterfactual): Bill of Rights binding on states in 1791.** Imagine Marshall had reached the opposite conclusion and held that the Bill of Rights does restrain the states from the outset. Reasoning: on the counterfactual, state criminal-procedure rules, state criminal-punishment regimes, state takings laws, state religious-establishment rules, and state firearms regulations would all be subject to federal judicial oversight a generation before Reconstruction. Federalism would look radically different because state constitutional law would lose much of its independent operating space. The hypo clarifies Chandler's point that Barron is not merely a textual ruling but a structural one: it preserves the dual-sovereignty design of the early Republic, and the Fourteenth Amendment only partially undoes that preservation.

**Hypothetical 4 (Fence-sitter): Federal prosecution, 1830.** A defendant indicted in federal court invokes the Sixth Amendment's speedy-trial guarantee. Reasoning: the Bill of Rights applies directly to the federal government, so the Sixth Amendment is available. Barron is off point because it speaks only to state action. The hypo underscores the narrow textual holding: Barron does not weaken the Bill of Rights against the federal government, it only refuses to extend it to state and municipal action. This distinction is critical for reading later incorporation cases, which do not expand the federal-government-facing scope of the Bill of Rights; they bring state governments under separate Fourteenth Amendment constraints that happen to track Bill of Rights content.

## Key Takeaways

- The Bill of Rights, as originally ratified, restrains only the federal government. States are bound by their own constitutions and by whatever explicit Article I, Section 10 limits apply.
- The textual argument: where the Constitution binds states, it says so. The Bill of Rights contains no such language.
- The historical argument: the Bill of Rights was demanded by anti-Federalists as a check on the new national government, not on the states.
- The structural argument: the federal Constitution creates a government of enumerated powers; state governments are regulated by state law. Each sphere stands on its own text.
- Barron is the baseline against which the Fourteenth Amendment works. Every incorporation case is a partial overruling of Barron for a single Bill of Rights guarantee.
- The Takings Clause itself was finally incorporated against the states in Chicago, Burlington & Quincy Railroad v. Chicago (1897), one of the earliest incorporation decisions.

## Connections

- [[Topics/Incorporation Doctrine|Incorporation Doctrine]]: Barron is the baseline against which every incorporation case is measured. The Topic page anchors the Palko, Duncan, McDonald, and Timbs lineage; this lecture supplies the pre-Fourteenth-Amendment premise that makes the entire doctrine necessary.
- [[Topics/Substantive Due Process|Substantive Due Process]]: Barron closes off the textually natural route for applying Bill of Rights protections to the states; after Slaughter-House narrows Privileges or Immunities, the Due Process Clause becomes the operational vehicle for reverse-engineering what Barron had foreclosed.
- [[Topics/Privileges or Immunities|Privileges or Immunities]]: the textual route most sympathetic to the framers of the Fourteenth Amendment, suppressed by Slaughter-House and periodically revived (Thomas concurrences in McDonald and Timbs). Barron supplies the pre-1868 baseline that made a new constitutional provision necessary in the first place.
- [[Topics/Individual Rights|Individual Rights]]: places the Bill of Rights in its original architectural role as a restraint on national power, which is what Barron polices.
- [[Cases/Palko v Connecticut (1937)|Palko]]: first modern incorporation case under the ordered-liberty test; the first real doctrinal move away from Barron's baseline through the Due Process Clause.
- [[Cases/Duncan v Louisiana (1968)|Duncan]]: reformulates the incorporation test around the American scheme of justice and incorporates jury trial in criminal prosecutions.
- [[Cases/McDonald v City of Chicago (2010)|McDonald]]: incorporates the Second Amendment via Due Process; Thomas concurrence revisits Slaughter-House and the Privileges or Immunities alternative that would have avoided the Barron-to-incorporation detour altogether.
- [[Cases/Timbs v Indiana (2019)|Timbs]]: incorporates the Excessive Fines Clause unanimously; the modern capstone of the doctrinal arc Barron started.

**Doctrinal placement.** Barron is the structural predicate of American rights federalism. It is the fixed historical fact that the Bill of Rights, at ratification, constrained only Congress and the federal executive. Everything the incorporation doctrine does after 1868 is, in effect, a partial and case-by-case undoing of Barron through the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause. Without Barron there is nothing to incorporate; without Barron the Privileges or Immunities suppression in Slaughter-House has nothing to respond to; without Barron the modern debate over total versus selective incorporation has no problem to solve. Marshall's opinion is short and, for the Court, uncontroversial, but it defines the entire terrain on which later rights litigation plays out. A reader who skips Barron cannot understand why incorporation arguments are about due process rather than about the Bill of Rights text directly.
