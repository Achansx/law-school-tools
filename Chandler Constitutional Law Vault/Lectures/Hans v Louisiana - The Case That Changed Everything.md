# Hans v Louisiana - The Case That Changed Everything

## Professor Emphasis

### From source

The deck's subtitle frames Hans as "The Case That Changed Everything—and Nobody Saw It Coming" (slide 1). The setting: Louisiana has defaulted on its bonds, and Hans walks into federal circuit court saying his state owes him money, the Constitution protects his contract, and he wants a federal judge to make Louisiana pay (slide 2). The constitutional world of 1890: the Bill of Rights does NOT apply to the states; Barron v. Baltimore (1833) is still the law; the 14th Amendment's Privileges or Immunities Clause has been gutted by Slaughter-House (1873); incorporation doctrine does not yet exist; Section 1983 is dormant (slide 2). The deck's key insight: "In 1890, sovereign immunity is not an obstacle to civil rights — because there are almost no federal civil rights enforceable against the states" (slide 4) -- Bradley was thinking about bonds and state treasuries, not civil rights (slide 4). (slide 8). The lesson: a decision about money became a decision about liberty, and the gap between what the deciding court was worried about and what the decision eventually controlled is the strongest argument for judicial minimalism (slide 11).

### Editor's framing

This page treats Hans as a case study in the unintended consequences of judicial ambition. The framing that Bradley was solving a "narrow, contextually specific problem" while announcing a "sweeping structural principle" is editorial packaging that the deck supports but does not state in those terms. The framing of Ex Parte Young as the "double fiction" workaround draws on the deck's brief description (slide 10) but extends with editorial commentary that the lecture does not explicitly make.

## Lecture Outline

### The Constitutional World of 1890

To understand Hans, you must understand what federal constitutional law looked like in 1890. The Bill of Rights did NOT apply to the states -- Barron v. Baltimore (1833) was still the law of the land (slide 2). Chief Justice Marshall had held that the amendments were "demanded as security against the apprehended encroachments of the general government — not against those of the local governments" (slide 3). The first real incorporation case (applying the Takings Clause to states) would not come until 1897, free speech incorporation came in 1925, and the great wave of incorporation was seven decades away (slide 3).

The Fourteenth Amendment's 1868 promise had been largely broken by 1873: the Slaughter-House Cases gutted the Privileges or Immunities Clause, limiting national citizenship protections to trivial interests like the right to travel to D.C. and use navigable waters (slide 4). Fundamental Bill of Rights protections were excluded (slide 4). Section 1983 was dormant (slide 2) and would not be revived until Monroe v. Pape (1961) (slide 9). In 1890, there were essentially no federal civil rights enforceable against the states, and Bradley was NOT thinking about civil rights -- he was thinking about bonds and state treasuries (slide 4).

### Louisiana's Repudiated Reconstruction Bonds

**The Political Context.** After the Civil War, Louisiana experienced near-total chaos with parallel governments -- Radical Republicans vs. ex-Confederates -- and four constitutions adopted between 1864 and 1879 (slide 5). The Colfax Massacre (1873) killed 100+ Black Republicans, and the Battle of Liberty Place (1874) was a White League armed insurrection in New Orleans (slide 5). Union troops dropped from 25,000 (1864) to 14,000 (Reconstruction) to zero after the Compromise of 1877, and Democrats/Redeemers seized full control (slide 5). Over 350 post-Civil War debt cases eventually reached the Supreme Court (slide 5).

**The Bond Deal and Its Betrayal.** Act No. 3 of 1874 consolidated bonds up to $15 million at 7% interest, dedicated a 5.5-mill property tax, made diversion of funds a felony, and included a constitutional amendment declaring the bonds a "valid contract" (slide 6). Hans lent $87,500 to Louisiana (slide 6). Louisiana's new Redeemer constitution of 1879 slashed interest rates from 7% to 2%, imposed a tax cap, remitted the Jan. 1, 1880 coupon payment, redirected coupon tax money to general expenses, restricted civil liberties of former slaves, and through Article 257 explicitly dishonored the bonds (slide 6).

**The Litigation Chessboard.** Creditors had three strategic options (slide 7). (1) Sue the officers -- blocked by Louisiana v. Jumel (1883), which treated suit against officers as suit against the state itself, holding that relief would "subvert" the state's financial administration (slide 7). (2) Assign claims to another state -- NH and NY enacted statutes allowing this, but the Court saw through it: the states were "nothing more nor less than a mere collecting agent" (slide 7). (3) In-state citizen + federal question: Hans is a Louisiana citizen suing Louisiana on a Contracts Clause claim; the 11th Amendment's text does not cover suits by a state's own citizens -- the last loophole (slide 7).

### Justice Bradley's Holding

**The Text Problem.** Bradley admitted the text does not cover this case (slide 8). The 11th Amendment bars suits by citizens of other states or foreign citizens against a state; Hans is a Louisiana citizen suing Louisiana.

**Bradley's Move.** Bradley asked: "Can we suppose that, when the eleventh amendment was adopted, it was understood to be left open for citizens of a state to sue their own state in the federal courts, while the idea of suits by citizens of other states was indignantly repelled?" (slide 8). He read the 11th Amendment not as an exhaustive boundary but as one illustration of a deeper structural principle: sovereign immunity is a background postulate of the constitutional order (slide 8). He invoked Hamilton's Federalist No. 81: "It is inherent in the nature of sovereignty not to be amenable to the suit of an individual without its consent" (slide 8). The holding: a state cannot be sued in federal court by its own citizen, even on a federal constitutional claim, without the state's consent (slide 8).

**The Problem.** Bradley was solving a problem about bonds; he created a doctrine that limits civil rights (slide 9).

### The Consequences Nobody Predicted

The deck traces three forks after Hans (slide 9): incorporation arrives (1897-2025), gradually applying the Bill of Rights to states via the 14th Amendment; Section 1983 wakes up in Monroe v. Pape (1961), becoming the primary vehicle for civil rights suits against state actors; but Hans is still there, leaving states themselves immune from suit without consent (slide 9).

The cascade: Seminole Tribe v. Florida (1996) -- Congress cannot use Article I powers to abrogate state sovereign immunity (slide 9). Alden v. Maine (1999) -- extended to state courts: states are immune even in their own courts under commerce power (slide 9). FMC v. SC Ports Authority (2002) -- extended to federal agency proceedings (slide 9).

The narrow exception is Section 5 of the 14th Amendment: Fitzpatrick v. Bitzer (1976) (9-0) held that "the Eleventh Amendment, and the principle of state sovereignty which it embodies, are necessarily limited by the enforcement provisions of §5 of the Fourteenth Amendment" because the 14th Amendment was ratified after the 11th and its provisions are "by express terms directed at the States" (slide 10). But this exception is narrower than it sounds: Congress must enforce the 14th Amendment's substantive guarantees with "congruence and proportionality," and many federal statutes -- environmental, patent, copyright, disability -- rely on the Commerce Clause or other Article I powers and cannot override state sovereign immunity under Seminole Tribe (slide 10).

**Ex Parte Young (1908).** The deck notes briefly that Ex Parte Young allows suits against individual officers for prospective injunctive relief -- a "double fiction" workaround -- but does not allow money damages from the state treasury (slide 10).

### The Lesson: Decide Narrowly

A decision about money became a decision about liberty; a decision rooted in the anxieties of the 1880s became a permanent feature of the constitutional architecture, constraining the enforcement of rights that did not even exist when Hans was decided (slide 11). Bradley could have decided Hans narrowly; instead he announced a sweeping structural principle that, once loose in the world, attached itself to problems Bradley never imagined -- disability rights, employment discrimination, environmental law, patent infringement by state universities (slide 11). The gap between what the deciding court was worried about and what the decision eventually controlled is the strongest argument for judicial minimalism (slide 11).

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

**The "What If Bradley Had Decided Narrowly?" Question.** The lecture asks students to construct a narrower holding for Hans -- one that solves the bondholder problem without announcing sovereign immunity as a structural constitutional principle. Options: read the Eleventh Amendment to cover suits by a state's own citizens on purely textual/purposivist grounds; hold that the specific mechanism (citizen-to-state suit on a bond claim) fails for standing or justiciability reasons; or hold that the Contracts Clause claim does not overcome the background principle of state immunity from damages while preserving injunctive or mandamus relief. What would sovereign immunity doctrine look like today if Bradley had taken any of these paths?

**The Section 1983 Interplay.** If Hans had never been decided, would Section 1983 provide adequate remedies for federal constitutional violations by states? The argument for yes: Section 1983 allows suits against state officers in their individual capacities, and municipalities are suable after Monell (1978). The argument for no: state agencies themselves (not just individual officers) are often the most efficient defendants, and Hans-based sovereign immunity blocks those suits.

**The Disability Rights Problem.** Title II of the ADA, passed under the Commerce Clause, provides that no qualified individual with a disability may be excluded from state programs. After Seminole Tribe and without a sufficient Section 5 nexus, states can violate Title II with relative impunity from private suits for damages. The Court partially addressed this in Tennessee v. Lane (2004), but the doctrine remains complex. Is this a defensible result?

## Key Takeaways

- In 1890, the Bill of Rights did not apply to the states. Bradley was not building an obstacle to civil rights enforcement -- he was stopping bondholders. The obstacle to civil rights came later, when civil rights became judicially enforceable but Hans was already in place.
- Bradley went beyond the Eleventh Amendment's text to announce sovereign immunity as a structural constitutional postulate. The words "sovereignty" and "sovereign immunity" appear nowhere in the Constitution.
- Hans closed the last litigation loophole for creditors trying to force Southern states to honor repudiated Reconstruction-era bond obligations.
- The cascade of consequences -- Seminole Tribe, Alden, FMC -- extended Hans far beyond its original context. Only Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment provides a reliable abrogation pathway.
- Ex Parte Young is the practical safety valve: prospective injunctive relief against individual state officers is available, but money damages from the state treasury are not.
- The lesson: a decision rooted in the anxieties of the 1880s became a permanent feature of the constitutional architecture, constraining the enforcement of rights that did not exist when Hans was decided. Courts should generally decide cases narrowly to limit unforeseeable doctrinal damage.
