## Professor Emphasis

### From source

The deck frames the Eleventh Amendment as central to federalism because it controls "the extent to which a federal judiciary can force the states themselves to conform to federal norms" -- whether *Hunter's Lessee* is otherwise enough, and how much the federal government can get a state where it hurts *(slide 2)*. Bradley's *Hans* opinion is presented through four questions: does Bradley admit a lack of textual support; how does he extend immunity; what are the jurisprudential consequences; what are the practical implications? *(slide 11)*. The deck calls *Hans* a case that "a lot of people hate" and notes that the leading federal courts treatise refused even to mention its existence -- with the deck's qualifier "wait until the course is over before you decry its lack of textual support" *(slide 13)*. The deck pairs *Seminole Tribe* with the question whether any justice on both *Union Gas* and *Seminole Tribe* changed their mind ("Nope, change in composition of the court") *(slide 20)*. Souter's *Seminole* dissent is framed by four prompts: what is the relevance of an earlier draft of the 11th Amendment; in what case did the courts go off the tracks; what pressured it to do so; what is Souter's creation myth? *(slide 22)*. The *Alden* slide tags the doctrinal move as "the federalism speech" and "the we-can-figure-out-the-bargain theory," and asks how much reservation statements from ratification debates should matter *(slide 25)*. The deck connects *Alden*'s ahistorical-literalism critique to *Griswold* and *Meyers v. Nebraska* as parallels *(slide 26)*. The closing emphasis: how to enforce FLSA against the state after *Alden* -- via *Ex Parte Young* injunctive relief, officer-personal-asset damages, federal suits, or explicit state waiver, but never retroactive monetary relief from the treasury *(slides 31, 33-34)*.

### Editor's framing

This page treats the doctrinal arc as Chisholm → Hans → Fitzpatrick → Union Gas → Seminole Tribe → Alden, with the Katz anomaly and the FMC/Hyatt aftermath -- a packaging that closely tracks the deck's chronology. ## Lecture Outline

**Origins: Chisholm and the Eleventh Amendment**

*Chisholm v. Georgia* (1793) held that a South Carolinian could sue Georgia in the Supreme Court for a debt and that Georgia could not assert sovereign immunity -- the Constitution explicitly gave federal courts (and the Supreme Court original) jurisdiction *(slide 3)*. Congress responded with the Eleventh Amendment: "The Judicial power of the United States shall not be construed to extend to any suit in law or equity, commenced or prosecuted against one of the United States by Citizens of another State, or by Citizens or Subjects of any Foreign State" *(slide 4)*. The deck flags the asymmetry: a maximal-immunity drafter might have stopped at the underlined "any suit in law or equity" rather than restricting only out-of-state and foreign citizens *(slide 4)*. Through the 19th century, sovereign immunity doctrine applied only when the state itself was sued -- not when state officials were sued in their official capacity, and that distinction remains the law *(slide 5)*.

**Hans v. Louisiana (1890) and the Extension Beyond Text**

The Post-Civil-War debt cases burdened reintegrating Southern states with massive obligations; 350 cases were litigated in the Supreme Court *(slide 6)*. Louisiana's Reconstruction-era political turmoil -- two parallel governments, four constitutions in less than a decade, declining Union troop presence, the Colfax Massacre, the White League's Battle of Liberty Place, and pervasive corruption -- set the political context *(slides 7-8)*. The Reconstruction Louisiana government had borrowed $87,500 from Hans in 1874; by 1879, Democrats in power explicitly dishonored the bonds in Article 257 of the new Louisiana constitution and restructured the courts *(slide 9)*. Hans, a Louisianan, sued Louisiana in federal circuit court for interest on the bonds rather than going to state court; the alternate path -- state court appeal to the U.S. Supreme Court under *Fairfax's Devisee v. Hunter's Lessee* -- would have permitted SCOTUS to re-decide both sovereign immunity and the merits *(slide 10)*.

Bradley's majority is presented through four pedagogical questions: does Bradley admit a lack of textual support; how does he extend immunity; what are the jurisprudential consequences; what are the practical implications? *(slide 11)*. The deck reproduces Bradley's key passage: appealing to the letter was "an attempt to strain the constitution and the law to a construction never imagined or dreamed of," and the supposition that a proviso permitting in-state-citizen suits would have been adopted was "almost an absurdity on its face" *(slide 12)*. The deck identifies the language as channeling *Prigg v. Pennsylvania* *(slide 12)*. The deck flags that *Hans* is hated -- the leading federal courts treatise refused to mention its existence -- but cautions students to wait until the course is over before decrying its lack of textual support *(slide 13)*.

**The 14th Amendment Exception: Fitzpatrick v. Bitzer (1976)**

The Civil Rights Act of 1964 was amended in 1972 to expressly add governments as Title VII defendants for race and sex employment discrimination; the deck notes that possibly state employment is not commerce -- but the 14th Amendment is available because the suit fights a state's failure to provide equal protection *(slide 14)*. *Fitzpatrick* (9-0) held that the Eleventh Amendment and the principle of state sovereignty it embodies are "necessarily limited by the enforcement provisions of section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment" -- which expressly grants Congress authority to enforce the substantive provisions of the Fourteenth Amendment by appropriate legislation *(slide 15)*. The deck asks the next question: do we even need Section 5, or could the commerce clause work just as well (Heart of Atlanta, McClung)? -- noting that *Fitzpatrick* did not need to resolve it *(slide 16)*.

**Pennsylvania v. Union Gas (1989) and Seminole Tribe (1996)**

In *Pennsylvania v. Union Gas* (1989), CERCLA authorized private parties to sue states for hazardous-waste-site operation; without Section 5 and with only the commerce clause available, the question was whether Congress could abrogate state sovereign immunity through Article I -- a move that, if accepted, would get back close to Chisholm *(slide 17)*. The Court could not get a majority opinion; Brennan argued each *Fitzpatrick* rationale "is as applicable to the Commerce Clause as it is to the Fourteenth Amendment" since the Commerce Clause "with one hand gives power to Congress while, with the other, it takes power away from the States" *(slide 18)*. Scalia's dissent for four justices said *Hans* was correct, *Fitzpatrick* was only about the 14th Amendment (which postdates the 11th), and Congress cannot abrogate state sovereign immunity when acting under the commerce power *(slide 19)*.

*Seminole Tribe of Florida v. Florida* (1996) is framed by five "consistent" prompts asking what *Fitzpatrick* and *Union Gas* held, how *Seminole* differs from *Hans* and *Bitzer*, and whether any justice on both *Union Gas* and *Seminole* changed their mind -- the answer being a change in the composition of the Court *(slide 20)*. The majority's textualism: "The dissent's lengthy analysis of the text of the Eleventh Amendment is directed at a straw man -- we long have recognized that blind reliance upon the text of the Eleventh Amendment is 'to strain the Constitution and the law to a construction never imagined or dreamed of'" *(slide 21)*. Stevens dissented along an anti-states-rights theme (copyright, patent, environmental law); Souter dissented on historical reading, prompting the deck's four-question probe about earlier drafts, when the courts went off the tracks, what pressured them, and what Souter's creation myth was *(slide 22)*.

The Katz anomaly: in *Central Virginia Community College v. Katz* (2006), Stevens wrote for the Court that sovereign immunity does not protect states in bankruptcy preference actions, with O'Connor switching sides; Indian tribes are not entitled to the same sovereign immunity (*Lac du Flambeau Band v. Coughlin* (2023)) *(slide 23)*.

**Alden v. Maine (1999): Immunity in State Courts**

In *Alden*, Congress created a commerce law requiring states to pay overtime under the FLSA; the statute purported to cover the state as employer, against the backdrop of *San Antonio v. Garcia* *(slide 24)*. Kennedy's majority delivered what the deck labels the "federalism speech": the more natural inference is that the Constitution preserved the States' traditional immunity from private suits "in light of its history and structure" -- the deck calls this "the we-can-figure-out-the-bargain theory" and asks how much reservation statements from ratification debates should matter *(slide 25)*. The deck's critique of the majority's method: ahistorical literalism is rejected in favor of history, practice, precedent, and constitutional structure; Congress had rejected weaker 11th Amendments restricted to situations where a state had previously made provision in their own courts; the deck tags this case for callback at *Griswold* and *Meyers v. Nebraska* *(slide 26)*. Souter, Stevens, Ginsburg, and Breyer dissented with a different view of history and a different feeling about *Hans* *(slide 27)*.

The aftermath: *Federal Maritime Commission v. South Carolina Ports Authority* (2002) extended *Alden* to federal agency proceedings; *Florida Department of State v. Treasure Salvors* held the 11th Amendment does not bar federal court jurisdiction in admiralty; the 11th Amendment usually does not protect municipalities but does protect state agencies *(slide 28)*. *Franchise Tax Board v. Hyatt* (2019) (5-4, Thomas) held that State B may not refuse to recognize State A's sovereign immunity, overturning *Nevada v. Hall* (1979) *(slide 29)*.

**The Ex Parte Young Escape Valve**

The deck poses the practical question: how the heck do you enforce FLSA against the State? *(slide 31)*. *Ex Parte Young* (1908) bars the 11th Amendment from suits against state officers for injunctive relief, even when adherence will cost a lot of money *(slide 31)*. A state officer can be sued for damages, and the state may voluntarily indemnify -- but executive immunity may apply, and only the official's personal assets are reachable, not state assets *(slide 31)*. Injunctions ordering retroactive monetary relief are barred (*Edelman v. Jordan* (1974)) *(slide 31)*. States can waive 11th Amendment immunity but the waiver must be explicit; they will do so to remove deterrence against contracting *(slide 31)*. The Feds can sue the state *(slide 31)*. The deck describes XPY as built on a double fiction: for the sovereign's immunity, a suit against an official is not a suit against the government; but for finding state action, the official's conduct is that of the state *(slide 32)*. The Court still adheres to the doctrine that some suits against officers are "really" against the state and are barred -- such as suits involving state property or relief requiring payment from the treasury to remedy past harms *(slide 33)*. The deck distinguishes individual-capacity suits (personal liability, officer's pocket, no 11th Amendment immunity but possibly common-law immunities) from official-capacity suits (against the state entity, treasury-payable, barred by 11th Amendment immunity), citing *Kentucky v. Graham* (1985) and *Ford Motor Co. v. Dep't of the Treas.* (1945) *(slide 34)*. Puerto Rico currently has sovereign immunity (*Ezratty v. Puerto Rico* (1st Cir. 1981)); other U.S. territories do not *(slide 35)*. The deck closes by flagging that the lecture only scratches the surface -- state agencies, TWIA, entities created by interstate compact, state officials, and official immunity are all rabbit holes *(slide 36)*.

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

**The FLSA/Garcia Problem:** How does a private party enforce the Fair Labor Standards Act against a state after Seminole Tribe and Alden? *(slide 31)*. The deck works through the remaining mechanisms: suits against state officers under Ex Parte Young for prospective injunctive relief (not back wages), the federal government suing the state directly (which sovereign immunity does not bar), and state waiver of immunity *(slide 31)*. Officers may also be sued in their individual capacity but only their personal assets are reachable *(slides 31, 34)*.

**Section 5 as the Key Variable:** Why does it matter whether Congress relies on Section 5 versus the Commerce Clause? Scope of remediation, the congruence-and-proportionality test under City of Boerne, and the degree of prophylactic legislation Congress can enact. The 11th Amendment doctrine is structurally linked to Section 5 enforcement power and City of Boerne -- understanding both is necessary to advise Congress on how to draft civil rights legislation that will survive an immunity challenge.

## Key Takeaways

- The Eleventh Amendment's text is narrower than its doctrine: it bars only out-of-state-citizen and foreign-citizen suits, but Hans extended immunity to all citizen suits against states in federal court on structural grounds.
- Congress may abrogate state sovereign immunity only when acting under the 14th Amendment (or other reconstruction amendments); Article I powers including the Commerce Clause are insufficient after Seminole Tribe.
- Alden extended immunity into state court proceedings and federal agency adjudications (FMC), making the doctrine nearly comprehensive absent Section 5 authority.
- Ex Parte Young provides the primary enforcement mechanism for constitutional rights: officers can be sued individually for prospective relief, but not for retroactive money damages (Edelman).
- The doctrine is contested at its historical foundations; Souter's Alden dissent argues the majority's creation myth misreads the evidence about pre-ratification understanding of immunity.
