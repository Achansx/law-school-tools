## Lecture Outline

### Case Background: Arizona SB 1070

In 2010, Arizona enacted Senate Bill 1070 to address illegal immigration (p. 2). The federal government sued for preemption (p. 2). The four challenged provisions (p. 2):

1. **Section 2(B):** the "show me your papers" requirement — state and local officers, when enforcing other laws, must determine the immigration status of any person where reasonable suspicion exists that they are unlawfully present (p. 2).
2. **Section 3:** state registration crime — created a state crime for failure to carry immigration registration documents, mirroring the federal requirement under 8 U.S.C. § 1304 (p. 2).
3. **Section 5(C):** criminalizing unauthorized work — made it a state crime for an unauthorized alien to apply for, solicit, or perform work (p. 2).
4. **Section 6:** warrantless arrests — authorized state officers to arrest without warrant any person they believed had committed a deportable offense (p. 2).

The district court enjoined the provisions; the Ninth Circuit affirmed (p. 3).

### Supreme Court Decision: 5-3 (Kennedy, J.)

The Court ruled 5-3 with Kennedy writing (p. 3). Sections 3, 5(C), and 6 were preempted; Section 2(B) was upheld "for now" (p. 3).

### Majority Reasoning: The Three Preemption Grounds

The deck identifies three preemption grounds — express, field, and conflict — and matches each SB 1070 provision to a specific ground (pp. 4-5).

**Section 3 (state registration crime): Field preemption.** The federal government occupies the entire field of alien registration (p. 4). Arizona's Section 3 — which imposed state criminal penalties for violating the same registration requirement — was preempted because states may not impose independent sanctions in a field Congress has chosen to regulate exclusively.

**Section 5(C) (unauthorized-alien work prohibition): Conflict preemption.** The conflict was with IRCA — the Immigration Reform and Control Act (p. 4). Congress made a deliberate choice not to criminalize unauthorized work by the alien themselves, and Arizona's Section 5(C) stood in direct conflict with that congressional design.

**Section 6 (warrantless arrest authority): Conflict preemption.** Section 6 undermines federal discretion over removal (p. 4). Federal immigration enforcement involves discretionary decisions about whether and how to pursue removal — decisions that depend on foreign policy considerations, prosecutorial resources, and humanitarian factors. An alien deemed deportable under Arizona's standard might be someone the federal government has decided not to pursue.

**Section 2(B) (immigration status check): Not facially preempted.** Section 2(B) survived because state officers conducting an immigration status check communicate with federal authorities — there is no facial conflict with federal law (p. 5). The majority expressly reserved future as-applied challenges (p. 5).

### Kennedy's Structural Rationale

The majority grounded its analysis in federal exclusivity in immigration: foreign relations implications, the need for uniform national policy, and the placement of removal discretion with the federal government (p. 5). The federal government has both the constitutional authority (Naturalization Clause, Plenary Power Doctrine) and the institutional capacity to make nuanced judgments about removal that states lack. This structural rationale — not just statutory text — is why field preemption sweeps broadly in immigration.

### Dissenting Views

**Scalia (dissenting):** States retain "inherent sovereignty" to exclude aliens from their territory (p. 6) — the majority's structural rationale is a federal-exclusivity claim without adequate textual or historical grounding.

**Thomas:** Would have applied a narrower conflict preemption analysis (p. 6) — a stricter impossibility standard rather than the broader "obstacle to federal purpose" approach.

**Alito:** Would have upheld the warrantless arrest authority (p. 6), viewing the disruption to federal discretion as insufficient to support preemption.

The originalism debate framed by the deck: the majority emphasizes historical federal primacy, while the dissent emphasizes that the Founders recognized state police powers (p. 6).

### Critical Perspectives

**Critical Race Theory:** Section 2(B) enables racial profiling and has a disproportionate impact on Latinx communities (p. 7). The "reasonable suspicion" standard, applied to immigration status, would in practice be applied based on ethnicity, language, and appearance.

**Feminist Theory:** Immigration enforcement creates increased vulnerability for immigrant women and risks family separation (p. 7).

**Economic Analysis:** Concerns about labor market distortions and the underground economy (p. 7).

**Legal Realism:** Reflects political polarization, with enforcement priorities shaped by ideology (p. 8).

**Pragmatism:** Federal-state coordination challenges and resource allocation issues (p. 8).

**Progressive Critique:** Creates a "chilling effect" in communities and undermines trust in law enforcement (p. 8).

### Post-Arizona Tensions: Sanctuary Cities and Enforcement Discretion

The deck flags federal preemption combined with inconsistent enforcement as creating a political vacuum, with states frustrated by perceived federal inaction (p. 9). Post-2012 developments listed on the deck include the sanctuary cities movement (now under attack), Remain in Mexico, and Title 42 expulsions (p. 10). The Arizona decision's logic runs in both directions: just as Arizona could not enforce immigration law more aggressively than federal law permits, federal courts after Arizona recognized limits on federal coercion of state and local governments into immigration enforcement (via the anti-commandeering principle from Printz). The "sanctuary cities" movement — where local governments declined to cooperate with federal detainer requests — drew on anti-commandeering logic. The Trump Administration's attempts to condition federal grants on immigration cooperation produced further preemption-in-reverse disputes. The deck closes by framing the fundamental questions as: who controls border policy, what is the proper federalism balance, and how to reconcile enforcement with civil rights (p. 10).

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

The lecture frames the core exam skill as preemption analysis: given a state law in a field with federal regulation, work through express preemption (text), field preemption (comprehensive federal scheme occupying the field), and conflict preemption (direct conflict with federal requirements or obstacle to federal purpose). Immigration is the paradigmatic field-preemption domain because of its foreign affairs dimension. For other fields (environmental regulation, labor law, consumer protection), Congress often explicitly addresses preemption and leaves room for state regulation that imposes higher standards.

## Key Takeaways

- Arizona v. United States applies all three preemption doctrines to a single state statute, making it an excellent preemption teaching vehicle.
- Field preemption in immigration is broad because federal authority is comprehensive and the structural need for uniform national policy is strong.
- Conflict preemption focuses on interference with federal discretion -- in immigration, that discretion includes prosecutorial choices about removal that depend on foreign policy, humanitarian, and resource considerations.
- Section 2(B)'s uphold on facial challenge signals that immigration status checks coordinated with federal authorities may be acceptable; prolonged detentions or discriminatory applications remain vulnerable to as-applied challenges.
- The decision feeds into broader anti-commandeering and sanctuary-city litigation by establishing that states cannot go further than federal law permits in immigration enforcement.
