## Professor Emphasis

### From source

This is a student-led review session, not a Chandler lecture — the deck is labeled "Con Law Review Session 2" and includes presenter-attribution markers ("-rhea" on the Barron slide and "MQD - Leo" on the West Virginia v. EPA slide) indicating the same Rhea-and-Leo presenter pairing as Tutor Review 1 (slides 1, 17, 24). Unlike Tutor Review 1 (structural Constitution and federalism), this session pivots to a different doctrinal stack: justiciability (political question, mootness), executive power (war powers, emergency powers, MQD, vetoes, removal, immunity), and the rights side of the Reconstruction Amendments (Barron baseline, Dred Scott, Contracts Clause and Bills of Attainder, 13th/14th, citizenship and the Privileges or Immunities Clause).

The political question section centers on Baker v. Carr as the doctrinal pivot from the pre-1962 "political thicket" shield (Colegrove) to the modern multifactor test, framing the change as Justice Brennan "retconning" Luther v. Borden by reading it as a Guarantee Clause case rather than an elections case (slide 2). The presenters tell students they do not need to memorize all six Baker factors but must know the "Big Two": textually demonstrable commitment and lack of judicially manageable standards (slide 2). Nixon v. United States is treated as the gold-standard textual-commitment case: the Senate's "sole Power to try all Impeachments" under Article I, § 3, with the Court focused on the word "sole," and the structural reason that judicial review of impeachments would let the Judiciary "grade its own homework" (slide 3). Rucho v. Common Cause is the manageable-standards case: Chief Justice Roberts saying there is no judicially manageable standard for partisan gerrymandering, Justice Kagan's dissent arguing Big Data and histograms provide a standard, and the rule that partisan gerrymandering claims are non-justiciable in federal court though they may remain live in state courts (slide 4). The PQD section also frames the doctrine as a function of separation of powers, with federal courts declining cases committed to another branch's discretion or lacking manageable standards (slide 5).

The mootness section covers the basic rule (a case is moot when post-filing events eliminate the plaintiff's personal stake), the timing difference from standing (mootness is assessed throughout litigation, not just at filing), the capable-of-repetition-yet-evading-review exception, and the class-action exception (slide 6). The voluntary-cessation exception is then unpacked separately: it prevents defendants from "gaming" the system by stopping illegal conduct just before an adverse ruling, and the defendant bears a "formidable burden" of showing it is "absolutely clear" the behavior cannot reasonably be expected to recur (slide 7).

The executive-power section runs in three blocks. War powers: The Prize Cases (1862) — Lincoln's blockade of Confederate ports while Congress was out of session was lawful because the President as commander-in-chief may use force against rebelling states during a civil war without waiting for a formal declaration, and Congress later confirmed the action (slide 8). The War Powers Resolution: the President must notify Congress within 48 hours of deploying armed forces, and military engagement is limited to 60 days with a 30-day withdrawal period absent congressional authorization (slide 9). Emergency powers: Youngstown (1952) — Truman's steel-mill seizure during the 1952 strike was unconstitutional because Congress had rejected such powers and the Constitution does not permit the executive to make law or unilaterally resolve labor disputes (slide 10). Jackson's Youngstown concurrence supplies the three-tier framework: actions with congressional approval, actions in the "zone of twilight" without approval, and actions against Congress's will (slide 11). Dames & Moore v. Regan (1981) upheld the IEEPA-grounded suspension of Iranian-asset litigation and transfer to a claims tribunal, citing longstanding congressional acquiescence (slide 12). The Hirabayashi/Korematsu/Endo trilogy is treated as a single emergency-powers block: Hirabayashi (1943) upheld the curfew on Japanese-ancestry citizens as a wartime measure; Korematsu (1944) upheld the exclusion order under strict scrutiny because of national-security concerns; Ex parte Endo (1944) ordered Endo's release because the War Relocation Authority lacked statutory authority to detain loyal American citizens (slides 14-16).

The major-questions block ("MQD - Leo") moves chronologically from West Virginia v. EPA (2022) to Biden v. Nebraska (2023) to Learning Resources v. Trump (2026) (slides 17-19). West Virginia is the founding statement: an agency claiming authority for matters of "vast economic and political significance" must point to clear congressional authorization, not ambiguous statutory text; MQD is typically triggered when an agency claims unprecedented power, relies on an obscure statute, or makes a move that will cost billions or reshape industries (slide 17). Biden v. Nebraska shifted MQD from rulemaking to executive spending — the HEROES Act's "waive or modify" language did not clearly authorize wholesale debt cancellation (slide 18). Learning Resources v. Trump (2026) extends MQD to IEEPA-grounded tariffs and is characterized as "non-delegation doctrine 2.0," with Gorsuch reading MQD as a strict clear-statement rule protecting Article I, Barrett treating it as standard textualism, and Kagan agreeing with the majority but saying MQD was not necessary because normal statutory interpretation would resolve the case (slide 19).

The vetoes block covers Chadha (legislative vetoes are unconstitutional because Congress acting with legislative effect must follow bicameralism and presentment), Raines v. Byrd (Congress lacked standing to sue over the Line Item Veto Act), and Clinton v. City of New York (line-item veto held unconstitutional once affected parties brought a justiciable challenge; the President must sign or veto a bill as written) (slide 20). The removal block organizes Myers (1926, at-will removal of purely executive officers), Humphrey's Executor (1935, FTC commissioners with for-cause protection upheld), and Seila Law (2020, single-director independent agencies with for-cause protection are unconstitutional and Humphrey's is narrowed to multi-member boards), with the removal rule that the President generally has unrestricted removal power subject to two narrow for-cause exceptions: quasi-legislative/quasi-judicial boards (Humphrey's) and inferior officers with limited duties (Morrison) (slide 21).

The privilege and immunity block covers United States v. Nixon (1974, executive privilege is not absolute and generalized confidentiality claims yield to a demonstrated specific need in a pending criminal trial), Nixon v. Fitzgerald (1982, former presidents are absolutely immune from civil damages for acts within the "outer perimeter" of official responsibility), Clinton v. Jones (1997, no temporary immunity for unofficial and pre-presidential acts), and Trump v. Vance (2020, sitting Presidents are not absolutely immune from state criminal subpoenas for personal records) (slide 22). Trump v. United States (2024) supplies the most recent framework: absolute immunity for core constitutional powers (Tier 1), presumptive immunity for other official acts within the outer perimeter (Tier 2), and no immunity for purely private conduct (Tier 3); the privilege rule from the same case treats presidential communications as presumptively privileged but not absolutely so (slide 23).

The rights-side block opens with Barron v. Baltimore (1833): the Bill of Rights does not apply to state governments because its purpose was to protect against the federal government, not state governments — if it had been meant to restrain the states it would have said so expressly (slide 24). Dred Scott (1857) held that slaves and descendants of slaves could never be U.S. citizens and could not sue in federal court, invalidated the Missouri Compromise on the theory that Congress lacked power to divvy up the territories, and is presented as a contributing cause of the Civil War (slide 25). The Contracts Clause is introduced via Article I, § 10 and Home Building & Loan v. Blaisdell (1934): Minnesota's Depression-era mortgage moratorium did not violate the Clause, the case established the "emergency exception doctrine," and the deck offers a three-part impairment test — substantial impairment of contractual expectations, legitimate public purpose tied to health/safety/welfare, and reasonable and narrowly tailored balance (slide 26). The Bills of Attainder slide gives both the federal prohibition (Article I, § 9) and state prohibition (Article I, § 10) and the United States v. Lovett test: targets identifiable individuals or groups, imposes traditional punishment, and bypasses judicial due process (slide 27).

The Reconstruction-amendments block (slides 28-37) walks through the 13th Amendment (abolishing slavery with a section-2 enforcement power and applying to private conduct), the four main provisions of § 1 of the 14th Amendment, and the citizenship clause via Elk v. Wilkins (14th Amendment did not grant citizenship to Native Americans because of allegiance to their tribes; Congress later made them citizens via the Snyder Act) and Wong Kim Ark (a person born in the U.S. to lawfully present foreign parents is a citizen under jus soli) (slides 29-31). The deck flags Trump v. Barbara as a current challenge to the Wong Kim Ark interpretation that the children of unlawfully present persons are citizens (slide 31). The Slaughterhouse Cases and Cruikshank are treated as the cases that "severely weakened the Reconstruction Amendments, which helped enable the Jim Crow era" by narrowing the Privileges or Immunities Clause to a handful of national-citizenship rights, which is why the doctrine had to "stretch incorporation (for enumerated rights) and substantive due process (for unenumerated rights)" to do the work P or Is was supposed to do (slides 32-34). The Article IV P&I clause and the 14th Amendment P or I clause are presented in side-by-side comparison: Article IV protects state-citizenship fundamental rights against discrimination against out-of-state residents (Corfield gives examples — conducting business, owning property, court access), while the 14th Amendment clause was read narrowly in Slaughterhouse to protect only national-citizenship rights like interstate travel (slide 35). The Civil Rights Cases (1883) closed the block: the Civil Rights Act of 1875 was unconstitutional because the 14th Amendment only restricts state action, Congress cannot remedy private discrimination through § 5, and the 13th Amendment did not reach inns and theaters as "badges or incidents of slavery" (slide 36). The deck closes with the bare proposition that only state actors can violate the 14th Amendment (slide 37).

## Lecture Outline

### Political Question Doctrine — From "Political Thicket" to Baker

The pre-1962 baseline treated electoral cases as a "political thicket" the Court refused to enter, with Colegrove v. Green as the shield (slide 2). Justice Brennan in Baker v. Carr "retconned" Luther v. Borden by reading it not as a non-justiciability based on the political nature of elections but as a Guarantee Clause case, since the Court has effectively buried the Guarantee Clause as a source of judicial power (slide 2). Baker thus opened the door to the "One Person, One Vote" era — a case being political in nature does not make it a political question in law (slide 2). The deck flags Baker's six factors but tells students they only need the "Big Two": textually demonstrable constitutional commitment to another branch, and the lack of judicially manageable standards (slide 2).

Nixon v. United States is the gold-standard textual-commitment case. Judge Walter Nixon challenged the Senate's use of a committee rather than the full Senate to hear his impeachment evidence; the Court focused on Article I, § 3's grant of "sole Power to try all Impeachments" — if the Senate has the "sole" power, the Judiciary has no power to review the Senate's methods (slide 3). The structural reason: judicial review of impeachments would let the Judiciary "grade its own homework" if the impeachment in question involved a judge (slide 3). The deck reads "try" as still meaningful — it means the Senate gets to define what a "trial" looks like in its unique constitutional role (slide 3).

Rucho v. Common Cause is the manageable-standards case. The deck frames the doctrinal puzzle as a question of measurement: everyone agrees partisan gerrymandering is undemocratic, but the question is how much is too much, and Chief Justice Roberts argued there is no judicially manageable standard to distinguish "fair" districting from "unconstitutional" partisanship — any line drawn would be a political policy choice rather than a legal one (slide 4). Justice Kagan's dissent argued that Big Data and histograms do provide a standard: if a map is a 1-in-10,000 statistical outlier designed to favor one party, that is a manageable standard of excessive partisanship (slide 4). The rule: partisan gerrymandering claims are non-justiciable in federal court but remain live in state courts under state constitutions (slide 4).

The deck closes the PQD section by reframing it as a function of separation of powers: federal courts decline to hear cases that the Constitution has committed to another branch or for which there are no manageable legal standards (slide 5).

### Mootness — Voluntary Cessation

A case is moot when events after the initial filing eliminate the plaintiff's personal stake; unlike standing, which is assessed at the time of filing, mootness must be maintained throughout litigation (slide 6). If there is no longer a live case or controversy that the court can redress, Article III jurisdiction is lost and the case must be dismissed (slide 6). The deck lists two stock exceptions: capable-of-repetition-yet-evading-review (an injury too fast for full judicial review, like pregnancy or election cycles, that is likely to recur to the same plaintiff), and class action (once a class is certified the case remains live even if the named plaintiff's individual claim becomes moot) (slide 6).

Voluntary cessation is treated separately because it is designed to prevent defendants from gaming the system by stopping illegal conduct just before an adverse ruling (slide 7). The defendant carries a "formidable burden" of proving that the case is truly moot and must show it is "absolutely clear" that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur (slide 7).

### Presidential War Powers — The Prize Cases and the War Powers Resolution

The Prize Cases (1862) supplies the wartime baseline: while Congress was not in session, Lincoln issued an executive order establishing a naval blockade of Confederate ports and warning that vessels violating it would be captured as prizes; Congress later passed a statute authorizing the blockade (slide 8). The Court held that a civil war is a state of war and the President has discretion to respond to such emergencies; later congressional approval confirmed the legality of the action (slide 8). The rule: the President as commander-in-chief may use force against rebelling states during a civil war without waiting for a formal declaration of war from Congress (slide 8).

The War Powers Resolution adds a statutory layer: the President must notify Congress within 48 hours of deploying armed forces, and military engagement is limited to 60 days (with a 30-day withdrawal period) absent congressional authorization (slide 9).

### Presidential Emergency Powers — Youngstown, Dames & Moore, and the Internment Trilogy

Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer (1952) supplies the emergency-power baseline: when Truman seized steel mills during an impending 1952 strike, the Court held the President lacked constitutional or statutory authority because Congress had rejected such powers and the Constitution does not permit the executive to make laws or unilaterally resolve labor disputes (slide 10). The rule: the President may not engage in lawmaking activity absent express authorization from Congress or the text of the Constitution (slide 10). Jackson's concurrence supplies the three-tier framework — actions with congressional approval, actions without approval in the "zone of twilight," and acts against Congress's will (slide 11).

Dames & Moore v. Regan (1981) is the post-Youngstown application: after the Iran hostage crisis, Carter froze Iranian assets and Reagan ordered related litigation transferred to a claims tribunal; the Court upheld both actions under the IEEPA, citing longstanding congressional acquiescence and executive practice (slide 12). The rule: the President may settle claims with foreign governments via executive order when necessary for foreign-policy resolution and with Congress's implicit approval (slide 12). The deck notes a connection to Learning Resources v. Trump (slide 13).

The Hirabayashi/Korematsu/Endo trilogy is presented as a continuation of emergency powers rather than as an equal protection case. Hirabayashi v. United States (1943) upheld a curfew on persons of Japanese ancestry under Executive Order 9066 as a constitutional wartime measure to prevent sabotage, holding that the curfew did not violate due process or constitute unconstitutional discrimination (slide 14). Korematsu v. United States (1944) upheld an exclusion order removing Japanese Americans from military areas under strict scrutiny because of national-security concerns, while also stating the rule that government restrictions targeting a specific racial group are inherently suspect and subject to strict scrutiny (slide 15). Ex parte Endo (1944) held that the War Relocation Authority lacked statutory authority to detain loyal American citizens, so Endo had to be released (slide 16).

### Major Questions Doctrine — From West Virginia v. EPA to Learning Resources

The MQD block walks chronologically through three cases. West Virginia v. EPA (2022) is the doctrinal founding statement: in a 6-3 decision the Court held that an agency claiming authority for matters of "vast economic and political significance" must point to a clear congressional authorization rather than relying on ambiguous statutory text (slide 17). MQD is typically triggered when an agency claims unprecedented power, relies on an obscure statute, or makes a move that will cost billions or reshape industries (slide 17).

Biden v. Nebraska (2023) extended MQD beyond agency rulemaking into executive spending: the Secretary of Education invoked the HEROES Act's "waive or modify" provision to cancel $430 billion in student debt, and the Court held the power to "waive or modify" did not clearly authorize wholesale cancellation of massive amounts of debt (slide 18).

Learning Resources v. Trump (2026) extends MQD again, this time to tariffs grounded in the IEEPA: Trump invoked the IEEPA to impose huge tariffs on Canada, Mexico, and China (about $130 billion collected), and the Court held that IEEPA's authorization to "regulate" importation did not carry with it the power to tax it (slide 19). The deck reads this as proving MQD's role as "non-delegation doctrine 2.0" — rather than knocking down statutes wholesale, MQD narrows the claimed authority; the decision was reached through congressional "power of the purse" reasoning combined with staggering economic impact and a lack of historical IEEPA precedent (slide 19). Concurrences split: Gorsuch read MQD as a strict clear-statement rule protecting Article I, Barrett treated it as standard textualism, and Kagan agreed with the majority but said MQD was not needed because normal statutory interpretation was enough (slide 19). The summary rule: the executive needs clear congressional authorization to exercise transformative power of vast economic or political significance, operating as a strict interpretive constraint with no exceptions for emergencies or foreign affairs (slide 19).

### Vetoes — Chadha, Raines, and Clinton

INS v. Chadha (1983) struck down legislative vetoes as unconstitutional: Chadha overstayed a student visa, the Attorney General used statutory discretion to suspend deportation, the House passed a resolution vetoing the AG's suspension, and the Court held that congressional action with legislative effect must follow bicameralism and presentment (slide 20). The deck packages the takeaway as "don't do legislative vetoes" because they circumvent the constitutional lawmaking process (slide 20).

Line-item vetoes follow: Raines v. Byrd held that Congress lacked standing to challenge the Line Item Veto Act; Clinton v. City of New York held that affected parties had standing when Clinton used the line-item veto to cancel specific tax-and-spending provisions, and the Court held the Line Item Veto Act unconstitutional because the President must sign or veto a bill as written (slide 20). Summary: no vetoes other than whole vetoes (slide 20).

### Removal Power — Myers, Humphrey's, Morrison, Seila Law

Myers v. United States (1926): Wilson fired a postmaster without Senate consent; the President may remove purely executive officers at will, and Congress cannot require Senate consent for those removals (slide 21). Humphrey's Executor (1935): FDR fired a member of the FTC over policy disagreements despite a for-cause-only statute; the Court ruled against FDR because Congress may create independent agencies with quasi-legislative and quasi-judicial functions and protect their principal officers from at-will removal (FTC, SEC, NLRB, the Fed are common examples) (slide 21). Seila Law v. CFPB (2020): a single-director agency with for-cause protection is unconstitutional, narrowing Humphrey's to multi-member boards (slide 21).

The removal rule: the President generally has unrestricted power to remove executive officers, subject to two narrow for-cause exceptions — quasi-legislative/quasi-judicial boards (Humphrey's Executor) and inferior officers with limited duties (Morrison) (slide 21).

### Executive Privilege and Presidential Immunity

United States v. Nixon (1974): a Watergate prosecutor subpoenaed Nixon's audiotapes for a criminal trial; the Court held executive privilege is not absolute and a generalized claim of confidentiality must yield to a demonstrated specific need for evidence in a pending criminal trial (slide 22).

Nixon v. Fitzgerald (1982): an Air Force analyst sued Nixon for retaliatory termination after blowing the whistle on cost overruns; the Court held former Presidents are absolutely immune from civil damages for all acts within the "outer perimeter" of their official responsibility (slide 22).

Clinton v. Jones (1997): the Court held that a sitting President does not have temporary immunity from civil litigation for unofficial and pre-presidential acts (slide 22).

Trump v. Vance (2020): a DA subpoenaed Trump's accounting firm for personal financial records as part of a state criminal investigation; the Court held a sitting President does not have absolute immunity from state criminal subpoenas for private papers or records (slide 22).

Trump v. United States (2024) supplies the most recent framework: Tier 1 — absolute immunity for core constitutional powers (pardoning, removing officials); Tier 2 — presumptive immunity for other official acts within the outer perimeter; Tier 3 — no immunity for purely private conduct (slide 23). The privilege rule from the same case treats presidential communications as presumptively privileged but not absolutely so, and the privilege must yield to a demonstrated specific need for evidence in a pending criminal trial (slide 23).

### Barron and Pre-Fourteenth Amendment Baseline

Barron v. Baltimore (1833): the City of Baltimore took property for public use without compensation, and Barron argued this violated the Fifth Amendment takings clause (slide 24). The Court held that the Bill of Rights does not apply to state governments — its purpose was to protect against the federal government, not state governments, and if it had been meant to restrain the states it would have said so expressly (slide 24). The Court noted that certain things are still off-limits to states under the original Constitution (no ex post facto laws, no titles of nobility, no religious tests for office), but states may presumably do anything else (slide 24). The doctrinal implication: Barron limited the scope of individual rights against state governments until the Fourteenth Amendment enabled selective incorporation, allowing many Bill of Rights protections to apply to states over time — but Barron and Article I, § 10 are still good law (slide 24).

### Dred Scott

The Missouri Compromise prohibited slavery in certain territories (slide 25). Dred Scott ended up in Missouri (a slave state) and believed that the moment he crossed into free territory he became a free man; the case reached the Supreme Court through diversity jurisdiction (slide 25). The Court held that slaves and descendants of slaves could never be citizens of the United States and therefore could not sue in federal court (slide 25). The deck frames the opinion as an originalism/intent-of-the-founders defense: the authors of the Constitution were slave owners, and giving privileges and immunities to freed slaves would have made them hypocrites (slide 25). The ruling invalidated the Missouri Compromise on the theory that Congress lacked power to divvy up the territories, and by preventing Congress from legislating the geographics of slavery, the decision helped bring about the Civil War (slide 25).

### Contracts Clause and Bills of Attainder

Article I, § 10 prohibits states from passing any Bill of Attainder, ex post facto law, or law impairing the obligation of contracts, or granting any title of nobility (slide 26). The Contracts Clause prohibits states from impairing already-existing contract obligations (slide 26). Home Building & Loan v. Blaisdell (1934): Minnesota enacted a Depression-era law pausing mortgage obligations, and lenders argued it violated the Contracts Clause; the Court held the Minnesota law did not violate the Clause and established the emergency-exception doctrine (slide 26). The deck offers a three-part impairment test: substantial impairment of contractual expectations, a legitimate public purpose tied to health, safety, or welfare, and a reasonable and narrowly tailored balance of interests (slide 26).

Bills of attainder are legislative acts that inflict punishment on a specific individual or group without a judicial trial (slide 27). The Constitution prohibits both federal and state bills of attainder — federal prohibition in Article I, § 9 and state prohibition in Article I, § 10 (slide 27). Under United States v. Lovett, a law qualifies as a bill of attainder if it targets identifiable individuals or groups (naming or effectively singling them out), imposes a traditional form of punishment, and bypasses judicial due process (slide 27).

### Reconstruction Amendments — 13th and 14th

The 13th Amendment's § 1 abolished slavery and involuntary servitude except as punishment for a crime; § 2 gave Congress authority to pass enforcement legislation; the deck flags that the 13th Amendment applies to private conduct too, not just state action (slide 29).

Section 1 of the 14th Amendment has four main provisions; the deck notes the course covers only the first two — the Citizenship Clause and the Privileges or Immunities Clause (slide 30). The Citizenship Clause overruled Dred Scott (slide 30).

Citizenship doctrine runs through Elk v. Wilkins and Wong Kim Ark (slide 31). Elk v. Wilkins held that the Fourteenth Amendment did not grant citizenship to Native Americans simply by leaving their tribes and living among non-Native citizens because "subject to the jurisdiction thereof" referred to political allegiance to the United States, and Elk had allegiance to his tribe — based on the notion that Indian tribes are their own sovereigns; Congress later made Native Americans citizens under the Snyder Act (slide 31). Wong Kim Ark held that a person born in the U.S. to lawfully present foreign parents is a citizen under jus soli (birthright citizenship), with an exception for the children of foreign diplomats (slide 31). The deck distinguishes Wong Kim Ark from Trump's executive order on birthright citizenship because Wong's parents were lawfully present, and the deck flags Trump v. Barbara as challenging the EO on the theory that "subject to the jurisdiction thereof" means citizenship does not automatically extend to persons born in the U.S.; the question presented is whether the children of unlawfully present persons are citizens (slide 31).

### The Privileges or Immunities Clause and Its Narrowing

Slaughterhouse and Cruikshank interpreted the Privileges or Immunities Clause to protect against almost nothing important, "severely weakened the Reconstruction Amendments, which helped enable the Jim Crow era," and narrowed P or I to rights like access to federal courts, protection on the high seas, and travel between states — which is why doctrine has had to "stretch incorporation (for enumerated rights) and substantive due process (for unenumerated rights)" to do the work P or I was supposed to do (slide 32).

The Slaughterhouse Cases: Louisiana granted a single corporation a monopoly on slaughterhouse operations in New Orleans for public health reasons; a group of butchers sued under the 14th Amendment's P or I Clause, arguing the law restricted their right to practice their trade (slide 33). The Court held that 14th Amendment P or I only protects rights gained by being a citizen of the United States, not rights held as citizens of the state, and that rights pre-dating the Bill of Rights are natural rights the federal government did not grant and so are not part of P or I (slide 33). The Clause protects only federal-law rights (interstate travel, access to federal institutions) and does not extend to state laws affecting economic liberties — significantly limiting the Fourteenth Amendment's reach and allowing states to continue restricting individual rights, particularly those of Black citizens during Reconstruction (slide 33).

United States v. Cruikshank: following the Colfax Massacre — white supremacists attacked Black citizens attempting to exercise their voting rights — several attackers were charged under the Enforcement Act of 1870, and they argued the federal government lacked authority to prosecute them (slide 34). The Court held the Bill of Rights is not protected by the P or I Clause, and the 14th Amendment only protects citizens against state actors, not private parties; the Second Amendment was held to protect only against federal infringement, not private actors or state laws — severely weakening federal enforcement of civil rights and allowing states to disregard protections for Black citizens, contributing to the rise of Jim Crow laws (slide 34).

The Article IV P&I clause and the 14th Amendment P or I clause are presented side by side. Article IV guarantees state-citizenship rights and prohibits state governments from discriminating against out-of-state residents without a substantial reason when denying fundamental rights — typically civil or commercial liberties like conducting business within a state, owning private property, or accessing a state's courts (Corfield gives examples); these rights are not absolute and only require equal treatment of non-state citizens (slide 35). The 14th Amendment P or I clause guarantees rights of national citizenship and prohibits states from abridging those rights, but because of Slaughterhouse the Clause is interpreted narrowly to protect only a handful of rights such as interstate travel and the right to reside in any state (slide 35).

### Civil Rights Cases and the State-Action Doctrine

The Civil Rights Cases (1883) combined multiple cases of discrimination against African Americans in inns, highways, and places of public accommodation in violation of the Civil Rights Act of 1875 (slide 36). The Court held the Act unconstitutional because the 14th Amendment only restricts state action, so Congress cannot remedy civil rights violations by private individuals or businesses; the extension of the statute to inns and theaters was also not justified under the 13th Amendment because such discrimination was not a "badge or incident of slavery" (slide 36).

The deck closes with the bare proposition that only state or state actors can violate the 14th Amendment (slide 37).

## Key Takeaways

- **The "Big Two" Baker factors suffice for PQD analysis on exams.** Textual commitment (Nixon v. United States) and manageable standards (Rucho) carry most of the doctrinal work; the other four factors are presented as memorization-optional (slide 2).
- **Voluntary cessation is the dominant mootness exception when a defendant stops conduct mid-litigation.** The "formidable burden" of "absolutely clear" non-recurrence is the operative phrase (slide 7).
- **Jackson's Youngstown three-tier framework remains the operative emergency-powers grid.** Approval, twilight, against-Congress — and Dames & Moore is the modern application that turns on congressional acquiescence (slides 11-12).
- **The internment trilogy belongs to "emergency powers," not equal protection in this deck.** Hirabayashi, Korematsu, and Endo are organized as exercises of presidential emergency power and only secondarily as race cases (slides 14-16).
- **MQD is "non-delegation doctrine 2.0" extending across rulemaking, spending, and tariffs.** West Virginia v. EPA — Biden v. Nebraska — Learning Resources v. Trump traces the doctrinal expansion (slides 17-19).
- **Removal-power exam structure: Myers default, Humphrey's exception, Morrison exception, Seila Law narrowing.** The President generally removes at will; for-cause protection survives only for multi-member quasi-legislative/quasi-judicial boards and inferior officers with limited duties (slide 21).
- **Trump v. United States's three-tier immunity framework is the current law.** Core powers absolute, official acts presumptive, private acts none (slide 23).
- **Slaughterhouse and Cruikshank are why we have incorporation and substantive due process.** P or I was supposed to do the work, but the early narrowing forced the doctrine to migrate (slide 32).
- **The state-action doctrine (Civil Rights Cases) bookends the rights side.** Only state or state actors can violate the 14th Amendment, which controls how Congress's § 5 power can reach private discrimination (slides 36-37).
