# Presidential Removal Power - Humphreys Executor to Trump v Slaughter

## Professor Emphasis

### From source

The deck frames the removal power arc as a century-long pendulum swing -- from Myers's broad presidential removal power, through Humphrey's, Morrison, Free Enterprise Fund, Seila Law, Collins, and now Slaughter *(slide 31)*. The congressional bargain is identified as a central theme: independence was the price of delegation, and Congress gave agencies enormous power precisely because they would be insulated from politics *(slide 8)*. The shadow docket assault is flagged as a discrete unit: Wilcox, Boyle, and Slaughter as a sequence of stays effectively permitting at-will removal while Humphrey's technically remains good law *(slides 4, 22)*. Chandler labels the shadow-docket sequence an "indecent burial" and contrasts it with Chevron's full public overruling in Loper Bright *(slide 23)*. Gorsuch's nondelegation agenda is flagged as a second front: if agencies lose both independence and rulemaking authority, the entire administrative state faces structural collapse *(slide 28)*.

### Editor's framing

This page treats the arc as a doctrinal pendulum with the congressional bargain as the through-line, organizing each case around what it did to the Myers/Humphrey's equilibrium. The framing of Seila Law and Slaughter as a coordinated narrowing of Humphrey's beyond what each opinion individually claims is editorial packaging. The "double-blow scenario" combining removal-power erosion with nondelegation revival is a synthesis of two slide threads (slides 28 + 31) that the deck keeps separate.

## Lecture Outline

### The Foundational Question

Who controls the administrative state -- the President who was elected, or the Congress that created these agencies and set the rules? *(slide 2)*. This question is the animating tension of every removal power case. The unitary executive theory answers: Article II vests ALL executive power in ONE President, and the Take Care Clause requires presidential control over everyone who executes the laws *(slide 13)*. The opposing view answers: Congress can structure agencies as the Necessary and Proper Clause allows, expertise requires insulation, and the Founders could not have anticipated the modern administrative state *(slide 13)*.

### Myers v United States (1926)

**The Facts.** President Wilson fired postmaster Frank Myers without Senate consent; an 1876 statute required Senate approval for removal, and Myers sued for back pay *(slide 5)*.

**The Holding (Taft, C.J.).** The President has constitutional power to remove executive officers, rooted in Article II's vesting of "the executive Power" and confirmed by the Take Care Clause and the Decision of 1789 *(slide 5)*. Congress cannot require Senate consent for removals *(slide 5)*. The opinion uses sweeping language: "removal of executive officials from office is an executive function," and the removal power is the "incident of the power to appoint" -- but the Senate's check on appointment does not extend to removal *(slide 5)*. Read broadly: the President has "illimitable" removal power over all executive officers *(slide 5)*.

### Humphrey's Executor v United States (1935)

**The Facts.** FDR fired FTC Commissioner William Humphrey with no statutory cause -- purely policy disagreement; Humphrey died and his executor sued for back pay *(slide 7)*. Same agency, same issue as today's Slaughter *(slide 7)*.

**The Holding (Sutherland, J.).** Congress may restrict removal of FTC commissioners to statutory causes; Myers is confined to "purely executive officers" like postmasters *(slide 7)*. The FTC's duties are "neither political nor executive" but quasi-judicial and quasi-legislative *(slide 7)*. Independence is essential: "one who holds his office only during the pleasure of another cannot be depended upon to maintain an attitude of independence" *(slide 7)*. The Court left the line between Myers and Humphrey's "for future consideration" *(slide 7)*.

**The Quasi-Agency Model.** The 1935 FTC was described as quasi-legislative -- it investigated and recommended policy to Congress and created industry standards -- and quasi-judicial -- it adjudicated complaints through formal hearings *(slide 6)*. The deck stresses it was NOT executive: it "enforced no policy except the policy of the law" *(slide 6)*. The 1935 FTC had no fining power, no monetary penalties, and had to go to court to enforce its cease-and-desist orders *(slides 6, 10)*. The Court would later observe in Seila Law that the modern FTC -- with rulemaking, massive penalties, merger blocking, and enforcement actions -- "would at the present time be considered executive" *(slide 10)*.

**The Congressional Bargain.** Independence was the price of delegation: Congress gave agencies enormous power precisely because they would be insulated from politics *(slide 8)*. This framework becomes central to Kagan's dissent in Seila Law and Trump v. Slaughter *(slide 8)*.

### Bowsher v Synar (1986)

**The Facts.** The Gramm-Rudman-Hollings Act (1985) gave the Comptroller General (head of the GAO) authority to calculate and enforce automatic spending cuts (sequestration) if deficit targets were exceeded *(slide 14)*. The Comptroller General was removable by Congress via joint resolution for "inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance" -- not by the President *(slide 14)*.

**The Holding (Burger, C.J., 7-2).** Congress cannot grant executive power to an officer under its own control: "the Constitution does not permit Congress to execute the laws; it follows that Congress cannot grant to an officer under its control what it does not possess" *(slide 14)*. "It is only the authority that can remove him . . . that he must fear and obey" *(slide 14)*. Remedy: struck the Comptroller General's sequestration role and triggered the statutory fallback of a joint resolution of Congress *(slide 14)*.

**The Road Not Taken.** Blackmun's dissent argued the Court should have severed Congress's removal power over the Comptroller General rather than invalidating the sequestration mechanism: "I cannot see the sense of invalidating legislation of this magnitude to preserve a 65-year-old removal power that has never been exercised" *(slide 15)*. The Court refused because the CG's duties extended far beyond Gramm-Rudman -- loan guarantees, civil penalties, ICA -- and the remedy would have subordinated him to the Executive Branch contrary to congressional intent *(slide 15)*. The Stevens/Marshall concurrence noted that functions are chameleon-like: "the great ordinances of the Constitution do not establish and divide fields of black and white" *(slide 15)*.

### Morrison v Olson (1988)

**The Facts.** The Ethics in Government Act created an independent counsel removable only for "good cause" by the Attorney General; the statute was upheld 7-1 (Kennedy recused) *(slide 16)*.

**The Functional Test.** Morrison is the high-water mark of congressional power over removal *(slide 16)*. The question is NOT whether the officer is "purely executive" -- the rigid Humphrey's categories are replaced by functional analysis *(slide 16)*. The question IS whether the restriction "impedes the President's ability to perform his constitutional duty" *(slide 16)*. Good-cause restriction for an inferior officer with limited duties does not "unduly trammel" executive authority *(slide 16)*. Morrison's functional test is now under attack as the Court returns to formalist categories *(slide 16)*.

**Scalia's Lone Dissent.** "This does not mean some of the executive power, but all of the executive power" *(slide 17)*. "Governmental investigation and prosecution of crimes is a quintessentially executive function" *(slide 17)*. "Without a secure structure of separated powers, our Bill of Rights would be worthless. . . . But this wolf comes as a wolf" *(slide 17)*. Once a lonely dissent, Scalia's view now commands a majority of the Court *(slide 17)*.

### Free Enterprise Fund v PCAOB (2010)

**The Problem.** PCAOB members were removable only for cause by the SEC; SEC commissioners were themselves removable only for cause by the President -- two layers of for-cause protection insulating PCAOB members from any effective presidential oversight *(slide 18)*.

**The Holding (Roberts, C.J., 5-4).** Dual for-cause removal is unconstitutional: "The President cannot 'take Care that the Laws be faithfully executed' if he cannot oversee the faithfulness of the officers who execute them" *(slide 18)*. Remedy: sever the PCAOB removal restriction so members are removable at will by the SEC *(slide 18)*.

**The Narrowing Begins.** The Court preserved Humphrey's single-layer for-cause protection but pointedly did not endorse it *(slide 18)*. Humphrey's and Morrison were treated as narrow "exceptions" to the Myers rule, not the other way around, and the majority's framework assumed at-will removal as the constitutional default *(slide 18)*.

### Seila Law LLC v CFPB (2020)

**The Problem.** The CFPB had a single Director, removable only for cause -- unlike the FTC, no multimember board and no partisan balance *(slide 19)*. The Director had vast unilateral power (rulemaking, enforcement, adjudication) and was not dependent on Congress for funding *(slide 19)*.

**The Holding (Roberts, C.J.).** Single-director for-cause protection is unconstitutional: "The CFPB Director's insulation from removal by an accountable President is enough to render the agency's structure unconstitutional" *(slide 19)*. The Court recognized only two exceptions to the presidential removal power: (1) expert multimember boards (Humphrey's), and (2) inferior officers with limited duties (Morrison) *(slide 19)*. The Court explicitly declined to "revisit Humphrey's Executor" but confined it to multimember expert agencies, noting that 1935 FTC powers "would at the present time be considered executive" -- the "dried husk" Chief Justice Roberts later described *(slide 19)*.

**Kagan's Dissent.** "The text of the Constitution, the history of the country, the precedents of this Court, and the need for sound and adaptable governance -- all stand against the majority's opinion" *(slide 20)*. The Constitution gives Congress wide leeway to limit the President's removal power "in the interest of enhancing independence from politics in regulatory bodies like the CFPB" *(slide 20)*. The congressional bargain: Congress can trade independence for delegation -- would Congress have delegated without it? *(slide 20)*.

### Collins v Yellen (2021)

The FHFA was structured identically to the CFPB -- single director, for-cause removal *(slide 21)*. Seila Law controlled and extended beyond the CFPB; the protection was unconstitutional *(slide 21)*. Thomas's concurrence went further: "Humphrey's Executor was wrongly decided" and should be overruled outright *(slide 21)*. This view gained open endorsement from Gorsuch (Humphrey's "poorly reasoned") and Roberts's "dried husk" characterization *(slide 21)*.

### The Shadow Docket Assault (2025)

**January-March 2025.** Trump fired NLRB member Wilcox and General Counsel Abruzzo, removed EEOC Democrats (Jan. 28), and fired FTC Commissioners Slaughter and Bedoya with no cause cited (March 18) *(slide 22)*. A February 18 Executive Order expanded White House control over independent agencies *(slide 22)*.

**May-September 2025.** The Court granted three shadow docket stays *(slide 4)*:
- Trump v. Wilcox (May 22): NLRB/MSPB members could remain fired, 6-3 along ideological lines *(slide 4)*
- Trump v. Boyle (July 23): CPSC commissioners, "squarely controlled by Wilcox," with Kavanaugh concurrence calling for cert before judgment *(slide 4)*
- Trump v. Slaughter (Sept. 22): FTC Commissioner Slaughter, stay plus cert before judgment granted *(slide 4)*

Lower courts had unanimously held the firings violated Humphrey's Executor *(slide 22)*. The stays effectively permitted at-will removal while Humphrey's technically remained "good law" -- "zombie jurisprudence" *(slide 22)*. Kagan dissented in all three orders: "Our emergency docket should never be used to permit what our own precedent bars. . . . Still more, it should not be used to transfer government authority from Congress to the President, and thus to reshape the Nation's separation of powers" *(slide 4)*. Kagan also described the Court as "layering nothing on top of nothing" -- "turtles all the way down" *(slide 22)*.

**The Contrast with Chevron.** Chevron got a full, public funeral in Loper Bright -- briefing, argument, a reasoned merits opinion *(slide 23)*. Humphrey's Executor is getting a back-alley execution via emergency applications -- Chandler labels this an "indecent burial" and "judicial fiat over reasoned deliberation" *(slide 23)*.

### Trump v Slaughter -- Oral Argument (December 8, 2025)

The Solicitor General argued for "exclusive and illimitable" presidential removal power, conceding possible exceptions only for the Federal Reserve (unique quasi-private structure) and non-Article III courts *(slide 25)*. Agarwal for Slaughter distinguished the multi-member FTC from the single-director CFPB, emphasized 90 years of reliance on Humphrey's, and argued Congress's "bargain" -- vast delegation in exchange for independence *(slide 25)*.

Justice-by-justice signals *(slide 25)*: Roberts called Humphrey's "a dried husk of whatever people used to think it was" -- near-certain to overrule. Thomas long committed; likely the strongest vote against Humphrey's. Gorsuch called it "poorly reasoned" and wants to reinvigorate the intelligible principle (nondelegation) doctrine. Kavanaugh was concerned about Federal Reserve independence and wants proper process but likely votes to overrule for FTC. Barrett pushed Sauer for limiting principles -- where does presidential power stop? Kagan is the strongest defender of the bargain framework: "Would Congress have delegated if it couldn't insulate?" Sotomayor the most vigorous defender of Humphrey's on the bench.

Predicted outcome: 6-3 to overrule or significantly narrow Humphrey's Executor *(slide 25)*. Decision expected June 2026 *(slide 32)*.

### Trump v Cook (Argued January 21, 2026)

Trump fired Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook -- the first time in 112 years a president has tried to fire a Fed governor; the administration alleged Cook committed mortgage fraud *(slide 27)*. The Wilcox majority had flagged the Fed as a "uniquely structured, quasi-private entity" following the tradition of the First and Second Banks of the United States *(slide 27)*. At oral argument, the Court signaled deep skepticism of Trump's power to fire Cook, with several conservative justices joining the liberals in tough questioning *(slide 27)*. Kavanaugh criticized "no judicial review, no process required, no remedy available, a very low bar for cause that the president alone determines" *(slide 27)*. The Fed may be the one agency that survives the death of Humphrey's -- but the reasoning matters enormously *(slide 27)*.

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

The lecture uses Trump v. Cook (argued January 21, 2026) as a live companion case: if Humphrey's falls, why should the Federal Reserve be different? *(slide 27)*. In favor of the exception: the Wilcox majority's "uniquely structured quasi-private entity" language, the Fed's tradition tracing to the First and Second Banks, the SG's own concession at Slaughter oral argument, and Kavanaugh's voiced concern about Fed independence *(slide 27)*. Against: if the unitary executive theory is true, the President controls all executive power -- a structural exception for one agency is hard to defend; consistency with Seila Law and Collins demands the same result.

The deck connects Gorsuch's nondelegation agenda to removal power: if agencies lose both independence and rulemaking authority, agencies become extensions of the White House and "Would Congress ever have delegated so much power to agencies it couldn't insulate from politics?" *(slide 28)*. The double-blow scenario captures both pressures.

## Key Takeaways

- The removal power arc runs: Myers (broad presidential power) → Humphrey's (congressional carve-out) → Morrison (functional expansion) → Free Enterprise Fund (dual-layer struck) → Seila Law (single-director struck) → Slaughter (Humphrey's itself under threat). A century-long pendulum now swinging decisively back toward the unitary executive.
- The congressional bargain principle: Congress's willingness to delegate vast regulatory authority was conditioned on agency independence. Retroactively destroying that independence raises serious structural questions about whether delegations were legitimately made.
- The shadow docket problem: using emergency applications to effectively kill a foundational precedent before full briefing and argument is a profound departure from judicial process -- an "indecent burial" compared to Chevron's public overruling in Loper Bright.
- Scalia's Morrison dissent is now essentially majority doctrine: the President must be able to control everyone who exercises executive power.
- The Federal Reserve exception, if it survives, will be a doctrinal anomaly -- the Court will need a principled reason why monetary policy is different from securities enforcement or labor law.
- Gorsuch's nondelegation project connects to removal power: agencies can't be both independent of the President AND exercises of broad legislative power delegated by Congress.
