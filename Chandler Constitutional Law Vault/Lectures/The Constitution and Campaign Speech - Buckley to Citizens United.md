# The Constitution and Campaign Speech - Buckley to Citizens United

## Professor Emphasis

### From source

The deck opens by laying out three First Amendment theories that justify protecting political speech -- the marketplace of ideas (Holmes), self-governance (Meiklejohn), and individual autonomy *(slide 3)*. The lecture's central tension is whether spending money on political communication is "speech" or "conduct" -- printing pamphlets, buying airtime, renting a hall all require money, raising the question whether limiting how much you spend limits speech itself *(slide 4)*. Buckley's contribution/expenditure distinction is the doctrinal backbone: contribution limits are a "marginal restriction" upheld under "closely drawn" scrutiny, while expenditure limits are a "substantial" restraint struck down under strict scrutiny *(slide 8)*. The deck emphasizes that Buckley recognized only one government interest sufficient to justify campaign-finance restrictions -- preventing quid pro quo corruption and its appearance -- and that this narrow definition became the foundation for Citizens United *(slide 9)*. The media-corporation paradox is given its own slide and called "Kennedy's most powerful argument": media corporations like the Times, Fox News, and CNN are among the largest corporate concentrations of power yet exempt from § 441b's ban *(slide 19)*. The deck also presses real-world consequences: $4.5B total outside spending in 2024, $2.7B Super PAC spending, $1.9B dark money *(slide 26)*, and notes 75-80% bipartisan opposition to the ruling *(slide 22)*.

## Lecture Outline

**I. Constitutional Theory — Why Protect Political Speech?**
Three theoretical foundations *(slide 3)*:
- *Marketplace of Ideas* (Holmes): The best test of truth is the power of an idea to prevail in open competition; the remedy for bad speech is more speech *(slide 3)*.
- *Self-Governance* (Meiklejohn): The First Amendment exists to ensure citizens have the information necessary for democratic self-rule; political speech receives "the fullest and most urgent" protection *(slide 3)*.
- *Individual Autonomy*: Citizens must be free to express political convictions and to use resources to amplify them *(slide 3)*.

The government asserts interests in preventing corruption (money buying political favors) and ensuring democratic equality (preventing wealthy voices from drowning out others) *(slide 4)*. The threshold question: is spending money "speech" at all? *(slide 4)* The O'Brien intermediate-scrutiny framework would apply if spending is conduct with an incidental expressive effect *(slide 4)*, but the Court in Buckley rejected that framing for direct restrictions on political expenditures.

**II. Buckley v. Valeo (1976)**
The foundational case. After Watergate, Congress enacted FECA (1971, amended 1974): contribution limits ($1,000/election), independent expenditure limits ($1,000/year), spending ceilings, FEC creation, and public financing for presidential campaigns *(slide 7)*. Senator James Buckley (R/Conservative-NY) led a broad coalition challenging the law *(slide 7)*.

The Court drew a central distinction *(slide 8)*:
- *Contributions*: upheld. Only a "marginal restriction" on expression; contribution amounts signal intensity of support but not the content of a message *(slide 8)*. The government has a "sufficiently important" interest in preventing corruption and its appearance; limits are narrowly tailored to serve that interest *(slide 8)*.
- *Expenditures*: struck down. A "substantial" restraint on political speech, "restricting the number of issues discussed, the depth of their exploration, and the size of the audience reached" *(slide 8)*. Independent spending lacks the danger of quid pro quo corruption; the anti-corruption interest is "inadequate" to justify the burden *(slide 8)*.

The only recognized government interest: preventing corruption and the appearance of corruption, focused narrowly on quid pro quo exchange of dollars for political favors *(slide 9)*. Equalizing influence and reducing access to power were not enough *(slide 9)*. Because independent expenditures are not coordinated with candidates, the Court concluded they pose less risk of quid pro quo corruption -- reasoning that became the foundation for Citizens United *(slide 9)*.

Unintended consequences: contribution limits produced relentless hard-money fundraising; the soft-money loophole (unlimited party-building contributions) became a major force; the contribution/expenditure distinction proved unstable, prompting decades of legislative whack-a-mole *(slide 10)*.

**III. Between Buckley and Citizens United (1990-2007)**
- *Austin v. Michigan Chamber of Commerce* (1990): Michigan prohibited corporations from using general treasury funds for independent expenditures supporting or opposing candidates *(slide 13)*. The Court upheld the law on an "antidistortion" rationale: government may regulate "corrosive and distorting effects of immense aggregations of wealth accumulated with the help of the corporate form" *(slide 13)*. Scalia dissented sharply, calling it a significant departure from ancient First Amendment principles *(slide 13)*. Austin was later overruled in Citizens United *(slide 13)*.
- *BCRA/McConnell* (2002-2003): Senators McCain and Feingold co-sponsored BCRA banning soft money to national parties (Title I) and restricting "electioneering communications" -- broadcast ads naming a candidate within 30 days of a primary or 60 days of a general election (Title II) *(slide 14)*. A fractured Court upheld most of BCRA in *McConnell v. FEC* (2003), adopting a broader view of corruption: not just quid pro quo but "undue influence" and the appearance of such influence *(slide 14)*.
- *FEC v. Wisconsin Right to Life* (2007): WRTL wanted to run ads urging citizens to contact senators about judicial filibusters during the blackout period; the ads did not explicitly say "vote for" or "vote against" anyone *(slide 15)*. Roberts held BCRA's ban could not reach genuine issue ads -- only the "functional equivalent of express advocacy" *(slide 15)*. This significantly narrowed McConnell, and with Alito replacing O'Connor, the Roberts Court was signaling a major shift *(slide 15)*.

**IV. Citizens United v. FEC (2010)**
Citizens United, a nonprofit corporation, produced *Hillary: The Movie* -- a 90-minute documentary critical of Senator Hillary Clinton during the 2008 Democratic primary *(slide 17)*. BCRA § 441b barred corporations from using treasury funds for electioneering communications within 30 days of a primary; Citizens United sought declaratory and injunctive relief *(slide 17)*.

The pivotal moment: during the first oral argument (March 2009), Deputy Solicitor General Malcolm Stewart conceded the government's theory would permit banning a book published by a corporation containing political advocacy *(slide 17)*. When the case was reargued in September 2009, Solicitor General Elena Kagan was asked the same question, and her answer did not satisfy Justice Kennedy *(slide 17)*.

The majority (Kennedy, 5-4): § 441b's ban on corporate independent expenditures is unconstitutional; Austin is overruled, and the relevant part of McConnell is overruled *(slide 18)*.

Core reasoning *(slide 18)*: (1) Political speech does not lose protection because its source is a corporation. (2) The government cannot restrict speech based on the speaker's identity. (3) Austin's antidistortion rationale is "not a permissible" basis for restricting speech. (4) Independent expenditures do not give rise to corruption or the appearance of corruption. Disclosure requirements upheld 8-1 -- voters have a legitimate informational interest in knowing who funds political speech *(slide 18)*.

The media corporation paradox: media corporations like the New York Times, Fox News, and CNN -- already among the largest concentrations of corporate power in America -- were already exempt from § 441b *(slide 19)*. If the government can ban political speech by some corporations but not media corporations, who draws the line? *(slide 19)* The deck flags this as the argument that resonates with those who observe critics of Citizens United are often comfortable letting corporate-owned mainstream media control political narratives while objecting when other corporations participate in the same discourse *(slide 19)*.

Stevens's dissent (joined by Ginsburg, Breyer, Sotomayor): the Framers deeply distrusted corporations and the First Amendment was not understood to protect corporate political speech; corporations are not members of society in the same way as natural persons; Austin's antidistortion rationale recognized a legitimate interest in preventing corporate treasury funds from overwhelming democratic debate; stare decisis should have preserved Austin and McConnell *(slide 20)*.

Scalia concurred: "The Amendment is written in terms of 'speech,' not speakers" *(slide 21)*.

**V. The Aftermath — Super PACs and Outside Spending**
*SpeechNow.org v. FEC* (D.C. Cir. 2010): If independent expenditures cannot corrupt (per Citizens United), then contributions to groups making only independent expenditures also cannot corrupt, allowing unlimited individual contributions to independent-expenditure-only committees (Super PACs) *(slide 25)*. The FEC issued advisory opinions enabling the Super PAC structure *(slide 25)*.

2024 cycle figures: $4.5 billion total outside spending in federal elections; $2.7 billion Super PAC independent expenditures; $1.9 billion dark money from sources not disclosing donors *(slide 26)*. The coordination wall between Super PACs and candidates is widely viewed as paper-thin -- candidates routinely appear at Super PAC fundraisers, share consultants, and signal their messaging needs publicly *(slide 25)*.

Reform efforts: at least 22 states and hundreds of cities have called for a constitutional amendment to overturn Citizens United, with 75%+ bipartisan support, but Article V's two-thirds-of-Congress / three-fourths-of-states bar is enormously high *(slide 27)*. The DISCLOSE Act has repeatedly failed in Congress despite Citizens United's 8-1 upholding of disclosure requirements *(slide 27)*.

## Cases Discussed

### [[Buckley v. Valeo]] (1976)
Foundational campaign finance case. Contribution limits upheld (marginal restriction on expression; anti-corruption interest sufficient). Expenditure limits struck down (substantial restraint on political speech; independent spending lacks quid pro quo corruption risk). Only recognized interest: preventing quid pro quo corruption and its appearance.

### [[Austin v. Michigan Chamber of Commerce]] (1990)
Corporate independent expenditure restrictions upheld under "antidistortion" rationale. Later overruled in Citizens United. Introduced speaker-based restriction into campaign finance doctrine.

### [[McConnell v. FEC]] (2003)
Upheld most of BCRA, including electioneering communications restrictions, using broader view of corruption as "undue influence." Significantly narrowed by WRTL (2007); partially overruled by Citizens United (2010).

### [[FEC v. Wisconsin Right to Life]] (2007)
BCRA's electioneering communication ban could not reach genuine issue ads—only the "functional equivalent of express advocacy." Signaled the Roberts Court's direction with the imminent Citizens United.

### [[Citizens United v. FEC]] (2010)
Corporate independent expenditure ban struck down. Austin overruled. Political speech does not lose protection based on the corporate identity of the speaker. Independent expenditures create no quid pro quo corruption risk. Disclosure requirements upheld 8-1.

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

**Can Democracy Function With Unlimited Money?** The lecture poses the central question: can democracy function when unlimited money flows into elections, or does restricting that money pose a greater threat to self-governance? *(slide 28)*

**The Media Corporation Paradox.** If corporate-owned media are free to influence elections without limit, is it coherent to restrict other corporations from doing the same? *(slide 28)* They already dominate political discourse. If the concern is truly about corporate influence on politics, why protect the most powerful corporate voices while restricting Citizens United? Is there a principled distinction, or does the case reveal an unstated trust-editorial-media assumption embedded in First Amendment doctrine?

**Corruption Defined Too Narrowly?** Buckley limits the recognized government interest to quid pro quo corruption -- the explicit exchange of dollars for favors *(slide 9)*. This forecloses arguments about systemic distortion: the concern that massive spending by the wealthy skews democratic outcomes regardless of explicit deal-making *(slide 11)*. Should "corruption" be defined beyond quid pro quo to include systemic distortion of democratic processes? *(slide 28)* What would it mean for campaign finance doctrine if the Court recognized an appearance-of-undue-access interest, as McConnell briefly accepted? *(slide 14)*

**Is Money Speech?** Critics argue the Court conflated the medium of expression with expression itself; if money is speech, then the wealthy have more speech than the poor, turning the First Amendment into a guarantor of plutocratic influence *(slide 11)*. Defenders argue that virtually all political expression requires money, so restricting how much you can spend to communicate restricts how much you can communicate *(slide 4)*.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Spending money on political communication is constitutionally protected speech.** Buckley established this premise; Citizens United extended it to corporations. The government's power to restrict political expenditures is severely limited by strict scrutiny.

2. **The contribution/expenditure distinction remains foundational but unstable.** Contribution limits survive; expenditure limits generally do not. But the distinction has created a system where money flows through Super PACs and dark money networks, replicating the practical effect of unlimited direct donations while formally complying with Buckley's framework.

3. **The only recognized government interest is preventing quid pro quo corruption.** Equalizing influence and preventing distortion of democratic outcomes are not sufficient interests to justify restricting political speech. This narrow definition of corruption is the engine of the post-Buckley cases.

4. **The speaker-identity restriction was rejected in Citizens United.** Corporations have the same First Amendment rights as natural persons for purposes of political speech. The antidistortion rationale (Austin) was insufficient. The media corporation paradox was central to the majority's reasoning.

5. **Disclosure survives.** Even as substantive campaign finance limits have been struck down, disclosure requirements have been upheld 8-1. The informational interest in helping voters evaluate political messages is sufficient. But congressional failure to pass the DISCLOSE Act has left dark money a major unregulated channel.

6. **The Super PAC system represents doctrine's gap from reality.** Formally, Super PACs must be independent of candidates. In practice, the coordination wall is widely viewed as thin. The legal fiction of independence may be the most significant gap between First Amendment doctrine and the actual political system it governs.
