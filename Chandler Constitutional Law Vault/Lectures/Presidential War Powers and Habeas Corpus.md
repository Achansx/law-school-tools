## Lecture Outline

**I. Habeas Corpus — The Foundation**
Article I, Section 9 provides: "The privilege of the writ of habeas corpus shall not be suspended, unless when in cases of rebellion or invasion the public safety may require it" *(slide 2)*. The deck flags "the perils of passive voice" -- the provision does not specify who may suspend, even though placement in Article I suggests Congress *(slide 2)*. Lincoln would exploit this ambiguity.

Habeas corpus prevents executive detention without judicial process; without it, government can imprison without trial, and rights of free speech, voting, religion, and most privacy rights are demolished once a person is in prison *(slide 2)*.

**II. The Civil War Context**
November 1860: Lincoln elected. December 1860: South Carolina secedes. By February 1861, Mississippi, Florida, Alabama, Georgia, Louisiana, and Texas join secession and form the Confederate States of America *(slide 3)*. March 1861: Lincoln takes office *(slide 3)*. The Northern military situation was dire: roughly 16,000 troops total, fewer than 1,000 east of the Mississippi, with Washington, D.C. surrounded by slave states -- Maryland on three sides, Virginia to the south *(slide 4)*.

April 1861: Fort Sumter falls; Lincoln calls for 75,000 troops; Virginia, Tennessee, Arkansas, and North Carolina join the Confederacy; Richmond is named Southern capital, 100 miles from D.C., with major battles within 25-40 miles of the capital by July *(slide 5)*.

Maryland posed an immediate crisis. Rioters in Baltimore effectively closed rail links to D.C., causing inconvenient diversions through Annapolis *(slide 6)*. The legislature voted 55-13 against secession but did nothing to reopen the railroads; allegedly the Governor ordered Merryman and others to blow up key railroad bridges *(slide 6)*. Lincoln was extremely unpopular in Maryland -- he received only 2.5% of the vote and zero votes in seven counties *(slide 6)*.

**III. The Merryman Crisis**
In May 1861, Lincoln's general arrested Merryman and confined him at Fort McHenry (the national anthem place) without charge of treason or any other crime *(slide 7)*. Over the next few months, Lincoln ordered the arrest of many more, including a third of the Maryland legislature and newspaper editors *(slide 7)*. The deck frames the fundamental problem as: "What system of justice are we going to use to deal with areas we imperfectly control during a civil war?" *(slide 7)*.

Chief Justice Taney, riding circuit, issued the writ. The Judiciary Act of 1789 Section 14 was the textual hook, though the deck notes it does not authorize federal courts to issue habeas to state prisoners *(slide 8)*. Taney's arguments against unilateral presidential suspension *(slide 9)*: danger to liberty; placement of the Suspension Clause in Article I; a fortiori from other restrictions on the President; that the President's job is to help the judiciary; and that even the King could not have done this.

Lincoln's response to Congress invoked the "survival theme": "The whole of the laws which were required to be faithfully executed were being resisted and failing of execution in nearly one-third of the States. . . . Are all the laws but one to go unexecuted, and the Government itself go to pieces lest that one be violated?" *(slide 10)*.

**IV. Murphy v. Porter — The Courts Fight Back, Uselessly**
James Murphy, a Union enlistee, allegedly wanted out; his father John filed for habeas in D.C. in October 1861, but Lincoln had suspended the writ in D.C. in July 1861 *(slide 11)*. Military commander General Porter arrested Murphy's lawyer and Judge Merrick; remaining D.C. judges directed the Marshals to compel Porter to appear, and Lincoln personally directed the Marshal to ignore the order *(slide 11)*.

The remaining judges ruled the Executive was acting unlawfully but acknowledged there was nothing they could do: "I do distinctly assert the following principles: (1) That the law in this country knows no superior. (2) That the supremacy of the civil authority over the military cannot be denied . . . (3) That this court ought to be respected by every one as the guardian of the personal liberty of the citizen . . . (4) I therefore respectfully protest against the right claimed to interrupt the proceedings in this case" *(slide 12)*.

The episode illustrates the gap between legal authority and practical enforcement: courts may say the executive is wrong, but when the Marshal follows the President rather than the court, the constitutional order depends on executive self-restraint.

**V. Declaring War — The Constitutional Structure and Prize Cases**
Article I, Section 8 gives Congress the power to declare war; Article II, Section 2 makes the President Commander-in-Chief. The Calling Forth Act of 1792 and the Militia Act of 1795 gave the President statutory authority to call forth the militia of the states most convenient to the place of danger when "the United States shall be invaded, or be in imminent danger of invasion" *(slide 14)*.

*The Prize Cases* (1863): Congress did not "declare" the Civil War; Lincoln ordered a blockade of many American ports; ships allegedly violating the blockade were towed to Union-controlled ports and their goods seized as prizes *(slide 15)*. After 12 days of argument, the Court upheld the blockade *(slide 15)*. The deck notes that the modern President's ability to engage the military "stem[s] significantly from The Prize Cases" *(slide 15)*.

Justice Grier's majority developed three propositions: (1) Congress alone has the power to declare war; the President "has no power to initiate or declare a war either against a foreign nation or a domestic State" *(slides 17, 21)*. (2) But by Acts of Congress (1795, 1807), the President is authorized to call out militia and use the military and naval forces "in case of invasion by foreign nations and to suppress insurrection" *(slides 17, 21)*. (3) "If a war be made by invasion of a foreign nation, the President is not only authorized but bound to resist force by force. He does not initiate the war, but is bound to accept the challenge without waiting for any special legislative authority" *(slides 18, 22)*.

The Court also treated the existence of war as essentially a political question committed to the President: "The proclamation of blockade is itself official and conclusive evidence to the Court that a state of war existed which demanded and authorized a recourse to such a measure" *(slide 20)*. Congress can ratify presidential actions afterwards, curing any deficiency *(slide 20)*. The dissent's framing -- that the law of nations contained no anomalous doctrine treating rebellion as not a "war" -- is dismissed *(slide 19)*.

**VI. The War Powers Resolution (1973)**
Enacted in the aftermath of Vietnam over Nixon's veto *(slide 23)*. Stated purpose: "to fulfill the intent of the framers of the Constitution of the United States and insure that the collective judgment of both the Congress and the President will apply to the introduction of United States Armed Forces into hostilities" *(slide 23)*. The Resolution rests on the Necessary and Proper Clause to carry into execution Congress's own powers and other powers vested in the government *(slide 25)*.

Key provisions: The President's Commander-in-Chief powers are exercised only pursuant to (1) a declaration of war, (2) specific statutory authorization, or (3) a national emergency created by attack on the United States, its territories or possessions, or its armed forces *(slide 26)*. The President "in every possible instance" shall consult with Congress before introducing forces and shall consult regularly afterwards *(slide 27)*. The President must advise Congress within 48 hours and must stop within 60 days unless Congress has declared war, authorized the force, extended the period, or is physically unable to meet *(slide 28)*. The President must also stop if Congress, by concurrent resolution, so directs *(slide 28)*.

**VII. The Record of Compliance**
The deck presents a color-coded survey of major military actions since the Civil War: declaration of war (red), AUMF or similar (yellow), UN support (blue), arguable WPR support (green), no support found (purple), contested (brown) *(slide 24)*. The list includes Spanish American War, WWI, WWII, Korea, Vietnam, Lebanon, Grenada, Panama, Gulf War, Bosnia, Kosovo, Afghanistan, Iraq, Libya, Syria *(slide 24)*. The aftermath of the WPR *(slide 29)*: used in Lebanon, first Gulf War, Afghanistan, Iraq; "possibly ignored" in Sudan 1998, Libya 2011, Syria Tomahawks 2017, attacks in Iran/Iraq in 2020 (Soleimani). No President has accepted WPR constitutionality, but many have acted as if they would voluntarily comply *(slide 29)*. Lower courts have found the issue to be a "political question" -- no Supreme Court case *(slide 29)*. Congress voted to deauthorize military activity in Yemen; Trump vetoed *(slide 30)*.

## Cases Discussed

### [[Ex parte Merryman]] (1861)
Chief Justice Taney (riding circuit) held that the President lacks constitutional power to suspend habeas corpus unilaterally; that power belongs to Congress under Article I. Lincoln ignored the order. The episode established that judicial orders are not self-enforcing against a determined executive.

### [[Cases/The Prize Cases (1863)]] (1863)
Upheld Lincoln's blockade of Confederate ports despite the absence of a formal congressional declaration of war. Established that the President may respond to a state of war without waiting for a congressional declaration; the existence of war is a political question committed to the political branches; Congress may ratify prior presidential action.

### [[Cases/Youngstown Sheet and Tube Co v Sawyer (1952)|Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer]] (1952)
Truman's seizure of steel mills during the Korean War struck down. Jackson's three-category framework: (1) President acts with congressional authorization—power at maximum; (2) Congress has not spoken—"zone of twilight"; (3) President acts against congressional will—power at "lowest ebb." Critical reference point for war powers and emergency powers doctrine across subsequent cases.

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

**Who Can Suspend Habeas?** Article I's passive voice creates genuine ambiguity. The placement in Article I suggests Congress, and Taney argued forcefully for this reading in Merryman. But Lincoln effectively suspended the writ without congressional authorization, and Congress subsequently ratified his actions. The question has never been definitively resolved by the Supreme Court. How would you resolve it textually? Does the Article I placement control?

**Lincoln's "All the Laws But One" Argument:** Lincoln asks whether one law (habeas corpus) should be allowed to prevent execution of all the other laws. Is this a legitimate constitutional argument, or is it the executive asserting the power to decide which laws can be violated in emergencies? If accepted, is there any principle that limits this argument?

**The Murphy v. Porter Gap:** The D.C. judges correctly identified that the executive was acting unlawfully. They also correctly acknowledged they could not compel compliance. What constitutional mechanisms exist to enforce judicial orders against an executive that refuses to comply? Impeachment? Budget control? Congressional statute? None of these proved effective for Lincoln's opponents.

**The Invade Greenland Problem:** The deck poses an "Invade Greenland" hypothetical synthesizing the doctrine *(slide 32)*: a clear WPR violation (no congressional authorization, no imminent threat under sections 1541(c), 1542, 1543, 1544(b)); a Commander-in-Chief defense citing Prize Cases and past executive practice; congressional countermeasures (funding cut, prohibition, suit, impeachment) constrained by vetoes and standing problems; a political question argument citing Goldwater v. Carter; and a NATO Article 5 dimension if Denmark invokes collective defense *(slide 32)*. The deck uses the hypo to illustrate that practical limits on executive military action are political and diplomatic, not judicial.

## Key Takeaways

1. **The habeas corpus suspension question remains constitutionally unsettled.** Taney said only Congress can suspend; Lincoln did it anyway; Congress later ratified his actions. The Supreme Court has never definitively resolved whether the placement of the Suspension Clause in Article I forecloses unilateral presidential suspension.

2. **The President may respond militarily to an attack without a congressional declaration.** Prize Cases established this for defensive responses. The President "is not only authorized but bound to resist force by force" without waiting for legislative authority. The existence of a state of war is a political question.

3. **Judicial orders are not self-enforcing against a determined executive.** Murphy v. Porter demonstrates that the constitutional order depends on executive compliance with judicial authority, not mechanical enforcement. If the Marshal follows the President rather than the court, legal authority and practical power diverge.

4. **The War Powers Resolution is the most circumvented statute in modern constitutional history.** No President has accepted it as constitutional; many have ignored its 60-day clock; the courts have treated challenges as non-justiciable political questions. Its formal requirements exist on paper while practice has diverged dramatically.

5. **Lincoln's "survival theme" recurs across emergency powers cases.** The argument that constitutional constraints must yield to national survival has been invoked from Lincoln through Bush-era detention cases. The counter-argument is that a government that destroys liberty to save itself has saved nothing worth saving.

6. **Jackson's Youngstown framework provides analytical structure but not resolution.** The three categories organize executive power claims but do not answer the threshold question—whether statutory authorization exists (Category 1) or Congress has prohibited the action (Category 3). That determination requires independent statutory analysis.
