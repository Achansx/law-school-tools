## Professor Emphasis

### From source

The orientation deck functions as a syllabus walkthrough and an expectations-setting document rather than a substantive lecture (slide 1). Chandler signals up front that the syllabus has many tabs, is subject to minor changes "in an eventless world," and to significant changes if events warrant (slide 2). He also flags Constitutional Law as a uniquely demanding course because it carries a large case-volume (at least 132 cases, plus 50-60 "Murder of *Gonzagos*" additions), because the cases are law-creating rather than pedagogic, and because political polarization is a perennial classroom condition (slides 14-16). He says he cares because students are becoming professional leaders; he warns that he may be superficially pleasant but is very demanding, and he frames Constitutional Law as a language he wants students to be fluent in (slide 15).

### Editor's framing

This page treats the deck as the course's operating manual: how class time is structured, what preparation is expected, who staffs the support roles, and what the professor signals about classroom norms. It is digest-style rather than doctrinal — the bulk of the substance is logistical, and the few thematic moves Chandler makes (immersion, language fluency, civility) are flagged where they appear.

## Course Structure and Class Mechanics

### Panels and the calling system

Class is structured around three rotating panels (slide 5). Students are divided into Groups A, B, and C at the start of the semester based on seating location; Group A is on panel Monday, Group B on Tuesday, and Group C on Thursday (slide 5). Being on panel is not a guarantee of being called on, but panelists are expected to be thoroughly prepared, to have the material "in their heads," and not to be looking at screens during discussion (slide 5). Unprepared or absent panelists receive a demerit; on Day 1 everyone is on panel, but Chandler says he will be merciful (slide 5).

### Segment organization

The course is organized into 82 segments of two kinds (slide 18). Non-problem segments open with a four-minute ungraded quiz (two minutes to take, two minutes to discuss), and lag the assigned material by one day; they then proceed as either a PowerPointed lecture-discussion (20-minute lecture, 12-minute open discussion) or a Socratic discussion where Chandler pushes students to answer and invites cross-discussion (slide 18). Problem segments also open with the four-minute ungraded quiz, then run two proposed student solutions (12 minutes each), followed by Chandler's eight-minute summary and lecture (slide 18). Students are expected to be on Zoom at least one minute before class starts; Chandler typically opens Zoom ten minutes early, and the early window can be used as extra office hours (slide 18).

## Preparation Expectations

### Required preparation

The required-preparation slide asks students to read everything assigned, to query a quality AI with the day's assignment and ask what questions to be prepared to answer well, to think about *why* the material was assigned, and to think about the relationship between assigned material and the rest of the course (slide 6). Two cross-cutting lenses are flagged for the "relationship" inquiry: consistency and ideology (slide 6). Students are also expected to think about issues left open by the decisions and about alternative perspectives under which the trajectory of the law might have been different (slide 6).

### Optional preparation ideas

Chandler offers a menu of optional preparation moves (slides 7-8). These include: writing a paragraph or two about the case and running it through an LLM for critique; using NotebookLM to generate a big-picture podcast (with the caveat that NotebookLM covers basics but not the nitty-gritty); developing an argument that the majority opinion was wrong, or that a concurrence or dissent was wrong; coming up with hypotheticals where the holding would be problematic or unresolved; and adopting some ideological framework — law and economics, CRT, textualism — against which to assess the cases (slide 7). The more lateral suggestions: develop a mnemonic for the facts and holding, use Udio or Suno to make a song about the material, create artwork that captures the central idea, or draw a flowchart (slide 8).

## Course Staffing

### Tutors

Three tutors are listed: Brad Edrington, Brady Mills, and Caroline Puryear (slide 12). Their job is to work with students on problems and on mastery and understanding; their job is *not* to do the student's work, and Chandler asks students to respect their boundaries (slide 12). ## Course Themes and Posture

### Why Con Law is hard

Chandler frames Constitutional Law as challenging for two distinct reasons (slides 14-15). First, the sheer volume: at least 132 cases, plus another 50-60 in the "Murder of *Gonzagos*" supplement (slide 14). Second, the cases are *law-creating* rather than pedagogic — case names matter because they are how lawyers communicate, and because of politics, history, plural authorship, and human frailty the cases do not always line up (slide 15). To address the volume problem he urges immersion, drawing the analogy to how deeply students know things they care about (a sport, a fictional world, Hollywood, a podcast club); two specific immersion tools are flagged — the casebook's embedded videos in Casebook Connect, and the *100 Big Cases* book by Barnett & Blackman (slide 14). He references an essay he wrote comparing NFL football to constitutional law as another immersion vehicle (slide 14).

### Civility in a polarized class

Chandler acknowledges that students care a lot about constitutional law in a way they do not care about party-joinder rules, and that the class will engage hot issues — abortion, affirmative action, presidential powers (slide 16). His norm: "constructively harness the passion"; students do not have to be dispassionate, but they do have to at least persuasively feign respect (slide 16).

### Changes for Spring 2025

The deck lists several changes — the slide title is "Changes for Spring 2025," which appears to be a leftover from a prior semester's version of this orientation (slide 17). The changes listed: more formative assessments, all closed-book; restoration of citizenship discussion and Indian-law cases; more opportunities for class discussion; greater use of the problem method; and new important cases across freedom of speech, freedom of religion, equal protection, separation of powers, and federal jurisdiction (slide 17).

## Key Takeaways

- The course runs on a three-panel calling system (A/B/C) with demerits for unpreparedness, and on a segmented class structure of 82 segments built around four-minute ungraded quizzes, lecture-discussion blocks, Socratic blocks, and problem-method blocks (slides 5, 18).
- Preparation is expected to go beyond reading: Chandler explicitly invites AI-assisted preparation and offers a menu of lateral techniques (LLM critique, NotebookLM podcasts, mnemonics, songs, artwork, flowcharts) (slides 6-8).
- The course has three tutors and three student-staffed administrative roles; Chandler treats this staffing as substantive enough to put on its own slide (slides 12-13).
- Two thematic claims dominate the deck: Constitutional Law is hard because it is law-creating and immersive (slides 14-15), and the classroom requires civility in spite of, not because of, political stakes (slide 16).
- The "Changes for Spring 2025" slide appears to be a leftover from the prior version of the orientation and may not reflect the actual Fall 2025 syllabus deltas (slide 17).
