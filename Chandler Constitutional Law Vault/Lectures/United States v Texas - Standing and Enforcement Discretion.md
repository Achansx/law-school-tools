# United States v Texas - Standing and Enforcement Discretion

## Professor Emphasis

### From source

The deck frames the case around a single overarching question: "Can Anyone Make the President Enforce the Law?" (slide 1). Two mandatory-language statutes set up the conflict -- Section 1226(c)'s "shall take into custody" command and Section 1231(a)(2)'s "shall detain" command, both using "shall" the Court has consistently interpreted as mandatory (slide 2). The Mayorkas memo's three enforcement priorities -- national security threats, public safety threats, recent border crossers -- leave noncitizens triggering the "shall detain" language to case-by-case judgment rather than categorical enforcement (slide 3). The deck stresses that the Court "never resolves" the underlying statute-versus-memo conflict and disposes of the case on standing grounds alone (slide 4). Kavanaugh's no-standing holding rests on Linda R.S. v. Richard D. (1973): real financial costs are insufficient because the injury must be "legally and judicially cognizable" and the dispute "traditionally thought to be capable of resolution through the judicial process" (slide 6). The deck flags the four caveats -- selective prosecution, congressional authorization, total abdication under Heckler v. Chaney, and benefits-versus-enforcement -- as doors left open but each "hedged with 'might' or 'arguably'" (slide 7). Gorsuch's redressability concurrence relies on Section 1252(f)(1)'s bar on enjoining the detention statutes and on the point that vacating guidelines that "merely advise" agents would not compel different behavior (slide 8). Alito's lone dissent invokes the pre-1688 English dispensing power and the consequence that "shall" becomes precatory if no one can sue (slide 9). The Laken Riley Act, signed January 29, 2025, is the live sequel: it mandates detention for noncitizens arrested for theft, burglary, larceny, or shoplifting and authorizes state AGs to sue with a $100 financial-harm threshold -- a direct response to Kavanaugh's second caveat (slide 10). The deck closes by framing standing as political architecture: Kavanaugh never says whether the President is breaking the law, only that nobody can make him answer that question in court (slide 12).

## Lecture Outline

**The Statutory Framework -- "Shall Means Shall"?**

Two statutes use mandatory language. 8 U.S.C. Section 1226(c) directs the Attorney General to "take into custody any alien who ... is deportable" for having committed certain offenses "when the alien is released" (slide 2). 8 U.S.C. Section 1231(a)(2) directs that during the removal period the Attorney General "shall detain" the alien during the 90-day period following a final order of removal (slide 2). Both statutes use "shall" -- language the Court has consistently read as mandatory (slide 2). The key question: if Congress commanded the Executive to arrest and detain, can the Executive decline, and if not, can anyone force compliance (slide 2)?

**The Mayorkas Memo**

Secretary Mayorkas issued enforcement-priority guidelines identifying three priorities: national security threats, public safety threats, and recent border crossers (slide 3). Non-priority individuals -- including those triggering the "shall detain" statutes -- might be deprioritized for arrest and removal under case-by-case judgment (slide 3). Texas argued the memo violated the mandatory detention statutes; the Court never resolves the conflict and disposes of the case on standing grounds alone (slide 4).

**District Court and SCOTUS Grant**

Judge Tipton (S.D. Tex.) found standing based on increased incarceration, healthcare, and education costs, and vacated the guidelines on the merits (slide 5). The Supreme Court bypassed the Fifth Circuit on cert before judgment, and declined 5-4 to stay the district court order (slide 5). The forum-shopping pattern -- states filing in friendly venues to obtain nationwide injunctions -- is acknowledged as a defining feature of immigration litigation and placed in the context of subsequent developments including Trump v. CASA (2025) on nationwide injunctions (slide 5).

**Kavanaugh's Majority -- No Standing (8-1)**

The majority relied on Linda R.S. v. Richard D. (1973): "A citizen lacks standing to contest the policies of the prosecuting authority when he himself is neither prosecuted nor threatened with prosecution" (slide 6). The states' financial costs are real but not "legally and judicially cognizable"; the dispute must be "traditionally thought to be capable of resolution through the judicial process" (slide 6). Courts ordering more arrests would intrude on a core Article II function that also implicates foreign policy (slide 6). There is no "precedent, history, or tradition of courts ordering the Executive Branch to change its arrest or prosecution policies so that the Executive Branch makes more arrests" (slide 6).

**The Four Caveats**

Kavanaugh left four doors open, each hedged with "might" or "arguably" (slide 7): (1) selective prosecution -- equal-protection challenges to being singled out for enforcement remain available (slide 7); (2) congressional authorization -- the calculus "might change" if Congress specifically authorized particular plaintiffs to sue, with the Court declining to decide whether such a statute would satisfy Article III (slide 7); (3) total abdication -- if the Executive wholly abandoned its statutory responsibilities, standing "might" exist under Heckler v. Chaney's abdication exception, but the majority does not say where that line falls (slide 7); and (4) benefits versus enforcement -- challenges to affirmative grants of immigration status, like DACA, are distinguishable (slide 7).

**Gorsuch Concurrence -- Redressability, Not Injury**

Gorsuch (joined by Thomas and Barrett) concurred in the judgment but diagnosed the jurisdictional defect differently: the problem is redressability (slide 8). 8 U.S.C. Section 1252(f)(1) bars federal courts from enjoining or restraining the operation of the detention statutes, and applies regardless of congressional standing grants (slide 8). The guidelines merely advise agents; vacating them would not compel different behavior because agents could simply continue the same practices without a written memo (slide 8). Gorsuch used the occasion to mount a broader attack on nationwide vacatur under the APA, arguing it creates the same problems as universal injunctions (slide 8). This concurrence may constrain future remedies even if Congress grants standing (slide 8).

**Alito Dissent -- The Dispensing Power**

Alito was the lone dissenter willing to reach the merits (slide 9). He argued the majority's conception of presidential authority "smacks of the powers that English monarchs claimed prior to the 'Glorious Revolution' of 1688, namely, the power to suspend the operation of existing statutes" (slide 9). If "shall" becomes precatory because no one can sue to enforce it, the Take Care Clause becomes dead letter (slide 9). Congress can command all it wants; if the President can disobey and courts cannot review, the statutory mandate is toothless (slide 9).

**The Laken Riley Act -- Congress Responds**

Signed January 29, 2025, the Laken Riley Act mandates detention of noncitizens arrested for theft, burglary, larceny, or shoplifting (slide 10). Crucially, it authorizes state attorneys general to sue the federal government for injunctive relief, with a threshold of financial harm exceeding $100 (slide 10). This directly addresses Kavanaugh's second caveat -- the absence of specific authorization for particular plaintiffs to sue (slide 10). The $100 threshold makes virtually any state a potential plaintiff for virtually any enforcement decision (slide 10). The deck poses the unresolved constitutional question as three scenarios (slide 11): did Kavanaugh's "judicially cognizable" holding rest on a default rule Congress can override by statute (Scenario A), or was it a constitutional Article III floor Congress cannot legislate around (Scenario B), or is it a hollow victory where standing is granted but Section 1252(f)(1) still bars relief (Scenario C)?

**The Deeper Architecture**

Standing doctrine lets the Court sidestep politically explosive questions (slide 12). Kavanaugh never says whether the President is breaking the law; he only says nobody can make him answer that question in court (slide 12). The framework is bipartisan: any President can deprioritize enforcement of any statute -- environmental, labor, tax, civil rights -- and states injured by non-enforcement will face the same courthouse door (slide 12). As the lecture concludes: "The real test comes when the next President decides not to enforce a law that the opposing party cares about" (slide 12).

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

The professor poses the structural symmetry question: could blue states sue a future administration for overenforcement of immigration law, arguing lost sales-tax revenue from deported residents is a concrete injury? How is that different from Massachusetts v. EPA (state land lost to climate inaction)? The answer likely turns on whether there is a "tradition of judicial cognizance" of suits seeking fewer arrests -- and the Kavanaugh majority suggests there is not.

The Laken Riley Act invites the follow-up: is $100 in financial harm enough to satisfy Article III's "substantial injury" standard, or has Congress bootstrapped state standing from a de minimis harm? And if Gorsuch is right about redressability -- that courts cannot enjoin the detention statutes and vacatur of guidelines has no behavioral effect -- does standing even matter?

## Key Takeaways

- United States v. Texas (2023) held 8-1 that states lack standing to challenge executive enforcement-priority guidelines for non-enforcement of immigration statutes.
- The test is whether the injury is "legally and judicially cognizable" -- rooted in a tradition of courts resolving this type of dispute -- not merely real or financial.
- The framework is party-neutral: courts cannot compel more arrests by any administration under any statute.
- Gorsuch's redressability concurrence is a potential alternative ground that may block relief even if Congress provides standing.
- The Laken Riley Act is a direct legislative response that may reach the Court to resolve whether Congress can override Article III standing limitations by statute.
