## Lecture Outline

**Origins: Luther v. Borden (1849)**

Rhode Island had no state constitution -- just a colonial charter from Charles II -- and only whites with $134 in property could vote, disenfranchising 60% of white males in a state of 108,000 with only 8,000 voters *(slide 2)*. In the Dorr Rebellion, two elections produced two governments (Charterites and Dorrites), the traditional legislature asked the feds for help and was declined, Dorr marched a cannon to the Providence arsenal where it misfired, and Charterite forces broke into Luther's house -- giving rise to a trespass case where the result turned on which government was legitimate *(slide 3)*.

Chief Justice Taney warned that holding the charter government illegitimate would mean its laws were nullities, its taxes wrongfully collected, its salaries illegally paid, its public accounts improperly settled, and its court judgments null and void *(slide 4)*. He held: "Under [the Republican form of government clause] it rests with Congress to decide what government is the established one in a State" -- and "its decision is binding on every other department of the government, and could not be questioned in a judicial tribunal" *(slide 6)*. The charter was promptly fixed to be more democratic afterward, and Dorr was tried for treason against the state and sentenced to life in prison (later commuted) *(slide 7)*.

**Apportionment and the Run-up to Baker**

Congress began regulating districting in 1840 by requiring single-member districts, flipped back and forth almost each decade, and in 1911 enacted a purportedly permanent requirement that districts be "contiguous and compact territory, and containing as nearly as practicable an equal number of inhabitants" *(slide 9)*. In 1929, however, Congress passed a new bill that appeared to repeal the 1911 Act, and in Wood v. Broom (1932) the Supreme Court refused to overturn a Mississippi system lacking contiguity, compactness, and equality *(slide 9)*. Mississippi took advantage of the removal of the equality constraint by redistricting so that districts ranged from 184,000 to 414,000 in population *(slide 15)*. Colegrove v. Green (1946) (Frankfurter, J., plurality) held that Illinois's unequal congressional districts presented a political question: "The short of it is that the Constitution has conferred upon Congress exclusive authority to secure fair representation by the States in the popular House" *(slide 10)*. "Courts ought not to enter this political thicket" *(slide 10)*. The deck flags the structural problem: only 26 states (or fewer with filibuster) need to block reform, so the remedy points to the very body that benefits from the malady *(slide 11)*.

**Baker v. Carr (1962)**

Tennessee, like Illinois, had not redistricted in 60 years with similar consequences -- entrenchment *(slide 17)*. Brennan performed a "Cardozian refitting" of the cases, finding five meta-variables that lined almost everything up except Colegrove, which he managed to distinguish as a Guarantee Clause case *(slide 17)*. Tennessee had not redistricted its General Assembly since 1901, leading to a massive violation of the equality constraint; under Colegrove this might have looked like a political question, but Brennan refactored political question doctrine, finding the case justiciable because numerosity is an easy standard to apply *(slide 18)*. Despite announcing a "capacious and discretionary test that gives the court broad latitude in the future," Brennan finds none of these factors present here *(slide 19)*. The six factors are: textually demonstrable constitutional commitment; lack of judicially discoverable and manageable standards; impossibility of deciding without an initial policy determination for nonjudicial discretion; impossibility of independent resolution without expressing lack of respect for coordinate branches; unusual need for unquestioning adherence to a political decision already made; potentiality of embarrassment from multifarious pronouncements *(slide 20)*. Unless one of these is "inextricable from the case at bar, there should be no dismissal for nonjusticiability on the ground of a political question's presence" *(slide 20)*.

Frankfurter's dissent: "The crux of the matter is that courts are not fit instruments of decision where what is at stake is the composition of those large contexts of policy traditionally fought out in nonjudicial forums, by which governments and the actions of governments are made and unmade" *(slide 21)*. Guaranty Clause claims are but an instance of this principle -- you can't escape by rebadging *(slide 21)*.

**Wesberry v. Sanders (1964)**

Black's 8-1 majority held that Article I, § 2's command -- Representatives be chosen "by the People of the several States" -- means "as nearly as is practicable, one man's vote in a congressional election is to be worth as much as another's" *(slide 25)*. Black added that the Framers did not intend to permit vote-diluting discrimination through "the device of districts containing widely varied numbers of inhabitants" *(slide 25)*. Harlan dissented, arguing the majority was teasing meaning out of sparse text and ignoring shape, area, and party affiliation in favor of abstractions *(slide 26)*. Harlan added a structural critique: by reading Article I, § 2 to require equality, the Court was not just exercising power but "overruling congressional judgment" reflected in proposed apportionment legislation Congress had declined to enact *(slide 26)*.

**Nixon v. United States (1993)**

A crooked federal judge was unhappy that a committee dealt with his impeachment rather than the whole Senate *(slide 29)*. Rehnquist held this a political question because the framers deliberately gave impeachment power to the most powerful branch; the judiciary should not judge impeachments of its own members; "Try" is not given with sufficient precision; and "What relief could we really order?" *(slide 29)*. Justice White: justiciable but meritless *(slide 30)*. Souter refused a blanket rule of non-interference *(slide 30)*.

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

The deck presents several hypotheticals to test the political question boundary:

*Examples of political questions:* (1) National Guard shoots student, family sues for negligent training; (2) Treaty continuity when Nation X merges or recreates itself as Nation Y; (3) President's unilateral rescission of a treaty recognizing the Taiwan government; (4) Republican-controlled Senate declaring its candidate has won the Florida Senate race against a Democratic challenger; (5) Whether a state has ratified a constitutional amendment; (6) Disputes over who are legitimate delegates to a political party convention *(slide 22)*.

*Non-examples (justiciable):* (1) House excludes one of its members because they believe he is a crook (Powell v. McCormack); (2) Revenue bill originating in the Senate; (3) Executive determination whether Japan's whaling practices diminish effectiveness of an international whaling treaty *(slide 23)*.

The deck challenges students directly: can you dislike Bush v. Gore without also disliking Baker v. Carr? *(slide 21)*. Slide 19 notes the tension that Brennan announces a "capacious and discretionary test that gives the court broad latitude in the future" yet finds none of these factors present in Baker itself *(slide 19)* -- leaving the doctrine more discretionary than principled in application.

## Key Takeaways

- The political question doctrine has two distinct homes: Guarantee Clause claims (Luther v. Borden), and structural questions where text commits the issue to another branch.
- Baker's six factors are highly discretionary; the crucial point is factor 2 (lack of judicially manageable standards) because once a measurable standard exists (like numerosity), courts can enter.
- Baker did NOT overrule Colegrove; Brennan distinguished it as involving only a Guarantee Clause claim (bad lawyering), suggesting Baker was really about rechanneling claims through Equal Protection.
- Frankfurter's dissent retains significant appeal: the political thicket concern does not vanish by relabeling a political controversy as an equal protection case.
- Nixon v. United States shows the doctrine's continuing vitality for institutional structure cases, especially where there is no effective remedy.
- Baker is widely credited with enabling the "one person, one vote" reapportionment revolution, which Warren saw as more transformative than any other Warren Court decision.
