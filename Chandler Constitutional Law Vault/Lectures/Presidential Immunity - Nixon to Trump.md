## Professor Emphasis

### From source

The deck's roadmap announces a reverse-chronological structure: begin with Trump v. United States and its three-tier framework, then flash back to the cases that built the doctrine -- US v. Nixon (1974), Nixon v. Fitzgerald (1982), Clinton v. Jones (1997), Trump v. Vance (2020) -- and return to the Trump dissents and concurrences *(slide 2)*. The deck's structural argument: the Trump majority "used Nixon's recognition of presumptive privilege as a springboard. If a mere subpoena warranted a presumptive privilege, then criminal prosecution -- a 'far greater threat of intrusion' -- warrants at least presumptive immunity" *(slide 12)*. And: "if civil damages warrant absolute immunity, criminal prosecution -- with its 'far greater deterrent' and 'peculiar public opprobrium' -- warrants at least as much protection" *(slide 15)*. The deck identifies the evidentiary rule as the most dangerous feature, per the dissent, because it insulates not just official acts but evidence about official acts, making it "extraordinarily difficult to prosecute any Presidential misconduct" *(slide 25)*. The Clinton v. Jones lesson is anchored on slide 17: the case "underestimated how disruptive litigation could be to a sitting President," and the deck explicitly extends the lesson to Trump v. Vance on slide 20. The Discussion Questions slide preserves the line-drawing critique, the workability question, and the Thomas Special Counsel concern *(slide 30)*.

This page treats the lecture as having a unifying analytical structure -- "the Court's predictions in earlier cases proved wrong" as a recurring theme -- which the deck illustrates by example on Clinton v. The "doctrinal arc" packaging at the end (presumptive privilege → absolute civil → no immunity for unofficial → no state subpoena immunity → three-tier criminal) is the deck's table on slide 28, rewritten as narrative.

## Lecture Outline

### Trump v United States (2024) -- The Framework

**The Facts.** In August 2023, a federal grand jury indicted Trump on four counts for post-election conduct, alleging a conspiracy to overturn the 2020 election by spreading knowingly false claims of election fraud to obstruct certification *(slide 4)*. Trump characterized the indicted conduct as official: public statements, communications with DOJ, state officials, VP, Members of Congress, and organizing contingent elector slates *(slide 4)*. Trump moved to dismiss on presidential immunity grounds; the district court denied, the D.C. Circuit affirmed, and the Supreme Court granted certiorari *(slide 4)*.

**The Three-Tier Framework (Roberts, C.J., 6-3).**

- Tier 1 (Core Powers): Exclusive constitutional authority (e.g., pardon, recognition, removal); immunity is ABSOLUTE; prosecution structurally barred *(slide 5)*.
- Tier 2 (Other Official Acts): Acts within the "outer perimeter" of official responsibility; immunity is at least PRESUMPTIVE; the government must show "no dangers of intrusion" to rebut *(slide 5)*.
- Tier 3 (Unofficial Acts): Purely private conduct, not an exercise of presidential power; NO IMMUNITY; prosecution proceeds normally per Clinton v. Jones *(slide 5)*.

**Two Critical Doctrinal Moves.**

First, no motive inquiry: courts may NOT inquire into the President's motives when classifying conduct as official or unofficial, because that would "expose even the most obvious instances of official conduct to judicial examination on the mere allegation of improper purpose" *(slide 8)*. An action is not deemed unofficial merely because it allegedly violates a law -- otherwise "every allegation that an action was unlawful" would defeat immunity *(slide 8)*.

Second, the evidentiary rule: evidence of immune official acts may NOT be introduced even to prove charges based on unofficial conduct -- "The Constitution deals with substance, not shadows" *(slide 5)*. The "outer perimeter" test extends immunity to all actions "not manifestly or palpably beyond" presidential authority -- an extremely generous boundary; even a President acting contrary to Congress's will (Tier 3) is not automatically reclassified as "unofficial" *(slide 8)*.

**Applying the Framework to Specific Conduct.**
- Discussions with Acting AG about investigations: "quintessentially executive function"; threatened removal of AG = conclusive and preclusive authority; ABSOLUTE IMMUNITY (Tier 1A) *(slide 7)*.
- Pressuring VP re: January 6 certification: official conduct (Art. II relationship), but the VP presides as President of the Senate (Art. I role); PRESUMPTIVE IMMUNITY -- remanded (Tier 2) *(slide 7)*.
- Contacts with state legislators re: elector certification: cannot be neatly categorized within a presidential function; REMANDED for fact-specific analysis *(slide 7)*.
- January 6 speech and tweets: presidential "bully pulpit" vs. candidate speech; classification requires "content, form, and context"; REMANDED *(slide 7)*.

**The Majority's Structural Justification.** Unlike anyone else, the President is a branch of government; immunity protects the office, not the person *(slide 26)*. The Constitution vests "sweeping powers and duties" in the President, and accounting for this "does not place him above the law; it preserves the basic structure of the Constitution" *(slide 26)*. Historical silence: since the founding, no President has ever faced criminal charges for official conduct *(slide 26)*. The cannibalization fear: without immunity, each successive President can prosecute his predecessor under broad statutes like § 371 -- "conspiracy to obstruct a department" *(slide 26)*. "The enfeebling of the Presidency through retaliatory prosecution is what the Framers' design sought to prevent" *(slide 26)*.

### Flashback: United States v Nixon (1974)

**The Facts.** June 1972 saw the Watergate break-in; the cover-up unraveled over 1972-73; Special Prosecutor Archibald Cox was appointed *(slide 10)*. In October 1973, Nixon fired Cox in the "Saturday Night Massacre" and Leon Jaworski was appointed *(slide 10)*. A grand jury indicted seven Nixon aides and named Nixon as an unindicted co-conspirator, and Jaworski subpoenaed tape recordings of 64 specific presidential conversations *(slide 10)*. Nixon moved to quash, claiming absolute executive privilege; his key gambit was declaring he would comply only with a "definitive" ruling -- pressuring the Court toward unanimity *(slide 10)*.

**The Holding (Burger, C.J., 8-0 -- Rehnquist recused).** Executive privilege is constitutionally grounded but NOT absolute *(slide 10)*. Presidential communications are "presumptively privileged"; the privilege is "fundamental to the operation of Government" and rooted in the President's need for "candid, objective, and even blunt or harsh opinions" from advisers *(slide 11)*. But there is no "absolute, unqualified Presidential privilege of immunity from judicial process under all circumstances," and a generalized assertion must yield to a "demonstrated, specific need" for evidence in a criminal trial *(slide 11)*.

**Deliberate Ambiguity.** The opinion was carefully crafted to achieve 8-0 unanimity and explicitly declined to address civil litigation, congressional demands, national security claims, and the scope of in camera review *(slide 12)*. The connection to Trump v. United States: the Trump majority used Nixon's recognition of presumptive privilege as a springboard -- if a mere subpoena warranted a presumptive privilege, then criminal prosecution warrants at least presumptive immunity *(slide 12)*.

### Flashback: Nixon v Fitzgerald (1982)

**The Facts.** A. Ernest Fitzgerald, an Air Force management analyst, testified before Congress about cost overruns on the C-5A transport aircraft *(slide 13)*. He was then fired in a "reduction in force" widely understood as retaliation for whistleblowing; Fitzgerald sued Nixon for civil damages, arguing the President personally ordered his termination *(slide 13)*.

**The Holding (Powell, J., 5-4).** A former President is entitled to absolute immunity from civil damages for all acts within the "outer perimeter" of official responsibility *(slide 14)*. This is "a functionally mandated incident of the President's unique office, rooted in the constitutional tradition of the separation of powers" *(slide 14)*. The risk that litigation would make the President "unduly cautious" in discharging duties justifies the rule, and immunity "merely precludes a particular private remedy" to advance "compelling public ends" *(slide 14)*.

**Not "Above the Law."** The majority identified alternative checks: impeachment remains available, press scrutiny and congressional oversight serve as deterrents, and historical stature plus reelection incentives cabin presidential misconduct *(slide 14)*.

**What Fitzgerald Left Open.** Criminal prosecution (explicitly reserved), suits for injunctive or declaratory relief, unofficial or private conduct, and whether Congress could create a statutory cause of action against the President *(slide 15)*. The Trump majority later used this reservation as evidence that absolute immunity for official acts logically extends to criminal prosecution -- the "far greater deterrent" with "peculiar public opprobrium" *(slide 15)*.

### Flashback: Clinton v Jones (1997)

**The Facts.** Paula Jones sued President Clinton for civil damages arising from alleged sexual harassment while he was Governor of Arkansas -- pre-presidential and purely unofficial *(slide 16)*. Clinton argued for temporary immunity, with the suit to be stayed until he left office; the district court granted a partial stay, and the Eighth Circuit reversed *(slide 16)*.

**The Holding (Stevens, J., 9-0).** No temporary immunity from civil litigation for unofficial, pre-presidential acts; separation of powers does not require federal courts to stay all private actions until a President leaves office *(slide 17)*. The rationale for immunity -- protecting official functions -- "is inapplicable to unofficial conduct" *(slide 17)*. The majority expressed confidence the case would be manageable and cause minimal disruption *(slide 17)*.

**The Court's Prediction Was Wrong.** Events proved otherwise: the Jones deposition led to the Lewinsky investigation and impeachment *(slide 17)*. Breyer concurred but warned the Court should retain flexibility to order stays *(slide 17)*. The deck poses the explicit comparison question: how does this experience inform our assessment of Trump v. Vance? *(slide 17)*

### Flashback: Trump v Vance (2020)

**The Facts.** Manhattan DA Cyrus Vance investigated potential state-law violations connected to hush-money payments during the 2016 campaign and issued a grand jury subpoena to Mazars USA (Trump's accounting firm) for personal financial records *(slide 18)*. Trump sued in his personal capacity to block compliance, claiming absolute immunity from state criminal process; the district court and Second Circuit both rejected absolute immunity *(slide 18)*.

**The Holding (Roberts, C.J., 7-2).** No absolute immunity from state criminal subpoenas under Article II or the Supremacy Clause; no heightened "need" standard for subpoenas seeking private papers *(slide 19)*. For private papers, a President stands "in nearly the same situation with any other individual" (citing Burr) *(slide 19)*. Safeguards: the President may challenge on the same grounds as any citizen (bad faith, undue burden, overbreadth); the Supremacy Clause bars state interference with official duties; courts should use "high respect" owed to the office to inform proceedings *(slide 19)*.

**The Dissents.** Thomas warned of "an avalanche of politically motivated investigations" and criticized the lack of a clear protective standard *(slide 19)*. Alito argued a "demonstrated specific need" standard should be required for state subpoenas against a sitting President *(slide 19)*. The deck flags the comparison to Clinton v. Jones explicitly: the Court "may have underestimated the degree of disruption," and the subsequent state prosecution -- conviction, sentencing, and the complications of prosecuting a President-elect -- arguably validates the dissenters' concerns *(slide 20)*.

### Concurrences and Dissents in Trump v United States

**Justice Thomas (concurring).** Writes separately to question whether the Special Counsel's office was lawfully established *(slide 22)*. The Constitution requires that federal offices be "established by Law" (Art. II, § 2, cl. 2), but the AG relied on general statutes of authority rather than a specific statute creating the Special Counsel office *(slide 22)*. Thomas analogizes to the independent counsel in Morrison v. Olson, created by the Ethics in Government Act, which later lapsed and was not reauthorized *(slide 22)*. If the Special Counsel's office is not "established by Law," the AG may lack authority to appoint anyone to prosecute a former President -- and "a private citizen cannot criminally prosecute anyone, let alone a former President" *(slide 22)*. Thomas also raises Appointments Clause issues: if the Special Counsel is a principal officer, Senate confirmation was required *(slide 22)*.

**Justice Barrett (concurring in part).** Agrees with most of the opinion but does NOT join Part III-C (the evidentiary rule) *(slide 23)*. Her two-step alternative: (1) Does the criminal statute reach the official conduct? (2) If so, does prosecution pose "dangers of intrusion"? *(slide 23)*. She argues Federal Rules of Evidence (FRE 403) can handle prejudice concerns case-by-case: "The Constitution does not require blinding juries to the circumstances surrounding conduct for which Presidents can be held liable" *(slide 23)*. Her example: in a bribery case, the jury must hear about both the quid and the quo *(slide 23)*.

**Justice Sotomayor (dissent, joined by Kagan and Jackson).** "The majority invents an atextual, ahistorical, and unjustifiable immunity that places the President above the law" *(slide 24)*. She concludes: "With fear for our democracy, I dissent" *(slide 24)*. Textual arguments: the Speech or Debate Clause gives Congress express immunity, with no comparable provision for the President; the Impeachment Judgment Clause says officials "shall nevertheless be liable" to prosecution -- expressio unius; Hamilton's Federalist 69 and 77 said a former President "would be liable to prosecution and punishment in the ordinary course of law"; Wilson and Story confirmed the same at ratification *(slide 24)*. Precedential arguments: Fitzgerald covers civil damages by private plaintiffs, while criminal prosecution is the Government enforcing the law on behalf of the public -- fundamentally different *(slide 24)*. US v. Nixon rejected even presumptive privilege for White House tapes; how can the majority grant absolute immunity from prosecution itself? *(slide 24)*

**The Parade of Horribles.** Under the majority's framework: a President who orders military assassination of a political rival is absolutely immune (commanding the military = core power); a President who organizes a coup is immune for the same reason; a President who accepts a bribe for a pardon is absolutely immune -- and the evidentiary rule bars using the pardon to prove bribery *(slide 25)*.

**Justice Jackson (dissenting).** Argues the majority's "core" immunity is "both unnecessary and misguided," its official-acts immunity is "just as bad as it sounds," and the evidentiary holding is "unprecedented" and "nonsensical" *(slide 27)*. "The majority's immunity doctrine ... will have disastrous consequences for the Presidency and for our democracy" *(slide 27)*.

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

The deck poses several structured discussion questions *(slide 30)*:

Is the three-tier framework workable? How should lower courts distinguish "core" from "other official" from "unofficial" acts? *(slide 30)*. The majority left these questions almost entirely to case-by-case development in lower courts with no guiding principles beyond the "outer perimeter" test.

Does the evidentiary rule effectively create near-total immunity? Is Barrett's narrower approach -- FRE 403 balancing rather than a categorical bar -- more practical? *(slide 30)*. Could a bribery prosecution ever succeed if courts cannot hear evidence about the official act that was the quid?

The cannibalization fear versus the impunity fear *(slide 30)*. The majority worries each President will prosecute his predecessor; the dissent worries Presidents can act with impunity knowing prosecution is unavailable. Which dystopia does the structure of American politics make more likely?

The deck explicitly compares the Clinton v. Jones-style miscalculation to Vance: "Did Trump v. Vance similarly underestimate the disruption of state criminal process?" *(slide 30)*. The "outer perimeter" test interacts with the no-motive-inquiry rule in a potentially sweeping way: any action taken through the apparatus of office receives presumptive immunity, and the government cannot prove the action was unofficial by showing it was politically motivated *(slide 30)*. What conduct falls clearly outside the outer perimeter?

Thomas's Special Counsel question: if the office is not "established by Law," does this eliminate the primary mechanism for prosecuting a sitting President's associates? *(slide 30)*

## Key Takeaways

- The doctrinal arc: presumptive executive privilege for subpoenas (Nixon, 1974) → absolute civil immunity for official acts (Fitzgerald, 1982) → no immunity for unofficial acts (Clinton v. Jones, 1997) → no immunity from state criminal subpoenas for private papers (Vance, 2020) → absolute immunity for core powers, presumptive immunity for other official acts in criminal prosecution (Trump v. United States, 2024).
- Courts making predictions about institutional disruption should be humble: Clinton v. Jones and Trump v. Vance both produced consequences the unanimous or large-majority Courts failed to anticipate.
- The no-motive-inquiry rule and the evidentiary rule combine to create a doctrine that may effectively be near-total immunity in practice: most presidential conduct occurs through official channels, and courts cannot look behind the official form to find unofficial substance.
- Barrett's concurrence points toward a potentially more workable framework -- as-applied constitutional challenges with FRE balancing, rather than a categorical evidentiary bar.
- Thomas's Appointments Clause concurrence is a separate challenge to the Special Counsel mechanism entirely, independent of the immunity holding.
- The Youngstown and Trump v. United States frameworks address different questions: Youngstown asks whether the President has the power to act; Trump v. United States asks whether the act, once taken, can be prosecuted. A President may be acting at the lowest ebb of legitimate power (Youngstown Category 3) but still receive presumptive criminal immunity if the act falls within the outer perimeter of official conduct.
