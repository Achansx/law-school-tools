## Professor Emphasis

### From source

The deck spends substantial time on Texas's pre-Loving anti-miscegenation history -- the Republic of Texas 1837 statute, the 1858 Texas Penal Code Article 386 (two-to-five years' confinement for whites who knowingly marry a "negro" or descendant), and the Texas Constitution of 1869 *(slides 3-4)*. The deck reproduces *Bonds v. Foster* (1872) and *Honey v. Clark* (1872) as Reconstruction-era Texas Supreme Court decisions reading the Fourteenth Amendment to abrogate Texas anti-miscegenation law *(slide 5)*. *Frasher v. State* (1877) is the counter-revolution: Texas Supreme Court rejects a Slaughterhouse-grounded challenge by a white defendant, and the deck quotes the court's "if the federal government can determine who may marry in a state, there is no limit to its power" passage from *State v. Gibson* *(slides 6-7)*. *Ex Parte Francois* (W.D. Tex. 1879) is presented as a federal habeas case in which the judge expressed doubts but ruled the statute did not violate the constitution's letter, and reproduced the white-responsibility paternalism that "for such unnatural marriages, the whites are mainly to blame, and this may furnish some excuse, if not a justification, for punishing them alone" *(slide 8)*. Texas cities went farther -- Fort Worth criminalizing interracial sexual intercourse, with *Strauss v. State* (1915) upholding *(slide 9)*.

The deck then turns to Loving. Virginia's Racial Purity Act of 1924 is reproduced verbatim, including the "Pocahontas exception" allowing one-sixteenth American Indian blood without disqualifying as "white" *(slide 10)*. Sections 254, 257, 258, and 259 of the updated Virginia code are reproduced -- intermarriage prohibited, void-without-decree, leaving the state to evade, and the felony punishment of one-to-five years *(slides 11-12)*. The deck notes 16 states still had anti-miscegenation laws and reproduces the Bazile trial-court "Almighty God created the races … and he placed them on separate continents" passage, with the deck's editorial aside "Curious, then, that the Almighty permitted boats" *(slide 13)*. The deck lists "other facts": when the 1866 Civil Rights Act was passed, proponents were asked about anti-miscegenation laws and assured CRA1866 would not interfere; the Fourteenth Amendment grew out of concerns about CRA1866's constitutionality and no one said anything about miscegenation; over 99.4% of Virginia was White or Black in 1960; the post-hoc rationale was sociological evidence about interracial marriage failure rates; unmarried sex was itself a crime in Virginia *(slide 14)*.

The deck poses the open argument question -- what precedents would help the Lovings, and what would help the state? *(slide 15)*. For the state: *Pace v. Alabama* (1883) and formal symmetry; the deck observes that under Pace, an A-W couple is punished more than a W-W couple having sex with the same white person -- evidently not a problem under Pace's logic *(slide 16)*. For the Lovings: *McLaughlin v. Florida* (1964) reproduced in extended block quote -- "judicial inquiry under the Equal Protection Clause does not end with a showing of equal application … the courts must reach and determine the question whether the classifications drawn in a statute are reasonable in light of its purpose," with the deck noting the McLaughlin Court found "nothing in this suggested legislative purpose which makes it essential to punish promiscuity of one racial group and not that of another" *(slide 17)*. The deck draws a "link to Romer v. Evans" alongside McLaughlin's purpose-based analysis *(slide 17)*. The result: the Court accepts both equal protection theory and due process theory, and the deck closes by asking what power remains after Loving to resist polygamous marriage, incestuous marriage, homosexual marriage, and "AI marriage (half kidding)" *(slide 18)*.

### Editor's framing

This page treats Loving as the doctrinal template for modern fundamental-rights adjudication of intimate associations, and runs a Pace→McLaughlin→Loving→Zablocki→Turner→Obergefell lineage. The Pace-McLaughlin-Loving doctrinal sequence comes from Slides 16-17; the Zablocki-Turner-Obergefell continuation is editorial synthesis the deck itself does not pursue (the deck stops at Slide 18's "what power is there after Loving to resist … homosexual marriage" rhetorical prompt). The framing of Loving as the doctrinal template for Obergefell's dual EP+DP deployment is editorial extrapolation from that rhetorical prompt, not a connection the deck makes directly. The Naim v Naim (1955) "Warren Court ducked the issue" framing is editor-supplied background -- the deck does not name Naim.

## Lecture Outline

The deck opens with a map of anti-miscegenation laws across U.S. states color-coded by repeal era (gray never had law, green pre-1887, yellow 1947-1967, red repealed in 1967 as a result of Loving) *(slide 2)*. It then walks through Texas's pre-Loving history in depth: the Republic of Texas 1837 statute, the 1858 Texas Penal Code Article 386 (two-to-five years' confinement for whites who knowingly marry a "negro or person of mixed blood … to the third generation inclusive"), and the Texas Constitution of 1869 *(slides 3-4)*. *Bonds v. Foster* (1872) and *Honey v. Clark* (1872) are framed as Reconstruction-era Texas Supreme Court decisions reading the Fourteenth Amendment to abrogate Texas anti-miscegenation law *(slide 5)*.

*Frasher v. State* (1877) is the counter-revolution: a white man charged under Article 386, the Texas Supreme Court declaring "no question more important … has ever been before this court," and rejecting both 14th Amendment and Civil Rights Act of 1866 challenges -- with the Slaughterhouse-grounded argument that the Fourteenth Amendment was for the protection of former slaves and not whites *(slide 6)*. The deck reproduces *State v. Gibson*'s "apocalypse music" passage about marriage as a "God-given, civilizing, and Christianizing institution" and the federalism alarm that "if the Federal government can determine who may marry in a state, there is no limit to its power" *(slide 7)*. *Ex Parte Francois* (W.D. Tex. 1879) is then presented: a white carpenter sentenced to the five-year maximum; the federal habeas court expressing doubts but ruling the statute did not violate the constitution's letter, and reproducing the white-responsibility paternalism that "for such unnatural marriages, the whites are mainly to blame, and this may furnish some excuse, if not a justification, for punishing them alone" *(slide 8)*. The deck flags Fort Worth's even-farther statute criminalizing interracial sexual intercourse, upheld in *Strauss v. State* (1915) *(slide 9)*.

The deck then turns to Loving. Virginia's Racial Purity Act of 1924 is reproduced verbatim, including the "Pocahontas exception" allowing one-sixteenth American Indian blood without disqualifying as "white" *(slide 10)*. Sections 254, 257, 258, and 259 of the updated Virginia code are reproduced -- intermarriage prohibited, void-without-decree, leaving the state to evade, and the felony punishment of one-to-five years *(slides 11-12)*. Richard Loving, a white man, and Mildred Jeter, a Black and Indigenous woman, married in Washington, D.C. and were arrested upon returning to Virginia. The deck reproduces Judge Bazile's infamous Virginia trial-court opinion: "Almighty God created the races white, black, yellow, Malay and red, and he placed them on separate continents. And, but for the interference with his arrangement, there would be no cause for such marriage. The fact that he separated the races shows that he did not intend for the races to mix" *(slide 13)*. The deck's editorial aside: "Curious, then, that the Almighty permitted boats" *(slide 13)*.

The deck lists "other facts": when the 1866 Civil Rights Act was passed, proponents were asked about anti-miscegenation laws and assured CRA1866 would not interfere; the Fourteenth Amendment grew out of concerns about CRA1866's constitutionality and no one said anything about miscegenation; over 99.4% of Virginia was White or Black in 1960; the post-hoc rationale was sociological evidence on interracial marriage failure rates; unmarried sex was itself a crime in Virginia *(slide 14)*. The deck then poses the open argument: what precedents would help the Lovings, and what would help the state? *(slide 15)*.

For the state: *Pace v. Alabama* (1883) and formal symmetry -- the Court upheld a conviction under an Alabama statute imposing greater penalties for adultery or fornication between a white person and a Negro than for same-race conduct, reasoning that punishment for each participant was the same *(slide 16)*. The deck observes that under Pace's logic, a Black-White couple is punished more than a White-White couple having sex with the same white person -- evidently not a problem *(slide 16)*.

For the Lovings: *McLaughlin v. Florida* (1964) is reproduced in extended block quote -- "judicial inquiry under the Equal Protection Clause does not end with a showing of equal application among the members of the class defined by the legislation. The courts must reach and determine the question whether the classifications drawn in a statute are reasonable in light of its purpose." The deck reproduces McLaughlin's finding that the State's suggested legislative purpose -- preventing breaches of sexual decency -- made nothing essential about punishing one racial group's promiscuity and not another's *(slide 17)*. The deck draws an editorial parallel ("Now what case does that sound like? Link to Romer v. Evans") *(slide 17)*.

The result: the Court accepts both equal protection theory and due process theory, and the deck closes by asking what power remains after Loving to resist polygamous marriage, incestuous marriage, homosexual marriage, and "AI marriage (half kidding)" *(slide 18)*.

## Key Takeaways

- Racial classifications require strict scrutiny regardless of whether they apply symmetrically to all affected races.
- Loving combines an Equal Protection holding (no racial classifications without compelling interest) with a Substantive Due Process holding (marriage as a fundamental right).
- The lineage runs Pace (1883) → McLaughlin (1964) → Loving (1967) → Zablocki v Redhail (1978) → Turner v Safley (1987) → Obergefell (2015).
- "Equal application" defenses are not a safe harbor when the classification is drawn on a suspect ground.
- Exam framing: Loving is the case to cite for both strict-scrutiny-for-race and fundamental-right-to-marry analyses.
