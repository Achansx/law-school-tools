## Lecture Outline

**The HEROES Act and the Loan Cancellation Plan**

The HEROES Act (2003) made permanent authority that had previously been provided only for Desert Storm (1991) and post-9/11 (2002): the Secretary "may waive or modify any statutory or regulatory provision applicable to the student financial assistance programs under title IV of the [Education Act] as the Secretary deems necessary in connection with a war or other military operation or national emergency" (slide 2). The Higher Education Act separately authorizes loan discharge only in narrow circumstances — death, permanent disability, bankruptcy, school closure, or specified public service after minimum-year requirements (slide 2). Secretary Cardona invoked the HEROES Act's "waive or modify" language, citing COVID-19, to cancel up to $10,000 in principal for borrowers earning under $125,000 and up to $20,000 for Pell Grant recipients (slide 3). The plan would have cancelled $430 billion in principal, completely erased 20 million debts, and reduced median balances from $29,400 to $13,600 (slide 3). The announcement came in August 2022, weeks before the midterm elections (slide 3). More than 80 student loan forgiveness bills had been introduced in Congress; none had passed (slide 3).

**Why Nebraska and Not Missouri?**

The case name is a procedural accident: Nebraska was the lead respondent when the case arrived at the Supreme Court on the government's cert petition (slide 4). In substance, only Missouri could articulate a concrete financial injury — through MOHELA, the Missouri Higher Education Loan Authority (slide 4). The Court wrote: "Because we conclude that the Secretary's plan harms MOHELA and thereby directly injures Missouri… we need not consider the other [states'] theories" (slide 4). Without Missouri's MOHELA argument, the entire case likely would have been dismissed for lack of standing (slide 4).

**Missouri's MOHELA Theory**

MOHELA is a nonprofit government corporation created by Missouri statute to help Missourians access student loans (slide 5). It services federal student loans under contract with the Department of Education, earning a per-loan administrative fee (slide 5). It has provided $230M for Missouri college development and nearly $300M in grants and scholarships (slide 5). Missouri's theory: MOHELA is a state "instrumentality" — created by the state, supervised by state officials, reporting to the Missouri Department of Education, and dissolvable only by the state — so harm to MOHELA performing its public function is a direct injury to Missouri itself, citing Arkansas v. Texas (1953) and Lebron v. National Railroad Passenger Corp. (1995) (slide 5). The chain of causation the deck diagrams: Secretary cancels $430B in loans → borrowers' debts erased or reduced → MOHELA loses $44M/yr in fees → Missouri claims direct injury (slide 5).

**Roberts's Majority on Standing**

Roberts held that MOHELA is, "by law and function, an instrumentality of Missouri" (slide 6). It was created by the State, supervised by the State, reports to the State, serves a public function, and is dissolvable only by the State (slide 6). "The harm to MOHELA in the performance of its public function is necessarily a direct injury to Missouri itself" (slide 6). The fact that Missouri chose to exercise authority through a public corporation "does not bar the State from suing to remedy that harm itself" (slide 6).

**Kagan's Dissent on Standing**

Kagan: "Where's MOHELA? As far from this suit as it can manage" (slide 6). MOHELA did not bring this suit, is not a party, not an amicus, and was "not involved with the decision" to file; when the AG sought documents he had to file formal sunshine-law demands (slide 6). MOHELA is a separate legal entity — financially independent, with its own power to sue, and Missouri's revenues don't depend on MOHELA's fees (slide 6). "This case should have been open-and-shut. Missouri and MOHELA are legally, and also financially, 'separate entities'" (slide 6).

**The Skeptic's View**

The deck identifies three problems with the majority's instrumentality holding (slide 7): (1) MOHELA's loss is not Missouri's loss — MOHELA is financially independent, the $44M annual fee loss accrues to a separate corporate entity, and not even Missouri claimed MOHELA's revenue gets passed through to the state; (2) MOHELA chose not to sue and actively distanced itself — it had express statutory authority to sue on its own behalf, was "not involved" with the AG's decision, and refused to cooperate voluntarily; (3) the "instrumentality" label shouldn't override separate corporate identity — many government corporations are called "instrumentalities" without losing their legal separateness, and as the dissent argued, the majority "blows through a constitutional guardrail intended to keep courts acting like courts" (slide 7). Kagan, dissenting: "In adjudicating Missouri's claim, the majority reaches out to decide a matter it has no business deciding" (slide 7).

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

The lecture's central hypothetical is implicit in the doctrinal tension: could a state sue the federal government for any policy that reduces the revenues of a state-created corporation? The $44 million annual fee loss to MOHELA becomes the template. If a federal policy reduces tuition payments to state universities (which are also state instrumentalities), does the state have standing? If the EPA approves a permit that costs a state power authority revenue, is that a direct state injury?

Kagan's rhetorical question frames the policy concern: "Where's MOHELA? As far from this suit as it can manage." The question of whether a corporation's unwillingness to sue itself should defeat instrumentality standing is unresolved.

## Key Takeaways

- Biden v. Nebraska introduces an instrumentality theory of state standing: injury to a state-created government corporation can be a direct injury to the state itself, even if the corporation is separately chartered and financially independent.
- The majority's holding is contested: Kagan's dissent argues the corporate separateness is legally and financially real and the majority "blows through" an Article III guardrail.
- Missouri's standing claim succeeded solely on the MOHELA theory; none of the other five states' theories were reached.
- The case simultaneously applies the Major Questions Doctrine: "waive or modify" does not clearly authorize $430 billion in across-the-board debt cancellation when the Higher Education Act provides only narrow discharge authority.
- The standing holding may significantly expand the range of state-plaintiff standing in federal litigation against administrative agency action.
