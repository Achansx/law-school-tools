## Lecture Outline

**I. The Conceptual Problem**
How does the President actually control a vast federal bureaucracy (slide 2)? Congress delegates enormous power to agencies without being highly specific, creating the risk of "rogue" officials who pursue independent agendas (slide 2). The judiciary must police separation of powers without dismantling the administrative state or eliminating the executive accountability that Article II demands (slide 2). The deck flags two procedural postures where these issues arise: high-profile employment lawsuits by removed officers seeking backpay/reinstatement (Myers, Humphrey's) and challenges by private parties injured by an improperly appointed or improperly insulated officer (Morrison, Free Enterprise, Seila, Collins, Arthrex) (slide 4).

**II. The Appointment Power (Article II, Section 2, Clause 2)**
The President nominates, and with the Senate's advice and consent appoints, principal officers, and Congress may vest appointment of inferior officers in the President alone, in courts of law, or in heads of departments (slide 3). The principal/inferior distinction determines who must approve an appointment.

The Edmond test (current doctrine from Edmond v. United States, 1997) treats an inferior officer as one who has a supervisor nominated by the President with Senate confirmation (a "de facto" principal officer) and whose work the superior directs and supervises (slide 5). A principal officer is one with no such supervisor, or one whose principal-officer superior does not directly supervise the officer's work (slide 5). Morrison (1988) applied the older inferior/principal dichotomy in a removal context, and Freytag (1991) said principal officers have "significant duties and discretion" without fully defining the phrase (slide 5).

Buckley v. Valeo (1976): after Watergate, one Federal Election Commission design gave the House, Senate, and President two appointees each as principal officers; the Court rejected this in a 294-page per curiam -- "no points for creativity," separation of powers wins (slide 6).

**III. The Removal Power**
The text of Article II does not explicitly grant a removal power (slide 7 prints the same Appointments Clause text). The President's removal authority has been developed through case law over nearly a century, oscillating between formalism/unitary executive theory and functionalism (slide 8).

Myers v. United States (1926) (C.J. Taft): an 1876 Civil Service Reform law made postmaster a four-year position requiring Senate consent for removal; in 1920 Wilson dismissed Myers (Portland postmaster) without that consent, and Myers sued for his salary; the Court held Congress cannot condition presidential removal power over the postmaster (slide 11).

Humphrey's Executor v. United States (1935): FDR fired Humphrey (a Hoover FTC appointee) without congressional consent; the FTC was an "independent" agency of five commissioners removable only for cause, intended as a "body of experts" engaged in "quasi legislative, quasi judicial" functions; Myers was recast as "confined to purely executive officers," and Congress could restrict removal here because the Commissioner was not an "arm or eye" of the President (slide 12). The deck flags Humphrey's as NOT GOOD LAW AFTER SEILA (slide 12). The rationale was that the independent agency was "wholly disconnected from the executive," provided reports to Congress (legislative) and recommendations to Courts (judicial), and consisted of experts needing long terms to give objective policy advice (slide 13).

2025 application: President Trump has dismissed members of the NLRB and EEOC without citing the "inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance in office" required by existing statutes, with no prior notice or hearing, and NLRB member Glynne Wilcox has sued (slide 14). The deck reproduces Sarah Harris's February 12, 2025 letter as Acting Solicitor General (slide 15).

Morrison v. Olson (1988): during the Reagan administration the EPA tried to avoid disclosure of Superfund documents; Ted Olson (assistant AG) refused to provide them and allegedly lied to Congress; Alexia Morrison was appointed under the Ethics in Government Act to investigate, and Olson argued Title VI prevented the President from controlling execution of the laws because he could not stop "rogue" special prosecutors (slide 17). Olson lost: "the real question is whether the removal restrictions are of such a nature that they impede the President's ability to perform his constitutional duty," the President still appointed and could remove the AG (the superior principal officer), and an AG trigger was required to initiate Morrison's investigation (slide 18). Scalia dissented: no creativity in separation of powers, no escape from constitutional responsibilities, democracy requires clear accountability even if the Rule of Law is sacrificed in individual cases (slide 18).

Free Enterprise Fund v. PCAOB (2010) (5-4, Roberts, CJ): Sarbanes-Oxley created the PCAOB after Enron as a federal nonprofit corporation under SEC oversight, with criminal penalties for violating PCAOB rules; the issue was whether the President could be restricted in removing a principal officer who is in turn restricted in removing an inferior officer (slide 19). Rule: two layers of for-cause removal is too much; political process/functionalism lose, accountability and separation-of-powers/"headless fourth branch" concerns win (slide 19). Roberts's slippery-slope passage: if Congress can shelter the bureaucracy behind two layers of good-cause tenure, why not a third -- at oral argument the Government would not concede even five layers would be too many -- and officers "safely encased within a Matryoshka doll of tenure protections" would be immune from Presidential oversight (slide 20).

Seila Law v. CFPB (2020) (5-4, Roberts, CJ): the CFPB had a single director with broad enforcement authority, removable only for cause (slide 21). The retrofit: Humphrey's authorizes for-cause protection only for a "group" of principal officers leading an expert agency, and Perkins (1886) / Morrison (1988) authorize tenure protections for certain "inferior" officers with "narrowly defined duties" (slide 22). Roberts's majority emphasized that with separation of powers, tradition matters: a lack of historical precedent is "[p]erhaps the most telling indication of [a] severe constitutional problem" with an executive entity; the Framers made the President the most democratic and politically accountable official; the strategy is to divide power everywhere except for the Presidency, with the President supervising individual executive officials (slide 23). The Take Care Clause requires that the President be able to remove those for whom he cannot continue to be responsible (slide 23).

Kagan's dissent: the accountability dilemma -- "five unelected judges rejecting the result of that democratic process" (slide 24). The adaptability song: "novelty is not the test of constitutionality when it comes to structuring agencies," the Framers understood new times require new measures, the administrative sphere is replete with experiment and change, citing McCulloch (slide 24). On the textualists making things up: bad to extrapolate from the Vesting Clause an unrestricted removal power constraining Congress under the Necessary and Proper Clause; worse to extrapolate from general structure and implicit values; worst to extrapolate a distinction "as prosaic as that between the SEC and the CFPB" -- a single vs. multi-headed agency (slide 39). The battle of the clauses: majority anchors on Vesting Clause and Take Care Clause; dissent emphasizes the Necessary and Proper Clause and the Senate's advice-and-consent power (slide 25). Kagan almost ended up in "Rucho-land herself" -- the right balance between presidential control and independence is contested and value-laden, and that is precisely why courts should stay (mostly) out of the way (slide 40).

Thomas concurrence: independent agencies "are made up and defended by an unconstitutional, circular logic" -- that is how Humphrey's worked (slide 26).

Collins v. Yellen (2021) refines Seila by reaffirming that single-director agencies like the Federal Housing Finance Agency must be removable at will, but limits Seila's impact by holding that an unconstitutional removal restriction does not automatically invalidate prior agency actions unless plaintiffs show actual harm (slide 28). Biden fired the head of the Social Security Administration without cause notwithstanding the for-cause standard; Trump tried to fire the head of the Office of Special Counsel notwithstanding the for-cause standard, drawing a TRO from the D.D.C. upheld 2-1 by the D.C. Circuit, with an emergency appeal pending at SCOTUS (slide 28).

**IV. Recess Appointments**
The President may fill vacancies during the Senate's recess with commissions that expire at the end of the next session (slide 32). Noel Canning (2014) (Breyer, J., 5-4) held that the recess clause can fill vacancies that "exist" during a recess rather than just those that "occur" during a recess, and that "recess" requires a substantial break with a presumptive floor of 10 days (slides 32, 37). The factual setup: Obama faced congressional gridlock over NLRB appointees and used weekend recess appointments; Pepsi challenged subsequent NLRB action on the ground that 3 members were improperly appointed, leaving no valid quorum, and the Court agreed (slide 36).

**V. Current State of the Doctrine**
The deck states the current rule: under Seila, Congress cannot restrict the President's power to remove an officer unless the officer is (1) part of a multi-headed independent agency with quasi-legislative, quasi-judicial powers (Humphrey's) or (2) an inferior officer (Morrison) (slide 31). Collins clarifies that the key in Seila was the single-vs.-multi-headed distinction, not the significant powers of the CFPB (slide 31). On remedy: if plaintiffs must show the removal restriction directly harmed them to set aside a decision, removal disputes will likely be resigned to a niche form of high-profile employment law (slide 31). The doctrine is fluid -- the current Court is not going to revive Humphrey's, but if they do not kill it off it may come back, and everyone thought Myers was dead for about 90 years until Seila revived it (slide 31).

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

**The FHFA Hypo (Collins Facts).** Congress creates the Federal Housing Finance Agency with a single director removable only for cause; the FHFA administers 1 statute (CFPB administered 19), has little enforcement authority, and operates on significantly less money than the CFPB (slide 29). After the 2008 crash, the FHFA placed Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac into conservatorship under a Plan that required some repayment, and private shareholders challenged the Plan as violating separation of powers (slide 29). Is the FHFA constitutional (slide 29)? Seila Law said the CFPB's single-director structure is the problem—does the FHFA's smaller footprint matter? Collins answers yes, it's unconstitutional, but the shareholders lose on the remedy. Trace that analysis.

**Formalism vs. Functionalism: Which Leads to More Accountability?**
The formalist position holds that clear presidential control over all executive officials ensures democratic accountability—if an agency acts badly, voters know to blame the President. The functionalist position holds that expert insulation from political pressure produces better governance. Evaluate: does the formalist model actually produce accountability, or does it produce political interference in technical agencies? Does the functionalist model produce better governance, or just unaccountable governance?

**Kagan's Rucho Parallel.** Kagan in Seila almost reaches the same place she did in Rucho: the proper balance is for the political branches to determine, and courts should mostly stay out. Is this an unusually consistent jurisprudential position (deference to political processes), or is it outcome-driven (Democrats support independent agencies and redistricting reform)?

**The 2025 Removal Actions.** The Trump administration is firing NLRB and EEOC members. The Acting Solicitor General's letter suggests the administration wants to fully overrule Humphrey's. Walk through the doctrinal analysis. Under current law, NLRB and EEOC members are protected by Humphrey's (multi-member expert agencies). What argument does the government need to win? What does the Court need to do?

## Key Takeaways

The appointment power (Article II) requires Senate confirmation for principal officers and allows Congress to vest inferior officer appointments in the President alone, courts, or department heads. Edmond provides the current test. Buckley enforces the scheme strictly. The removal power tracks a formalist-functionalist arc. Myers established presidential removal authority over purely executive officers. Humphrey's Executor created an exception for multi-member expert agencies with quasi-legislative and quasi-judicial functions—now demoted to a narrow exception by Seila Law. Morrison extended for-cause protection to inferior officers with narrowly defined duties. Free Enterprise Fund prohibited double layers of protection. Seila Law held that single-director independent agencies with broad executive power are unconstitutional—Myers is the rule, Humphrey's and Morrison are exceptions. Collins added that unconstitutional removal restrictions don't automatically void prior agency actions. The doctrine remains fluid; the current Court has not overruled Humphrey's but has signaled it may. The 2025 Trump administration removals are testing where the current Court draws the line.
