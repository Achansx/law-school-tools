## Lecture Outline

### I. Constitutional Baseline: Who Controls Spending?

The Appropriations Clause (Art. I, § 9, cl. 7) vests the spending power in Congress: no money may be drawn from the Treasury except in consequence of appropriations made by law (p. 3). Congress controls spending, but Presidents claim discretion, and impoundment subverts legislative intent (p. 3). The question is whether the President can refuse to spend appropriated funds (p. 3).

### II. Kendall v United States ex rel Stokes (1838)

**Facts:** Amos Kendall, Postmaster General, refused payments, and Congress had passed a law directing payments (p. 4).

**Holding:** Executive officials must follow legislative mandates; there is no inherent executive discretion to impound funds (p. 4).

**Significance:** Established judicial enforcement of congressional spending laws (p. 4). The ministerial/discretionary distinction announced in *Marbury* dicta was applied here to compel performance.

### III. Train v City of New York (1975)

**Facts:** Nixon impounded Clean Water Act funds (p. 5).

**Holding:** The Supreme Court held the President must follow appropriation laws (p. 5). There was no constitutional discussion, but an implied rejection of an impoundment power (p. 5).

**Significance:** Strengthened Congress' control over spending and influenced the creation of the ICA (p. 5).

### IV. The Impoundment Control Act of 1974

Enacted to regulate presidential impoundment, the ICA differentiates rescission (permanent) from deferral (temporary): rescissions require congressional approval; deferrals were originally subject to legislative veto, until Chadha (1983) weakened Congress' control over deferrals (p. 7).

**Comptroller General vulnerability:** The CG is authorized to sue for ICA violations, and lawsuits enforce budgetary obligations; there is no private enforcement -- only the Comptroller General can sue -- and no precedent of the CG actually suing, raising the possibility of presidential resistance by firing the Comptroller General (p. 8). The presidential removal power line of cases (p. 9) frames this concern: Myers v. United States (1926) recognized broad removal power; Humphrey's Executor (1935) imposed limits on removing independent agency heads; Morrison v. Olson (1988) imposed further limits; Free Enterprise Fund v. PCAOB (2010) strengthened executive control; Seila Law LLC v. CFPB (2020) struck down the independent single-director model (p. 9). Seila Law limits constraints on presidential removal; the CG's 15-year term with for-cause removal could be at risk, with the argument running that GAO is legislative rather than executive so removal limits apply -- likely a key issue if impoundment law is challenged (p. 10).

### V. Clinton v City of New York (1998)

**Facts:** The Line Item Veto allowed selective spending cuts (p. 6).

**Holding:** The Supreme Court struck down the line-item veto; the President cannot alter laws after passage (p. 6).

**Significance:** If selective veto is unconstitutional, so is unilateral impoundment; the decision reinforces congressional spending power (p. 6).

## Key Takeaways

- The constitutional baseline is that the President must spend appropriated funds for their designated purposes. Impoundment = unlawful unless Congress consents.
- *Kendall* (1838): ministerial duties can be judicially compelled; the ministerial/discretionary distinction is essential to executive power analysis.
- *Train* (1975): direct impoundment of specifically appropriated funds is unlawful. The Clean Water Act gave the President no discretion to allot less than Congress appropriated.
- The ICA (1974) creates lawful channels for presidential spending flexibility (deferrals subject to override; rescissions requiring approval) -- but the baseline is still that unapproved withholding is illegal.
- *Clinton* (1998): Congress cannot circumvent the baseline by delegating cancellation authority to the President. Once money is appropriated by enacted law, only another enacted law can cancel it.
- Comptroller General removal: Congress cannot vest executive sequestration power in an officer subject to congressional removal (*Bowsher*). Separation of powers is a two-way street.
- The modern relevance: executive impoundment re-emerged as a live controversy in 2025. The ICA framework and *Train* remain the primary legal constraints.
