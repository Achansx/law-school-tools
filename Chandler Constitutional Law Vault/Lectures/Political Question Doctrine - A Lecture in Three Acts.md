## Lecture Outline

**I. Act One — Where the Doctrine Came From**

*Luther v. Borden (1849): The Foundational Case*
The Dorr Rebellion arose because Rhode Island operated under a colonial charter granted by Charles II, which restricted voting to whites with $134 in property -- about 60% of the population was disenfranchised in a state of 108,000 with only 8,000 voting *(slide 3)*. Dorr organized an alternative election, two governments emerged, and Dorr marched a cannon to the arsenal where it misfired; Charterite forces broke into Luther's house, producing a trespass case *(slide 3)*. Chief Justice Taney warned: if the charter government had no legal existence, then "the laws passed by its legislature during that time were nullities, its taxes wrongfully collected, its salaries and compensation to its officers illegally paid … and the officers who carried their decisions into operation answerable as trespassers, if not, in some cases, as criminals" *(slide 4)*. This is the birth of the doctrine: some questions, if answered wrong, could blow up the foundations of governance, and the Guarantee Clause commits these determinations to Congress *(slide 4)*.

*Colegrove v. Green (1946): The Political Thicket*
Frankfurter's plurality refused to intervene: "Courts ought not to enter this political thicket," because the Constitution "has conferred upon Congress exclusive authority to secure fair representation by the States in the popular House" *(slide 6)*. The deck identifies the structural problem: Frankfurter told citizens to seek a remedy from the very body that benefited from the malady, and only 26 states needed to block reform in Congress -- a recipe for permanent entrenchment *(slide 6)*.

*Baker v. Carr (1962): The Reformation*
Tennessee had not redistricted in 60 years, producing a massive violation of the equality constraint; under Colegrove this should have been a political question *(slide 8)*. Brennan performed a "Cardozian refitting" of all prior cases, extracting six meta-variables -- the Baker factors -- and finding none present, because numerosity is an easy standard *(slide 8)*. Warren called it "the most important case my Court ever decided" *(slide 8)*. The six Baker factors are: (1) textually demonstrable commitment to a coordinate political department; (2) lack of judicially discoverable and manageable standards; (3) impossibility of deciding without an initial policy determination for nonjudicial discretion; (4) impossibility of independent resolution without expressing lack of respect for coordinate branches; (5) unusual need for unquestioning adherence to a political decision already made; (6) potentiality of embarrassment from multifarious pronouncements *(slide 9)*. Frankfurter's dissent: "The crux of the matter is that courts are not fit instruments of decision where what is at stake is the composition of those large contexts of policy traditionally fought out in nonjudicial forums, by which governments and the actions of governments are made and unmade" *(slide 10)*. You cannot escape the principle by rebadging Guaranty Clause claims as Equal Protection claims *(slide 10)*. The deck poses Frankfurter's lasting question: can you dislike Bush v. Gore without also disliking Baker v. Carr? *(slide 10)*

*Nixon v. United States (1993): The Senate's Sole Power*
Judge Walter Nixon was convicted of perjury and impeached by the Senate, which used a committee rather than the full body to hear evidence *(slide 11)*. The Senate's "Sole Power to try all Impeachments" rendered the question unreviewable per Rehnquist; "Try" was too imprecise for manageable standards *(slide 11)*. The deck adds the structural concern that judiciary reviewing its own impeachment = institutional self-interest *(slide 11)*. White would have found the case justiciable but meritless; Souter refused a blanket rule, leaving the door open for outrageous procedures *(slide 11)*.

**II. Act Two — The Factors Applied and Dismantled**

*The Powell-Nixon Problem*
Powell v. McCormack (1969): The Constitution gives the House power to "judge" the "Qualifications" of its members; the Court read "Qualifications" narrowly to mean only age, citizenship, and residency, so the House exceeded its scope and the case was justiciable *(slide 14)*. Context: a Black congressman was excluded with racial overtones, and the Warren Court was not walking away *(slide 14)*. Nixon v. United States (1993): "Sole Power to try all Impeachments" -- "Sole" = unreviewable; "Try" = too imprecise; context was a judge convicted of perjury, and the Rehnquist Court was not touching it *(slide 14)*. Justice White's observation: "Try" is no more ambiguous than "Qualifications" -- "The doctrine bent to accommodate the result. Both times" *(slide 14)*.

*Rucho v. Common Cause (2019): Partisan Gerrymandering*
The NC redistricting chair designed the map for 10R-3D and would have done 11-2 if he could; MD Democrats gerrymandered the 6th District to flip it from R to D, and both maps were challenged as unconstitutional partisan gerrymanders *(slide 15)*. Roberts's 5-4 majority: some partisanship in districting is constitutionally permissible; how much is too much has no constitutional answer; there is no constitutional right to proportional representation; there is no baseline from which to measure deviation; partisan gerrymandering is a political question beyond federal courts *(slide 15)*. The Roberts-Kagan exchange: Kagan would use the state's own neutral criteria as a baseline, generate thousands of simulated maps by computer, treat the enacted map as a massive statistical outlier, and refuse to draw a precise line -- just say NC is on the wrong side of any reasonable line, because law is full of judgments of degree (antitrust, criminal law) *(slide 16)*. Roberts replied that Kagan proposed a methodology, not a standard; which metric (efficiency gap, mean-median)? What threshold (why 99% and not 95%)? -- the choice still requires irreducibly political judgments, and could the same logic have rejected one person, one vote? *(slide 16)*. The deck's editorial closer: "Equal Protection yielded one person, one vote in 1964 but yields nothing for partisan fairness in 2019. The text hasn't changed. The Court's composition has" *(slide 16)*.

*After Rucho: Real Consequences and Remaining Avenues*
Partisan gerrymandering contributes directly to political polarization because safe seats reward extremism, and it can veil racial gerrymandering because packing minority voters looks like partisan sorting *(slide 17)*. Remaining avenues: state constitutions (free-election clauses, state equal protection -- PA, NC, OH have struck down gerrymanders; Rucho doesn't touch state claims); racial/VRA claims (Shaw v. Reno still provides a cause of action, though proving race over partisanship is harder post-Rucho -- see Callais v. Louisiana pending 2026); and congressional action under Art. I, § 4 (Congress required compactness in 1911 and could ban partisan gerrymandering by statute tomorrow) *(slide 17)*. Frankfurter's structural problem returns: the beneficiaries of gerrymandering would have to vote to end it *(slide 17)*.

*The Structural Critique Synthesized*
"Textual commitment": broad in Nixon ("sole" = unreviewable), narrow in Powell ("Qualifications" = enumerated list) *(slide 18)*. "Manageable standards": found in 1964 (one person, one vote), not found in 2019 (partisan fairness) *(slide 18)*. "Respect for branches": interpreting a statute is not disrespectful (Zivotofsky); questioning a bombing is disrespectful (El-Shifa) *(slide 18)*. "Embarrassment": competing policy pronouncement, or authoritative legal resolution? *(slide 18)*. The characterization does the work; the factor provides the label; each factor functions not as a constraint on judicial discretion but as a resource for its exercise *(slide 18)*.

**III. Act Three — The Directional Asymmetry Problem**

*Zivotofsky I and II*
Zivotofsky I (2012) held the case justiciable -- determining the constitutionality of a statute is "what courts do" and no political question is presented *(slide 20)*. Zivotofsky II (2015) held that the President's recognition power is exclusive, § 214(d) is unconstitutional, and the President wins -- preserving "one voice" on Jerusalem *(slide 20)*. The deck flags the counterfactual directly: if Congress had won, the U.S. would have had contradictory statements on Jerusalem -- the textbook Factor 6 scenario *(slide 20)*.

*The Impossible Trilemma*
Option 1: Take potentiality seriously ex ante -- but this immunizes vast swaths of executive action from scrutiny *(slide 21)*. Option 2: Ignore potentiality and decide on the merits -- formally correct but only "worked" in Zivotofsky because the merits came out the "safe" direction *(slide 21)*. Option 3: Peek at the merits sub silentio -- doctrinally forbidden but may be what actually happens; the Court takes the case only when it is confident the merits will break the right way *(slide 21)*. Breyer's dissent in Zivotofsky I (invoking Learned Hand) argued the political branches have nonjudicial methods of working out their differences -- budget authorizations, personnel confirmations, hearings, committee contacts, and "a host of more informal contacts" *(slide 22)*. Breyer lost; the majority took the case; the bet on the merits paid off; but should justiciability depend on which way the merits break? *(slide 22)*

*Conclusion: Double Consciousness*
Read the six factors as what the Court says it applies; then read them as the vocabulary through which the Court rationalizes outcomes driven by other considerations *(slide 23)*. The doctrine is real; so is the gap between the doctrine as stated and the doctrine as practiced *(slide 23)*.

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

**The Powell-Nixon Contrast.** White says "Try" is no more ambiguous than "Qualifications." Is he right? Or is there a principled distinction—perhaps that "Qualifications" is defined in the text (age, citizenship, residency), giving the Court a limiting principle, while "Try" is an open-ended standard that invites judicial supervision of every procedural choice the Senate makes? Is the difference principled or pretextual?

**The Kagan-Roberts Exchange.** Kagan said she could identify maps that cross any reasonable line without drawing the precise line. Roberts said she proposed a methodology, not a standard. Is there a principled distinction between a methodology and a standard? If the Court can apply the "reasonable person" standard in tort without deciding exactly where reasonableness ends, why can't it apply a "reasonable map" standard in redistricting?

**Factor 6 and Sub Silentio Review.** If Chandler is right that the Court sometimes peeks at the merits to decide whether to take a case on Factor 6 grounds, what follows? Is this kind of covert result-orientation defensible—a pragmatic tool for managing the Court's docket—or does it undermine the rule of law by making justiciability secretly depend on the merits? Is it better for the Court to be openly opportunistic or covertly so?

**Frankfurter's Question.** Can you dislike Bush v. Gore without also disliking Baker v. Carr? Both involved courts imposing their resolution on what Frankfurter would call a "nonjudicial" contest over political power. Is there a principled distinction—perhaps that one person, one vote is an objective standard while winner-selection in a contested election is not? Or does Baker make Bush v. Gore possible in a way that cannot be distinguished?

## Key Takeaways

The political question doctrine was born in Luther v. Borden—the Court refused to determine which Rhode Island government was legitimate because the wrong answer could void all governmental acts. Baker v. Carr extracted six factors from prior cases and found none present in a reapportionment challenge where the claimed right was numerical equality. But the factors are elastic: each can be read broadly or narrowly to produce the result the Court prefers. The Powell-Nixon contrast is the strongest evidence: identical textual-commitment and manageable-standards analysis, opposite results, driven by different contexts and different incentives. Rucho shows that the "manageable standards" factor turns not on whether courts can weigh evidence but on whether courts will accept an irreducibly political judgment as law. Factor 6 creates a logical trilemma that the Court resolves through covert merits-peeking in cases like Zivotofsky. The prescription of "double consciousness"—reading the doctrine both as stated and as practiced—is Chandler's summary of what this material teaches about the gap between law on the books and law in action.

## Connections

- **Baker v. Carr** → directly produced Reynolds v. Sims (1964) (one-person-one-vote for state legislatures); the "Cardozian refitting" method echoes Cardozo's use of case-law synthesis in *The Nature of the Judicial Process*
- **Rucho and state constitutions** → Pennsylvania Supreme Court in *League of Women Voters v. Commonwealth* (2018) struck down gerrymandering under state constitution; North Carolina Supreme Court oscillated on the question depending on court composition
- **Rucho and racial gerrymandering** → Shaw v. Reno (1993) and Cooper v. Harris (2017) show racial gerrymandering claims survive; Callais v. Louisiana (pending 2026) may reshape the racial-vs-partisan distinction
- **Factor 6 and foreign affairs** → El-Shifa Pharmaceutical v. United States (D.C. Cir.) held that questioning a military bombing target is nonjusticiable; cf. Hamdi v. Rumsfeld where the Court did review enemy combatant detention
- **Zivotofsky I and II** → discussed in separation-of-powers context; President's exclusive recognition power confirms strong unitary executive theory in foreign affairs; compare Morrison v. Olson and Seila Law in domestic administration
