## Lecture Outline

**Text, History, and the Framing Generation**

The Clause -- "No State shall... pass any... Law impairing the Obligation of Contracts" -- applies only to state governments, not the federal government; covers both public grants and private contracts; is retrospective only, applying only to existing contracts; and was once the most-litigated clause in the Constitution but is now largely dormant *(slide 2)*. The Framers included it because colonial and state legislatures had enacted debtor-relief laws that devalued contracts, paper money emissions and tender laws had undermined creditor confidence, Madison described such laws as "contrary to the first principles of the social compact" (Federalist 44), and Hamilton warned contract violations could become a source of hostility among states (Federalist 7) *(slide 3)*. The Clause was barely discussed at the Convention, added by the Committee of Style, and the original draft barred "altering or impairing" contracts -- the "altering" was dropped *(slide 3)*. Early courts treated the Clause as enshrining a natural, inherent right -- Justice Paterson (1795) called the right to property "natural, inherent, and unalienable," and the Marshall Court converted a structural limitation on state power into a source of individual rights, presaging Lochner's freedom of contract *(slide 4)*. The deck poses the framing question: is the Clause fundamentally about federalism (limiting states) or about individual economic liberty? *(slide 4)*.

**The Marshall Court Golden Age**

*Fletcher v. Peck* (1810) was the first time the Supreme Court invalidated a state law under any provision of the Constitution: Georgia sold the Yazoo public lands, then rescinded the grants due to bribery allegations; Marshall held the grant was an executed contract and the Clause barred rescission, with innocent third-party purchasers protected even if the original grant was tainted by fraud *(slide 5)*. *Dartmouth College* (1819): New Hampshire tried to make a private college public by altering its royal charter, but Marshall held the charter was a contract the state could not unilaterally alter, effectively interpreting "impair" to include "alter" despite the drafting history *(slide 6)*. *Ogden v. Saunders* (1827) limited the Clause to retrospective operation: prospective bankruptcy laws applying to contracts formed after enactment do not violate it, with Marshall dissenting that the Clause "admits of a prospective, as well as of a retrospective operation" *(slide 6)*. The pre-1889 Golden Age numbers: approximately 40% of all challenges to state legislation invoked the Clause, about 50% of successful challenges relied on it, and roughly 75 decisions invalidated state laws under it *(slide 7)*. The deck's footnote: the Taney Court began limiting the Clause, but it remained potent through the Gilded Age *(slide 7)*.

**The Lochner Connection**

The Contract Clause was never invoked in Lochner because Ogden limited it to retrospective claims, but the natural-rights language was nearly identical -- "freedom of contract" as an individual right *(slide 8)*. The Clause protected existing contracts from retrospective impairment; Lochner protected the right to make future contracts; two sides of the same coin, both grounded in laissez-faire economic liberty *(slide 8)*. Both doctrines would fall to the same force: expanded deference to state police power *(slide 8)*.

**Blaisdell (1934) and the Great Turning Point**

*Home Building & Loan v. Blaisdell* upheld Minnesota's Depression-era Mortgage Moratorium Act extending the redemption period after foreclosure, 5-4, holding that the Contract Clause is not absolute and must be harmonized with the state police power *(slide 10)*. Hughes' formulation: "While emergency does not create power, emergency may furnish the occasion for the exercise of power," and the reservation of state sovereign power is "read into all contracts as a postulate of the legal order" *(slide 10)*. The Blaisdell five criteria: adequate basis, legitimate end, appropriate relation to emergency, reasonableness, and temporariness *(slide 10)*. Sutherland's dissent: the Constitution "does not mean one thing at one time and an entirely different thing at another time" *(slide 10)*. The Parallel Decline slide pairs Blaisdell with West Coast Hotel v. Parrish (1937), both relying on state police power to overcome economic-rights claims, with the Sunstein observation that Blaisdell "replicated the outcome in West Coast Hotel, rendering the contracts clause functionally identical to the due process clause" *(slide 11)*. After 1937 the Clause entered a long dormancy *(slide 11)*.

**Modern Framework**

The modern two-step *(slide 12)*. Step 1 (substantial impairment): does the law undermine the contractual bargain, interfere with reasonable expectations, or prevent safeguarding or reinstating rights? If minimal, the inquiry ends under Sveen v. Melin (2018). Step 2 (reasonable and appropriate): is there a significant and legitimate public purpose, are the means drawn in a reasonable way, and are they appropriate to the purpose? *(slide 12)*. The key principle: severity of impairment sets the height of the hurdle, drawing Sveen and Energy Reserves Group v. Kansas Power & Light (1983) *(slide 12)*.

The deck flags a critical distinction *(slide 13)*: impairment of private contracts gets reasonableness review with customary deference (heavily regulated industries get less protection, and most impairments survive), while impairment of the state's own contracts triggers heightened scrutiny because the state's self-interest is at stake -- "if a State could reduce its financial obligations whenever it wanted... the Contract Clause would provide no protection at all" -- requiring both reasonableness and necessity *(slide 13)*. *U.S. Trust Co. v. New Jersey* (1977): NJ and NY repealed a 1962 covenant that limited the Port Authority's ability to subsidize rail transit; the repeal was neither reasonable nor necessary; heightened scrutiny applied because the state was impairing its own financial obligation, and "complete deference to a legislative assessment of reasonableness is not appropriate because the State's self-interest is at stake" *(slide 14)*. The reserved-powers doctrine: a state cannot "bargain away" its police power, but can bind its taxing and spending powers *(slide 14)*. *Allied Structural Steel v. Spannaus* (1978): Minnesota's retroactive pension obligations on companies closing offices were sudden, unanticipated, and imposed a substantial new obligation in a previously unregulated area; the deck observes "the Contract Clause is not a dead letter" but flags that this was the last time the Supreme Court struck down a state law under the Clause *(slide 15)*. The "how weakened" timeline puts zero Supreme Court invalidations between 1978 and the present, though scholars calling it "a dead letter" coexist with the text remaining in the Constitution *(slide 16)*.

**COVID-19 and Dormant Revival**

The pandemic produced widespread state impairments -- eviction moratoria, rent relief, vaccine mandates, rehiring ordinances -- and most Contract Clause challenges failed under the permissive Blaisdell standard *(slide 17)*. The deck poses the question "When would the impairment of a mere contract outweigh the public purpose in combatting a pandemic? Could it ever?" *(slide 17)*. The Ninth Circuit (2021) found that "the challenges that COVID-19 presents" almost automatically satisfy the public purpose requirement, declining to second-guess state expertise *(slide 17)*. *Melendez v. City of New York* (2d Cir. 2021) reversed dismissal of a challenge to NY's Guaranty Law that permanently voided commercial lease guaranties: not temporary, not conditioned on need, not narrowly crafted, no compensation for landlords; the dissent warned of "Lochnerian overtone" and a "backdoor to Lochner-type jurisprudence" *(slide 18)*. *Heights Apartments v. Walz* (8th Cir. 2022) reversed Minnesota's eviction moratorium because its indefinite end date and non-pandemic-related blocks on eviction strained reasonability *(slide 18)*. The takeaway: the Clause is "dormant but not dead," even during an unprecedented emergency *(slide 18)*. The § 1983 question is whether 42 U.S.C. § 1983 furnishes a private cause of action for Contract Clause violations *(slide 19)*. *Carter v. Greenhow* (1885) said the Clause confers rights only "indirectly and incidentally"; *Dennis v. Higgins* (1991) cast doubt on Carter in a footnote; the Ninth Circuit (S. Cal. Gas Co.) allows it; the 4th and 6th (Crosby, Kaminski) do not *(slide 19)*. Both successful COVID cases arose through § 1983, making the split's resolution consequential *(slide 19)*.

**The Absent Federal Contracts Clause**

Article I, § 10 limits only state governments; Elbridge Gerry proposed extending the prohibition to the federal government but no one seconded the motion *(slide 22)*. Courts use the Fifth Amendment Due Process Clause as a substitute under Lynch's "valid contracts are property" framing *(slide 22)*. The deck observes evolution runs in reverse: Article I, § 10 went from specific to general; the federal substitute goes from general due process to specific contract protection *(slide 22)*. The *Sinking Fund Cases* (1878) held Congress can impose new financial obligations on railroads post-contract but cannot "arbitrarily impair" contracts, observing the federal government is "not included within the constitutional prohibition which prevents States from passing laws impairing the obligation of contracts" *(slide 23)*. *Lynch v. United States* (1934) held that War Risk Insurance policies are "property" Congress cannot abrogate merely to save money: "Punctilious fulfillment of contractual obligations is essential to the maintenance of the credit of public as well as private debtors"; the case distinguished contracts (vested rights) from gratuities like pensions, which Congress can freely modify *(slide 23)*. *Winstar* (1996) held the government must honor express accounting promises made to S&Ls before FIRREA changed the rules *(slide 24)*. *Eastern Enterprises* (1998) produced a plurality finding unconstitutional taking and a Kennedy separate opinion finding substantive due process violation in response to retroactive coal company health-benefit obligations *(slide 24)*. *Maine Community Health Options* (2020) held the government must honor express statutory commitments to ACA insurers despite appropriations riders that blocked funding *(slide 24)*. The deck closes with the ongoing tension between contractual stability and government flexibility, observing that the Clause remains "absolute in its field" as constitutional text and that "reinvigoration may be near" *(slide 25)*.

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

**COVID Moratorium Analysis:** The deck poses the COVID question directly: "When would the impairment of a mere contract outweigh the public purpose in combatting a pandemic? Could it ever?" *(slide 17)*. The Ninth Circuit in 2021 effectively held "never," while the Second Circuit (Melendez) and Eighth Circuit (Walz) found limits in permanence and indefinite duration *(slide 18)*. This page reads the cases as showing severity and temporariness remain live at step two even under strong deference.

**State vs. Its Own Contracts:** The Port Authority hypo from U.S. Trust illustrates the heightened-scrutiny rule -- the state can bind its taxing and spending powers but cannot bargain away its police power *(slide 14)*. The repeal of the 1962 covenant was neither reasonable nor necessary; less drastic alternatives existed; the need for mass transit was foreseeable when the covenant was made *(slide 14)*.

## Key Takeaways

- The Clause was the dominant vehicle for constitutional review of state legislation before the 14th Amendment; it declined with Blaisdell (1934) and the parallel fall of Lochner.
- Modern doctrine uses a two-step test -- substantial impairment, then reasonable and appropriate -- with severity of impairment scaling the second-step scrutiny.
- Impairment of the state's own contracts triggers heightened scrutiny because legislative self-interest undermines the usual deference rationale.
- The Court has not struck down a state law under the Clause since Allied Structural Steel in 1978, but lower courts have found violations in pandemic contexts, suggesting a dormant revival.
- The federal government lacks a parallel Contracts Clause constraint; Fifth Amendment due process serves as a weaker substitute (Lynch, Winstar, Maine Community Health Options).
