## Lecture Outline

### Calder v Bull (1798): The Founding Debate

The ur-dispute over unenumerated constitutional limits. Justice Chase argued that some things are so outrageous -- taking property from A and giving it to B -- that "it cannot be presumed" the legislature was so empowered *(slide 2)*. Justice Iredell countered that "the ideas of natural justice are regulated by no fixed standard" and that courts must enforce the text, nothing more *(slide 2)*. Every substantive due process case since Calder replays the divide -- the deck makes this the organizing claim of the recap *(slides 1, 5)*.

### Lochner v New York (1905): Liberty of Contract

The Court held that liberty of contract is in the Fourteenth Amendment and that the Court will decide for itself whether baking is dangerous enough to regulate *(slide 2)*. The deck reads the real story as an anti-immigrant provision dressed up as health regulation: established bakers did not want hard-working immigrants undercutting them *(slide 2)*.

Justice Holmes's dissent: "The Constitution is not intended to embody a particular economic theory." A law is constitutional unless no rational person would think it consistent with our traditions *(slide 2)*. Holmes sides with Iredell.

**The Same-Term Paradox.** Jacobson (February 1905) upheld compulsory vaccination -- a bodily intrusion far more invasive than limiting work hours -- while Lochner (April 1905) struck down the bakery-hours law; same Court, same Term, opposite results, valuing economic liberty more than bodily autonomy *(slide 2)*.

### West Coast Hotel v Parrish (1937): The Switch in Time

Hughes's majority adopts the Iredell position: "The Constitution does not speak of freedom of contract"; liberty of contract was never in the text; reasonable regulation adopted in the community's interest *is* due process *(slide 3)*.

Justice Sutherland's dissent: "The meaning of the Constitution does not change with the ebb and flow of economic events"; Roberts, you took the oath -- and now you've changed your mind? *(slide 3)*.

### United States v Carolene Products (1938): Footnote 4

The deck distills Footnote 4 to three sentences *(slide 3)*. Sentence one: laws touching the Bill of Rights get less deference -- the error costs of getting free speech or search-and-seizure wrong are too high *(slide 3)*. Sentence two: laws that clog the political process get heightened scrutiny -- the Darwinian self-correction of democracy can't work if the process itself is rigged *(slide 3)*. Sentence three: laws targeting discrete and insular minorities -- groups too small and too hated to protect themselves through ordinary politics *(slide 3)*.

The framework: rational basis for ordinary commercial legislation ("unless it is completely bonkers"), heightened scrutiny for everything else; the deck notes that "an awful lot of 14th Amendment law flows from this single footnote" *(slide 3)*.

### Williamson v Lee Optical (1955): Rational Basis as Near-Impossibility

Oklahoma makes it a crime for an optician to fit eyeglasses; one needs an optometrist *(slide 4)*. The deck's read of the real motivation: optometrists protecting their turf via campaign contributions *(slide 4)*. Justice Douglas's move: the Court hypothesizes reasons the legislature never stated (periodic eye exams catch glaucoma); the justices themselves invent the justification *(slide 4)*. The deck calls this the high watermark of rational basis -- almost impossible to fail *(slide 4)*.

### BMW of North America v Gore (1996): Lochner Rises

BMW repaints a scratched car and doesn't disclose it; an Alabama jury awards $4 million in punitive damages on a $4,000 injury *(slide 4)*. The deck's gloss: "Lochner comes out of the grave!" *(slide 4)*. There was no process flaw -- the Court just thinks something must have gone wrong for this result to occur *(slide 4)*. The Court's three guideposts: egregiousness, ratio (single digits per *State Farm*), and comparable civil penalties *(slide 4)*.

The deck's question: if Lochner was wrong because unelected judges shouldn't second-guess economic policy, what are the BMW justices doing -- if not exactly that? *(slide 4)*. This page reads BMW as Chase over Iredell -- substantive second-guessing of a legislative-scale economic judgment made by a jury, vulnerable to the same critique that killed economic SDP.

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

**The Same-Term Paradox.** Jacobson and Lochner were decided in the same Term by the same Court; Jacobson upheld compulsory vaccination as a reasonable police-power exercise while Lochner struck down a sixty-hour workweek limit *(slide 2)*. This page reads the juxtaposition as showing that Lochner was not neutral: it made a normative judgment about which liberty matters more, a ranking modern SDP (bodily autonomy, intimate association) has inverted.

**BMW as Lochner in a New Suit.** If the anti-Lochner principle is that unelected judges should not second-guess economic policy judgments, is BMW defensible? *(slide 4)*. The deck notes that there was no process flaw -- the Court just thinks something must have gone wrong for this result to occur *(slide 4)*. This page extends that into the editor's claim that the reasoning is structurally identical to Lochner's independent review of whether sixty hours of baking was actually dangerous.

## Key Takeaways

- The Chase-Iredell divide is permanent: every SDP case asks whether courts may impose unwritten limits or must defer to the text and the legislature. West Coast Hotel sides with Iredell on economic regulation; BMW quietly sides with Chase on punitive damages.
- Footnote 4 of Carolene Products is the operating system of modern constitutional law: rational basis for ordinary legislation; heightened scrutiny for Bill of Rights violations, political-process distortions, and laws targeting discrete and insular minorities.
- Williamson sets the floor for rational basis: courts hypothesize conceivable legitimate purposes and will uphold a law unless it is wholly irrational.
- BMW v Gore demonstrates that economic SDP was buried rather than destroyed -- the punitive-damages line of cases imposes substantive judicial limits on civil judgments without formal reliance on Lochner's liberty-of-contract framework.
