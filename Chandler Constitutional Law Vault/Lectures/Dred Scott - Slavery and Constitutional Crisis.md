# Dred Scott - Slavery and Constitutional Crisis

## Professor Emphasis

### From source

The deck's closing warning is unambiguous: "Be very careful citing Dred Scott for any argument," and "You can not cite Dred Scott in any positive way" *(slides 31, 34)*. The deck closes by asking whether criticism of Taney is just "a convenient but polite way of criticizing the founders," or whether Taney and his majority did "an independent wrong" by deciding more than necessary -- a question framed as a lesson about "confident judges deciding more than they need to" *(slide 34)*. The lecture situates the decision in the full political and legislative history of slavery's expansion, tracing the Louisiana Purchase, the 1807 Importation Act, Northern abolition, the Missouri Compromise, the 1850 Compromise, and the Kansas-Nebraska Act *(slides 2-19)*. The deck also flags that Dred Scott contains dicta reaching modern doctrinal areas -- personhood vs. citizenship, negative and positive rights, Native American law, immigration, miscegenation (Loving), Insular Cases, and the Second Amendment *(slide 31)*.

### Editor's framing

This page treats Dred Scott as the canonical example of a court reaching further than necessary, with disastrous results. The packaging of the territorial-history slides as a single arc -- showing how constitutional law tracked but failed to resolve the underlying political crisis -- is editorial synthesis of the deck's chronological treatment. The connection between Taney's Fifth Amendment territorial holding and modern substantive due process doctrine is doctrinal gloss beyond the deck.

## Lecture Outline

**Historical Background: Slavery's Territorial Spread**

The lecture opens with the Louisiana Purchase (1803) and Article III of the treaty, which promised inhabitants the rights, advantages, and immunities of United States citizens -- a promise that evidently did not extend to slaves *(slide 3)*. The deck traces Northern abolition state by state -- Vermont 1777, Pennsylvania 1780, Massachusetts 1780, New Hampshire/Rhode Island/Connecticut 1783, New York 1799-1827, Ohio 1802, New Jersey 1804, Indiana 1820 -- against the simultaneous expansion of slavery into Southern and Western territories *(slide 5)*. The 1807 Act Prohibiting Importation of Slaves enters the chronology here *(slide 4)*.

The Missouri Compromise (1820) drew the 36 degrees 30 minutes line, prohibiting slavery north of that line in the Louisiana Purchase territory but expressly excluding Texas, Utah, New Mexico, and later acquisitions; the deck asks where Congress gets the power to restrict slavery in the territories at all *(slide 7)*. Missouri's constitution expressly required the new legislature to pass laws preventing free Blacks and "mulattos" from coming to and settling in the State, illustrating how formal accommodations of slavery were layered with state-level racial exclusion *(slide 8)*. The Texas Republic Constitution (1836) went further -- denying Congress (of the Republic) any power to emancipate, barring free persons of African descent from permanent residence, and locking in slavery for those already enslaved *(slide 9)*.

The 1850 Compromise admitted California as a free state and organized the Utah and New Mexico territories on the popular-sovereignty template, while Texas shrank in exchange for federal assumption of its debts *(slide 10)*. The Kansas-Nebraska Act (1854) then effectively repealed the Missouri Compromise line by declaring it inconsistent with the principle of non-intervention by Congress with slaves in the States and Territories, leaving the people to form their domestic institutions for themselves *(slides 13, 17)*. Combined with the popular-sovereignty principle, the result was "Bleeding Kansas" and the political chaos that set the stage for Dred Scott *(slides 18-19)*.

**The Litigation**

Dred Scott was born in Virginia in 1799 and in 1830 was sold to Army surgeon John Emerson, who took him to Fort Armstrong, Illinois (free state) from 1834 to 1836, then to Fort Snelling in Wisconsin Territory (free under the Missouri Compromise) from 1836 to 1840 *(slide 20)*. Scott met and married Harriet Robinson in Wisconsin *(slide 20)*. After Emerson died in 1843 leaving a life estate in Dred and Harriet to his wife, the Scotts sued for freedom in Missouri state court in 1846 -- one of several hundred such suits filed, roughly one third successful *(slide 20)*.

The Missouri Supreme Court rejected the freedom claim *(slide 21)*. The majority reasoned that the laws of the United States enacted for governing a territory had no force in the States and that comity to other states' laws was a matter of state discretion *(slides 22-23)*. The opinion invoked the political climate explicitly -- "Times are not now as they were" -- and the deck notes that the political composition of the Missouri Supreme Court had become much more pro-slavery *(slide 25)*. The deck flags the majority's concluding "providence of God" rationalization of slavery and the American Colonization Society as the doctrinal-rhetorical context Taney would later inherit *(slide 26)*. Scott then filed in federal court based on diversity; the Supreme Court could have decided on res judicata but did not *(slide 27)*.

**Taney's Opinion**

Taney could have resolved the case on res judicata grounds but chose instead to reach both the citizenship and the Missouri Compromise questions *(slide 27)*. His principal holding was that persons of African descent whose ancestors were brought to the United States as slaves could never be citizens of the United States, and therefore could not invoke diversity jurisdiction. Taney's motivating concern, foregrounded by the deck, was the consequence for Southern states if African-descent persons could be citizens -- triggering Privileges and Immunities Clause and Full Faith and Credit Clause obligations *(slide 28)*. The deck quotes Taney's fear that Black citizenship would let recognized citizens enter every other State without pass or passport, sojourn there, speak publicly on all subjects, hold political meetings, and keep and carry arms *(slide 29)*.

Taney's originalism was explicit. The deck reproduces his rule of construction: no change in public opinion or feeling should induce the court to give the Constitution a more liberal construction than the framers intended; while it remains unaltered it must be construed now as it was understood at adoption; any other rule would make the Court "the mere reflex of the popular opinion or passion of the day" *(slide 30)*. The deck labels this "the amendment theme" *(slide 30)*. Beyond citizenship, Taney declared the Missouri Compromise unconstitutional on the ground that Congress lacked power to prohibit slavery in the territories, treating enslaved persons as property protected by the Fifth Amendment Due Process Clause -- an early and consequential use of substantive due process.

**Curtis Dissent**

Justice Curtis was so unhappy with the decision that he resigned from the Court *(slide 32)*. The deck frames his dissent as proof by counterexample: free Africans had been treated as citizens by at least five states at the founding, which under Taney's theory could not be true *(slide 32)*. The fact that free Negroes might lack every right did not disable them from being citizens -- women and children also lacked full rights *(slide 32)*. Curtis also argued that the framers' acquiescence to slavery did not make them hypocrites and that the Missouri Compromise's constitutionality should not have been reached *(slide 32)*. The deck offers a number-theoretic analogy for proof by counterexample: the conjecture that every odd number is prime or the sum of a prime and twice a square holds until 5777 *(slide 32)*.

**Aftermath and Significance**

Dred Scott was overturned legislatively and constitutionally: Congress abolished slavery in the territories by the Act of June 19, 1862; the Emancipation Proclamation ended slavery in parts of the South under rebel control; and the Thirteenth and Fourteenth Amendments completed the constitutional transformation *(slide 33)*. The decision is generally considered the worst the Supreme Court ever rendered, and the deck asks whether criticism of Taney is just shooting the messenger -- or "a convenient but polite way of criticizing the founders" -- versus whether Taney and the majority did "an independent wrong" *(slide 34)*. The deck closes with the warning that confident judges deciding more than they need to is exactly the failure Dred Scott illustrates *(slide 34)*.

The lecture notes that Taney's opinion contains dicta reaching many modern doctrinal areas: personhood vs. American citizenship, negative and positive rights, Native American law, state citizenship, contemporary immigration debates, miscegenation as a badge of slavery (connecting to [[Cases/Loving v Virginia (1967)|Loving v Virginia]]), rights of persons in territories (Insular Cases), and Second Amendment comments *(slide 31)*.

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

The lecture poses the meta-question throughout: was excoriation of Taney and Dred Scott a convenient but polite way of criticizing the founders *(slide 34)*? This page reads the deck as making the case that the wrong may have been in the original text, not in Taney's interpretation of it -- an editor's gloss on the deck's open-ended question.

The lecture also raises the question of Curtis's resignation as a data point: so unhappy with the decision that he quit the Court *(slide 32)*. Students are invited to evaluate whether that response reflects a failure of the legal process, a failure of the political process, or both.

The slide deck implicitly raises a broader question about what it means to take an "originalist" approach to a constitution ratified under conditions of profound moral compromise.

## Key Takeaways

- Dred Scott is the prime example of a court deciding more than necessary, using a minor jurisdictional dispute as the vehicle to constitutionalize the entire slavery question.
- Taney's originalism was self-consciously deployed to freeze the constitutional status of African Americans at the founding consensus, explicitly refusing to evolve doctrine as public opinion shifted.
- The citizenship holding rested on Privileges and Immunities concerns: recognizing Black citizenship would have obligated all states to extend full civil and legal rights under the P&I Clause.
- The Missouri Compromise holding treated enslaved persons as property protected by the Fifth Amendment Due Process Clause, an early and powerful use of substantive due process in a deeply wrong direction.
- Curtis's "proof by counterexample" methodology remains a model for refuting historical claims: if the principle were true, these documented counter-instances would be impossible.
- The decision was cured by three constitutional amendments and is today cited only to illustrate judicial failure.
