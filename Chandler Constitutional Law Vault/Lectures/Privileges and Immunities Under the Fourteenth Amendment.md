## Professor Emphasis

### From source

The deck labels itself "The One Slide": "Slaughterhouse and Cruikshank, both decided in mid-Reconstruction, interpreted the privileges or immunities clause of the 14th amendment to protect against almost nothing salient"; left African Americans without strong constitutional protections; and left any quest to constrain state "police power" on fragile textual footing *(slide 3)*. The deck repeatedly anchors interpretive moves to Miller's "one pervading purpose" passage about "the freedom of the slave race, the security and firm establishment of that freedom, and the protection of the newly-made freeman" *(slide 10)*. The "perpetual censor" passage is flagged as the doctrinal pivot -- giving plaintiffs what they wanted would make the Court "a perpetual censor upon all legislation of the States" *(slide 15)*. The "AS" emphasis -- only privileges held *as* a U.S. citizen are protected -- is highlighted as the critical textual move *(slides 12, 25)*. The Cruikshank passages get the same treatment: the right to peaceably assemble "existed long before the adoption of the Constitution of the United States" and so is "not a right granted to the people by the Constitution" *(slide 38)*; the Second Amendment right "is not a right granted by the Constitution" *(slide 40)*; the due process and equal protection clauses add nothing to rights of one citizen against another *(slides 41-42)*. The deck closes with the question whether the Court should go back and overturn Cruikshank: "Thomas says yes; Liberals doubtful / Afraid our workarounds will be killed" *(slide 46)*.

### Editor's framing

This page treats the doctrine as a "tragedy" -- a value-loaded frame that the deck supplies through Slide 14 ("And here is where the tragedy occurs"). The packaging of substantive due process and incorporation as "doctrinal workarounds" forced by the gutting of PI-XIV is editorial synthesis of the deck's "Pressure Mounts" slide (slide 45). The framing that Dobbs's attack on substantive due process "implicitly threatens the entire rights-protection edifice" is editorial sharpening of the deck's brief slide-46 note that liberals might be more receptive to revisiting Cruikshank post-Dobbs.

## Lecture Outline

**Slaughter-House Cases (1873): The Basic Move**

New Orleans stimulated creation of a centralized abattoir that would be profitable by giving it monopoly rights *(slide 4)*. Plaintiff butchers argued the monopoly violated the 13th Amendment (involuntary servitude, like Scottish "thirlage"), the 14th Amendment (unable to use their own property, unequal protection, due process violation) *(slide 5)*. Miller called the questions "so farreaching and pervading in their consequences, so profoundly interesting to the people of this country" that none equal had been before the Court in his time *(slide 8)*.

First, Miller narrowed the Privileges or Immunities Clause by distinguishing "citizenship of the United States" from "citizenship of a State" *(slide 12)*. Only privileges and immunities held *as* a U.S. citizen are placed under federal protection; the others "must rest for protection on the state" *(slide 12)*. The "AS" emphasis: "It is a little remarkable, if this clause was intended as a protection to the citizen of a State against the legislative power of his own State, that the word citizen of the State should be left out when it is so carefully used, and used in contradistinction to citizens of the United States in the very sentence which precedes it" *(slide 25)*. Rights that antedate the Constitution and belonged to citizens of the States lay "within the constitutional and legislative power of the States, and without that of the Federal government" *(slides 13, 28)*. The deck flags Miller's use of Corfield v. Coryell -- whose "fundamental" privileges were never absolute and required only equal treatment of non-state-citizens -- to show the PI-IV/PI-XIV distinction: "the more fundamental it is, the less PI-XIV would appear to protect it" *(slides 26-27)*.

Second, the "perpetual censor" concern *(slide 15)*: giving plaintiffs the broad reading would "constitute this court a perpetual censor upon all legislation of the States, on the civil rights of their own citizens, with authority to nullify such as it did not approve."

Third, on equal protection, the majority signaled it would not reach beyond race: "We doubt very much whether any action of a State not directed by way of discrimination against the negroes as a class, or on account of their race, will ever be held to come within the purview of [the equal protection clause]" *(slide 22)*.

The "Crandall Rights" enumerated as protected national-citizenship privileges *(slides 17-20, 48)*: the right to come to the seat of government to assert claims, transact business, seek protection, share offices, engage in administering functions; free access to seaports, sub-treasuries, land offices, and courts of justice; the right to protection on the high seas or within the jurisdiction of foreign governments; the right peaceably to assemble and petition for redress of grievances; the privilege of habeas corpus; the right to use navigable waters; rights secured by treaties; the right to become a citizen of any State by bona fide residence; and rights secured by the 13th and 15th Amendments and by the other clauses of the 14th. The deck flags that the Court "disclaims need to identify list of PI-XIV; but then creates a partial list that seems to become exhaustive" *(slide 54)*. On occupation: "no construction of [due process] that we have ever seen, or any that we deem admissible, can the restraint imposed by the State of Louisiana upon the exercise of their trade by the butchers of New Orleans be held to be a deprivation of property" *(slide 21)*.

**The One Pervading Purpose Reading**

Miller grounded the narrow interpretation in the historical purpose of the Reconstruction Amendments *(slide 10)*: "the one pervading purpose found in them all, lying at the foundation of each, and without which none of them would have been even suggested; we mean the freedom of the slave race, the security and firm establishment of that freedom, and the protection of the newly-made freeman and citizen from the oppressions of those who had formerly exercised unlimited dominion over him." The Court acknowledged that "we do not say that no one else but the negro can share in this protection" but stressed that any fair construction must look "to the purpose which we have said was the pervading spirit of them all" *(slide 11)*. The deck asks whether reading the Amendments to apply only to race-based oppression of the newly freed slave would kill much modern 14th Amendment case law -- "the court's answer is maybe, possibly, but not likely" *(slide 24)*.

**United States v. Cruikshank (1876): From State to Private**

The Colfax Massacre saw hundreds of armed whites from paramilitary groups (KKK and others) kill Black persons assembled in front of the county courthouse after a disputed gubernatorial election; whites used a cannon; 60-150 Black persons killed, many after surrendering *(slide 34)*. Federal prosecution by U.S. Attorney James Beckwith proceeded under the Enforcement Act of 1870, which criminalized conspiracies to injure or intimidate citizens in the free exercise of "any right or privilege granted or secured to him by the constitution or laws of the United States" *(slides 31, 37)*. The deck notes the Court's "shriveled view of PI14 rights": "the Government of the United States . . . can neither grant nor secure to its citizens any right or privilege not expressly or by implication placed under its jurisdiction" *(slide 36)*.

The Court's analysis of each claimed right mirrored the PI-XIV narrowing. The right peaceably to assemble "existed long before the adoption of the Constitution," "is and always has been one of the attributes of citizenship under a free government," and was "not, therefore, a right granted to the people by the Constitution" *(slide 38)*. The Second Amendment right to bear arms "is not a right granted by the Constitution" and is "in [no] manner dependent upon that instrument for its existence" *(slide 40)* -- so the CRA1870 only protects rights founded on the U.S. Constitution, "reading PI14 into oblivion" *(slide 40)*. On due process: the Fourteenth Amendment only constrains state action and "adds nothing to the rights of one citizen as against another" *(slide 41)*. On equal protection: the Amendment requires only that the United States "see that the States do not deny the right," not that it police private actors *(slide 42)*. On the Civil Rights Act of 1866: the indictment did not allege the crime was committed "on account of color or race" *(slide 43)*; the deck flags this as "the perils of writing sloppy opinions" *(slide 44)*. The deck notes that there appear to have been no state prosecutions against the whites for homicide *(slide 37)*.

What's left of PI-XIV after Cruikshank? "We've gotten rid of most of the bill of rights on grounds that the Constitution did not create the right" and "we've gotten rid of rights against private parties unless we can come up with a theory of blaming the state" *(slide 44)*. The deck observes the "real cure" was to send in the military -- left to the executive rather than the judiciary -- "but what happens when the military withdraws?" *(slide 44)*. The withdrawal of troops as part of the Compromise of 1876 followed the disputed election: the deal exchanged Hayes's 20 contested electoral votes for withdrawal of troops from the South *(slide 52)*.

**The Lasting Impact and Workarounds**

The deck flags the "Pressure Mounts" between constitutional text and modern notions *(slide 45)*: how to enforce Bill of Rights norms against states; how to handle unenumerated rights (right to earn a living, informational privacy, parental decision-making, reproductive autonomy); equality norms based on things other than race (gender, age, income, legitimacy, alienage, religion); and how to address state failure to recognize and curb private oppression. The result has been substantive due process and incorporation as workarounds for the gutted PI-XIV.

Saenz v. Roe (1999) briefly resurrected PI-XIV *(slide 53)*: Stevens found PI-XIV barred California from reducing welfare benefits to newcomers because it deters people from becoming citizens of the new state. Thomas dissented -- "this has nothing to do with the purposes of PI14, which we should revisit" *(slide 53)*. The deck calls PI-XIV "basically a sleeping giant" *(slide 53)*. Should the Court go back and overturn Cruikshank? "Thomas says yes; liberals doubtful / afraid our workarounds will be killed / might be more receptive post Dobbs? / and PI interpreted less broadly" *(slide 46)*.

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

The lecture asks: what rights would the PI-XIV have protected under a more expansive reading? Chandler lists the "pressure points" that built up as a result of the narrow reading: how to enforce Bill of Rights norms against states (resolved through incorporation/SDP rather than PI-XIV), how to protect unenumerated rights (right to earn a living, informational privacy, parental decision-making, reproductive autonomy), equality norms based on gender, age, income, legitimacy, alienage, and religion, and how to define "state action" to address private oppression.

The lecture poses the question of whether reversing Cruikshank today would destabilize more modern doctrine than it would fix. Thomas says yes, reverse it; Chandler notes liberals are doubtful. Post-Dobbs, the calculus may have shifted.

## Key Takeaways

- The Slaughter-House Cases' central holding is the distinction between national and state citizenship, confining PI-XIV to a narrow list of national-citizenship rights.
- The "perpetual censor" concern was the Court's institutional worry about becoming a general appellate court over all state police power decisions, but it produced a constitutional vacuum that forced rights through less textually grounded doctrines.
- Cruikshank's holding that the Bill of Rights was not "granted" by the Constitution (it merely recognized pre-existing rights) meant Congress could not use Section 5 enforcement power to protect those rights against private violation.
- Saenz v. Roe (1999) is the only significant post-Slaughter-House application of PI-XIV, and it remains an outlier.
- The doctrine's legacy is that every modern civil liberties protection rests on Due Process and Equal Protection rather than on the clause most obviously designed to protect rights, PI-XIV. This may be structurally unstable: Dobbs's attack on substantive due process implicitly threatens the entire rights-protection edifice built on that workaround.
