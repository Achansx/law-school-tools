## Professor Emphasis

### From source

The deck frames justiciability around four doctrines -- standing, ripeness, mootness, and abstention -- and explicitly poses each as a question (right to bring? dispute ready? controversy resolved? federal court declining?) *(slide 3)*. Standing is presented as a "quick review" of the Lujan three-prong framework, with the lecture flagging it will "spend our time on the harder edges" rather than re-litigate Lujan *(slide 5)*. The concrete-injury doctrine is then developed through Spokeo and TransUnion: bare procedural violations are not enough, and intangible harm must bear a "close relationship" to a harm traditionally recognized in Anglo-American courts *(slides 6-7)*. The TransUnion analogy to defamation is reproduced verbatim in the deck: "A letter that is not sent does not harm anyone, no matter how insulting the letter is" *(slide 7)*. *Lyons* is the deck's anchor for the standing-for-damages-versus-injunction distinction, and the deck calls Lyons's required showing for the injunction an "incredible assertion" *(slide 9)*. Abbott Labs is presented as a two-part test (fitness + hardship) and Ohio Forestry as the negative example: programmatic plans that do not "command anyone to do anything" or "grant any formal legal license" are not ripe *(slides 12-14)*. SBA List supplies the three-part credible-threat test for pre-enforcement constitutional challenges *(slide 15)*. Mootness is framed as "standing set in a time frame," with three exceptions enumerated explicitly: capable of repetition/evading review, voluntary cessation, and class action *(slide 20)*. The Laidlaw standard -- the "formidable burden" of showing it is "absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur" -- is the operative test for voluntary cessation, and the deck contrasts Laidlaw's reversible closure against Nike's unconditional, irrevocable covenant *(slide 23)*. The abstention slides distinguish three doctrines by trigger, remedy, and threshold: Pullman (stay), Younger (dismiss), Colorado River (exceptional circumstances, stay or dismiss) *(slides 28-33)*.

## Lecture Outline

### I. The Article III Foundation
- "The judicial Power shall extend to all Cases…[and] to Controversies…" — U.S. Const. art. III, § 2 *(slide 2)*
- Courts lack power to issue advisory opinions; every federal case must present a live dispute between adverse parties with real stakes *(slide 2)*
- Justiciability doctrines divide into: (A) constitutional requirements, standing, ripeness, mootness; (B) prudential limits, abstention *(slide 3)*

### II. Standing Quick Review (Lujan Framework)
- Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife (504 U.S. 555): three elements, all at plaintiff's burden *(slide 5)*
  1. Injury in fact: concrete and particularized, actual or imminent, not abstract or hypothetical *(slide 5)*
  2. Causation: fairly traceable to defendant's conduct, not independent third-party action *(slide 5)*
  3. Redressability: favorable decision must be likely to redress the injury, not merely speculative *(slide 5)*
- Burden: general allegations suffice at pleading stage; specific facts required at summary judgment and trial *(slide 5)*

### III. Concrete Injury — The Spokeo/TransUnion Line

**Spokeo, Inc. v. Robins (2016)**
- Facts: Spokeo's people-search website generated an inaccurate profile of Robins (wrong marital status, children, age, employment, wealth); Robins sued under FCRA alleging inaccuracies damaged his job prospects *(slide 6)*
- Held: A bare procedural violation of a statute, divorced from any concrete harm, is insufficient for Article III standing *(slide 6)*
- Test: intangible harm must bear a "close relationship" to a harm traditionally recognized as providing a basis for a lawsuit in Anglo-American courts *(slide 6)*
- Rule: Congress's judgment that a harm is cognizable is "instructive and important" but Congress "may not simply enact an injury into existence" *(slide 6)*
- The deck uses the wrong-zip-code example to illustrate an inaccuracy that has no traditional-tort analogue and causes no real harm *(slide 6)*

**TransUnion LLC v. Ramirez (2021)** — 5-4
- Facts: TransUnion flagged 8,185 consumers as potential matches to OFAC's watchlist using a name-only algorithm; Ramirez was denied a car purchase in front of his family; 1,853 class members had reports sent to third-party creditors; 6,332 had false alerts only in internal files *(slide 7)*
- Held: Only the 1,853 with disseminated reports had standing; 6,332 with internal-only files lacked standing *(slide 7)*
- Key quote: "A letter that is not sent does not harm anyone, no matter how insulting the letter is" *(slide 7)*
- Rationale: Analogy to defamation — publication to third parties is required; internal records not communicated externally do not support standing *(slide 7)*

**Hypothetical: The Data Breach** *(slide 8)*
- MegaMart data breach exposes 2 million customers; federal statute gives "affected consumer" $500 in statutory damages; Sarah cannot show her data was actually viewed, sold, or misused *(slide 8)*
- Does Sarah have Article III standing? How does this compare to Spokeo's zip code example? Does spending 10 hours monitoring credit "manufacture" standing through self-imposed mitigation costs (Clapper v. Amnesty International, 568 U.S. 398)? Would exposure of Social Security numbers change the analysis because identity theft is more analogous to a traditionally recognized tort? *(slide 8)*

### IV. Injunctive Standing: The Future Injury Problem

**City of Los Angeles v. Lyons (1983)**
- Facts: Adolph Lyons stopped for a burned-out taillight; officers applied a chokehold without provocation, rendering him unconscious; Lyons sued for damages plus injunction barring LAPD chokeholds; between 1975 and 1982, LAPD chokeholds killed 16 people *(slide 9)*
- Held: Lyons had standing for damages but lacked standing for the injunction; he would need to show a "real and immediate" threat of being choked again *(slide 9)*
- Rule: Standing for damages and standing for injunctive relief are different inquiries; for an injunction the plaintiff must demonstrate a likelihood of future injury, not just that they were harmed in the past *(slide 9)*
- The deck calls Lyons's required allegation -- that he'd be stopped again *and* that officers would choke him again -- an "incredible assertion" *(slide 9)*
- Significance: connects standing and ripeness; plaintiff's showing for forward-looking relief must be much stronger than for damages

**Hypothetical: The Traffic Stop** *(slide 10)*
- Marcus, a rideshare driver, is subjected to a 15-minute prone restraint by Greenville PD after a minor traffic violation; bruised ribs; seeks $50,000 damages plus injunction requiring retraining and banning the prone restraint; drives through Greenville daily *(slide 10)*
- Standing for damages? For the injunction? Does daily driving distinguish him from Lyons? Does a statistical showing (40% of traffic stops use prone restraint) change the analysis? Does bringing as a class action change anything? *(slide 10)*

### V. Ripeness — Is the Dispute Ready for Judicial Resolution?

**Abbott Laboratories v. Gardner (1967)**
- Purpose: prevents courts from "entangling themselves in abstract disagreements"; protects agencies "from judicial interference until an administrative decision has been formalized and its effects felt in a concrete way" *(slide 12)*
- Two-part test (Harlan): *(slide 12)*
  1. **Fitness for judicial decision**: is the issue purely legal? Has the agency action been finalized? Purely legal questions not dependent on further factual development are most fit for immediate review *(slide 12)*
  2. **Hardship to the parties**: will withholding review cause direct and immediate harm? Courts weigh whether parties face a dilemma between costly compliance and the risk of prosecution *(slide 12)*
- Facts: FDA required generic name printed alongside brand name on all labels and advertisements; drug companies challenged before any enforcement, facing pressure to begin expensive relabeling *(slide 13)*
- Why ripe: (1) fitness — pure legal question (statutory construction), final regulation, no further factual development needed; (2) hardship — genuine dilemma between expensive compliance and criminal prosecution for distributing misbranded drugs *(slide 13)*

**Ohio Forestry Ass'n v. Sierra Club (1998)**
- Facts: Sierra Club challenged a Forest Service management plan for the Wayne National Forest designating 126,000 acres as available for logging with a 10-year ceiling of 75 million board feet; the plan did not authorize cutting a single tree — further steps (site-specific proposals, environmental review, notice and comment) were required *(slide 14)*
- Held: Not ripe *(slide 14)*
- Why: (1) fitness — the plan did not "command anyone to do anything" or "grant any formal legal license"; courts would benefit from factual development at the site-specific stage; (2) hardship — no direct hardship from waiting; Sierra Club could challenge each specific logging decision when it arose *(slide 14)*
- Lesson: programmatic plans ≠ site-specific actions; a plan that sets goals but requires further steps is not ripe, while a final regulation with immediate compliance demands is *(slide 14)*

**Susan B. Anthony List v. Driehaus (2014) — Pre-Enforcement Standing**
- Facts: Ohio law criminalized false statements about political candidates; SBA put up a billboard claiming a congressman voted for "taxpayer-funded abortion" through the ACA; state commission found probable cause of violation; SBA challenged the law's constitutionality before any criminal prosecution *(slide 15)*
- Three-part test for pre-enforcement ripeness:
  1. Intent: plaintiff intends to engage in conduct arguably proscribed by the statute *(slide 15)*
  2. Credible threat: credible threat of prosecution -- past enforcement is strong evidence, and the deck notes "any person" could file a complaint, including political opponents *(slide 15)*
  3. Self-censorship/chilling effect: the law forces a choice between self-censorship and risking prosecution *(slide 15)*
- Distinguished from speculative future prosecution: here the threat was not imaginary; a complaint had actually been filed

**Hypotheticals**
- **New Regulation**: Restaurant Transparency Act (calorie/allergen/sourcing posting), effective in 18 months, implementing regs not yet issued; FastBurger sues immediately under First Amendment; the AG says the office "looks forward to vigorous enforcement" — apply the Abbott Labs two-part test, and compare the AG's statement to SBA List credible-threat analysis *(slide 16)*
- **Campus Speech Code**: State University adopts a "Bias-Related Incident Policy" prohibiting speech that "demeans, degrades, or disrespects" based on identity, with discipline up to expulsion; Professor Chen wants to assign offensive primary sources for a colonialism course; no discipline yet — apply SBA List's three-part test; compare to Ohio Forestry *(slide 17)*
- **Development Plan**: Riverdale rezones the Elm Street neighborhood from single-family to high-density mixed use (20 stories vs. 3); no developer has applied; homeowners sue for takings and due process — Abbott Labs (ripe) or Ohio Forestry (not ripe)? *(slide 18)*

### VI. Mootness and Its Exceptions

Core rule: Article III requires a live controversy throughout litigation, not just at filing. Mootness is "standing set in a time frame"; unlike standing (assessed at filing), mootness can arise at any point; if no live controversy, court loses Article III jurisdiction *(slide 20)*.

**Exception 1 — Capable of Repetition, Yet Evading Review** *(slides 20-21)*

Two elements, both required: *(slide 21)*

1. The challenged action is inherently limited in duration; it will always become moot before litigation can reach appellate review *(slide 21)*
2. There is a reasonable expectation or demonstrated probability that the same complaining party will be subjected to the same action again *(slide 21)*

Classic examples: Roe v. Wade (pregnancy resolves in ~9 months; plaintiff could become pregnant again); Dunn v. Blumstein (election cycles end before litigation; voter could face same residency rule); Southern Pacific Terminal v. ICC (short-lived ICC orders expired during review but would predictably recur) *(slide 21)*. The exception is narrow: "some day" recurrence is not enough; the same party must face the same action *(slide 21)*.

**Exception 2 — Voluntary Cessation**

*Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw (2000)*
- Facts: Environmental groups sued a company for discharging pollutants above Clean Water Act limits; after years of litigation, the company closed the facility and came into compliance *(slide 23)*
- Held: Not moot. A defendant's voluntary cessation of challenged conduct does not automatically moot a case *(slide 23)*
- Standard: defendant bears the "formidable burden" of showing it is "absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur" *(slide 23)*
- Why? The closure happened only under litigation pressure, and the company could not prove violations wouldn't recur *(slide 23)*

*Already LLC v. Nike (2013)*
- Facts: Nike sued Already for trademark infringement, then issued a broad, unconditional, irrevocable covenant not to sue Already or any affiliate for any current or "colorable imitation" of its designs *(slide 23)*
- Held: Moot. Nike met its burden; the covenant's terms made future litigation "absolutely" impossible *(slide 23)*
- Key contrast: form of cessation matters enormously; Laidlaw's closure was reversible and came under litigation pressure; Nike's covenant was unconditional, irrevocable, and legally binding *(slide 23)*

**Exception 3 — Class Action** *(slide 20)*
- Once a class is certified, it takes on a legal life of its own; the named plaintiff's individual claim can become moot without destroying the case *(slide 20)*

**Standing vs. Mootness Comparison**

| | Standing | Mootness |
|---|---|---|
| When? | At time of filing | Can arise any time during litigation |
| Question | Did plaintiff ever have a stake? | Does plaintiff still have a stake? |
| Burden | Plaintiff must prove | Defendant bears heavy burden |
| Speculation | Too speculative, no standing | Too speculative to recur, case survives |
| Result | Dismissed for lack of jurisdiction | Dismissed as moot |

**Hypotheticals**
- **Festival Permit**: LCA denied permit for annual June festival; applies every year for 8 years; case reaches summary judgment after festival date passes — apply "capable of repetition" two-part test; what if the city passes a new ordinance specifically guaranteeing future permits but subject to repeal by simple majority? *(slide 22)*
- **Factory on the River**: Apex Chemical installs $2M filtration system 3 months after suit filed; CEO gives a press interview stating they've fixed the problem; no consent decree — apply Laidlaw standard; compare to Already v. Nike's irrevocable covenant *(slide 24)*
- **Expelled Student**: Taylor suspended 10 days under zero-tolerance policy for a Swiss Army knife; sues under § 1983 for lack of individualized hearing; graduates while case pending; district then revises policy by 3–2 board vote — does graduation moot the case? Voluntary cessation? Class action exception? *(slide 25)*

### VII. Abstention Doctrines

Unlike standing, ripeness, and mootness (constitutional requirements), abstention doctrines are judge-made and prudential; courts have jurisdiction but choose not to exercise it out of deference to state courts, comity, or avoidance of unnecessary constitutional rulings *(slide 28)*.

**Pullman Abstention** (Railroad Comm'n of Texas v. Pullman Co., 1941)
- Three requirements: (1) uncertain issue of state law underlies the federal constitutional claim; (2) the state issue is amenable to state court interpretation that could moot or narrow the federal constitutional question; (3) erroneous construction of state law by the federal court could result in an unnecessary ruling on the federal constitutional question *(slide 29)*
- Mechanism: Federal court stays the federal action and retains jurisdiction while the state court clarifies the state law question *(slide 29)*
- Rationale: Federalism — state courts should have first opportunity to interpret their own law; avoids unnecessary constitutional rulings

**Younger Abstention** (Younger v. Harris, 1971)
- Federal courts must not interfere with ongoing state judicial proceedings; rooted in "Our Federalism" — state courts are equally capable of safeguarding constitutional rights *(slide 30)*
- Three requirements: (1) ongoing state judicial proceedings; (2) proceedings implicate important state interests; (3) adequate opportunity to raise federal claims in state proceedings *(slide 30)*
- Mechanism: Federal court dismisses the action (or stays if damages are sought)
- Exceptions: bad-faith prosecution brought to harass, flagrantly unconstitutional statute, or extraordinary circumstances calling for federal equitable relief *(slide 30)*

**Colorado River Abstention** (Colorado River Water Conservation District v. U.S., 1976)
- Narrowest form; federal courts have a "virtually unflagging obligation" to exercise jurisdiction; only in "exceptional circumstances" involving parallel state proceedings may they stay or dismiss *(slide 31)*
- Six factors (no single one determinative): (1) which court first assumed jurisdiction over the res; (2) inconvenience of the federal forum; (3) desirability of avoiding piecemeal litigation; (4) order in which jurisdiction was obtained; (5) whether state or federal law controls; (6) adequacy of state court to protect federal rights *(slide 31)*
- Distinguished from forum non conveniens: Colorado River is constitutional (Article III limits); does not apply merely because state court would be more convenient

**Hypothetical: The Zoning Prosecution**
- Rivera prosecuted in state court for operating a legal aid clinic in a commercially zoned area; ordinance does not define "professional offices"; Rivera files federal § 1983 while state prosecution is pending, alleging vagueness plus retaliation because the clinic serves low-income immigrants *(slide 32)*
- Could both Younger and Pullman apply? Walk through each *(slide 32)*

### VIII. Synthesis: Why These Doctrines Matter Together
- Together, standing, ripeness, mootness, and abstention define the outer limits of federal judicial power *(slide 34)*
- They reinforce the Article III requirement that federal courts resolve only live disputes between parties with genuine stakes
- They also reflect separation of powers and federalism values: courts should not issue advisory opinions, should not micromanage other branches, and should respect state autonomy

## Key Takeaways

1. Article III limits federal courts to actual cases and controversies; standing, ripeness, and mootness all enforce this requirement at different points in time.
2. Standing requires concrete, particularized injury, not just a statutory violation; Congress can identify cognizable harms but cannot manufacture Article III injury from a bare procedural violation (Spokeo; TransUnion's dissemination requirement).
3. Standing for damages and standing for injunctive relief are distinct: for prospective relief, the plaintiff must show a real and immediate future threat, not just past harm (Lyons).
4. Ripeness turns on fitness (is the legal question ripe for review?) and hardship (will the parties suffer if review is withheld?). Programmatic plans that require further steps are generally not ripe; final regulations with immediate compliance demands generally are (Abbott Labs vs. Ohio Forestry).
5. Mootness has teeth but has three major exceptions: capable of repetition/evading review (same party plus inherently short duration); voluntary cessation (defendant must meet a formidable burden showing absolutely clear non-recurrence); class action (certified class survives named plaintiff's mootness).
6. Abstention doctrines are prudential, not constitutional: Pullman (stay for uncertain state law); Younger (dismiss to respect ongoing state proceedings); Colorado River (stay or dismiss for parallel state suits in truly exceptional circumstances).
