# Sex Discrimination and Equal Protection - Class Recap

## Professor Emphasis

### From source

The recap structures the sex-discrimination story as an arc from divine-will exclusion in Bradwell (1873) to the brink of strict scrutiny in Frontiero (1973) to intermediate scrutiny in Craig v Boren (1976) *(slide 3)*. Craig's significance is flagged as reaching beyond its beer facts: it extends Equal Protection definitively beyond race, departs from the Carolene Products discrete-and-insular-minority framework, and announces an anti-classification symmetry principle by which male plaintiffs challenging laws that favor women also prevail *(slide 4)*. Geduldig's pregnancy-versus-sex distinction is presented as still unresolved in 2026 and explicitly flagged as the framework that will return in Skrmetti *(slide 5)*. The session opens by completing the SFFA recap from April 8 — race in essays admissible only to corroborate hardship, the military-academy exception now moot — and by resolving the Marshall Legacy Scholarship state-action problem as probably not state action under Blum v Yaretsky, with Burton-style symbiosis arguments available if the school promotes and provides material support *(slide 2)*.

## Lecture Outline

### Completing the Affirmative Action Recap

The session opened with a brief review of SFFA's essay exception (race admissible in essays only to corroborate hardship claims tied to character or unique ability) and the military academy footnote (reserved; currently moot after the Trump administration ended military affirmative action) *(slide 2)*. The Marshall Legacy Scholarship problem from the prior session was resolved: private firm funding of race-conscious scholarships at a state law school probably does not constitute state action under Blum v Yaretsky (no decision-making authority in the school), though Burton-based symbiosis arguments are available if the school actively promotes and provides material support *(slide 2)*. Exam tip: the Marshall Legacy problem is a model state-action / equal-protection crossover *(slide 2)*.

### The Early Cases: Bradwell, Reed, and Frontiero

**Bradwell v Illinois (1873).** Myra Bradwell was denied a license to practice law in Illinois on the ground that she was a woman *(slide 3)*. The majority held that the Privileges or Immunities Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment did not encompass the practice of law (a state privilege under the Slaughter-House Cases framework) *(slide 3)*. Justice Bradley's concurrence grounded exclusion in divine will and natural law: women's "timidity and delicacy" and their "paramount destiny" as wives and mothers justified exclusion from professional life *(slide 3)*. A critical flaw the deck highlights: the Court affirmed that Illinois was correct on the merits -- not merely that Illinois was permitted to act -- which reflects a substantive view of gender roles dressed up as constitutional analysis *(slide 3)*.

**Reed v Reed (1971).** Idaho law automatically preferred men over women as estate administrators when a deceased left multiple equally-related applicants *(slide 3)*. The Court struck it down under rational basis *(slide 3)*. The deck asks whether the preference was genuinely irrational: in 1971, women often had not taken business courses, suggesting the Court may have shoehorned facts into a lower scrutiny tier because it was not yet prepared to apply heightened scrutiny *(slide 3)*. Reed nonetheless marks the first time the Court invalidated a sex-based classification under the Equal Protection Clause *(slide 3)*.

**Frontiero v Richardson (1973).** The military presumed wives of servicemen to be dependents but required husbands of servicewomen to prove dependency *(slide 3)*. Four Justices (Brennan, Douglas, White, Marshall) pushed for strict scrutiny on the ground that sex, like race, is an immutable characteristic tied to a long history of discrimination *(slide 3)*. Justice Powell, however, declined to provide the fifth vote -- he argued the Equal Rights Amendment was working its way through the ratification process and the Court should not preempt democratic resolution of the issue *(slide 3)*. A theoretical difficulty with applying Carolene Products: women are not a "discrete and insular minority" in the sense Carolene contemplates *(slide 3)*.

### Craig v Boren (1976): Intermediate Scrutiny Arrives

Oklahoma permitted women to purchase 3.2 percent beer at 18 but required men to wait until 21 *(slide 4)*. The statistical predicate: 0.18 percent of females and 2 percent of males had been arrested for DUI *(slide 4)*. Brennan's majority characterizes this as "less than 2% correlation" — the deck notes this understates the male disproportion by subtraction rather than division; the correct comparison shows men are arrested roughly ten times more often than women *(slide 4)*.

The new standard: sex-based governmental action must serve important governmental objectives and must be substantially related to achievement of those objectives *(slide 4)*. This is tighter than rational basis (conceivable rationale, loose fit) but looser than strict scrutiny (compelling interest, necessary means). The intermediate standard has never been formally renamed and sits between the two traditional tiers.

Craig's significance beyond beer is threefold *(slide 4)*:

(1) Equal Protection now definitively extends beyond race -- far from the Slaughter-House holding that the Clause was addressed primarily to newly freed slaves *(slide 4)*.

(2) The Carolene Products framework (heightened scrutiny for discrete and insular minorities) cannot explain Craig, because neither women nor men buying beer fit that description; the Court is protecting against arbitrary legislative classification generally *(slide 4)*.

(3) Symmetry: Equal Protection protects all persons regardless of sex — the anti-classification principle by which male plaintiffs challenging laws that favor women succeed and which establishes the doctrinal groundwork for challenges from any direction *(slide 4)*.

Chandler's characterization: Craig represents a new form of Lochnerism applied to sex classifications — the Court substitutes its judgment that most sex-based laws are just stupid for the legislature's and demands real evidence to justify them *(slide 4)*.

### Geduldig v Aiello (1974): Pregnancy, Intersectionality, and Tiers

California's disability insurance program covered temporary disabilities but excluded normal pregnancy from coverage *(slide 5)*. The Court held this is not sex discrimination at all and therefore never subjected the program to any heightened scrutiny *(slide 5)*. The classification distinguishes between pregnant women and nonpregnant persons *(slide 5)*. Because the nonpregnant group includes both men and women, the distinction is pregnancy discrimination (a physical condition), not sex discrimination (a protected characteristic) *(slide 5)*. The asymmetry that prostate care was covered while pregnancy was not is flagged on the slide as a gender-insensitivity or legitimate-program-design question *(slide 5)*.

The 8-foot-tall hypothetical: deny benefits to people over 8 feet tall; no women qualify, but the criterion is height, not sex *(slide 5)*. The answer depends on intent — was height used as a proxy for sex? *(slide 5)*. Geduldig suggests intent picks the tier, not disparate impact *(slide 5)*.

**The intersectionality problem (still unresolved in 2026).** When a statute combines classification criteria from different scrutiny tiers -- for example, sex (intermediate) and age (rational basis) -- which standard applies? *(slide 5)*. Possible rules listed on the slide: (1) apply the highest tier triggered by any criterion; (2) apply the lowest; (3) average them; (4) look to intent — was the lower-tier criterion added specifically to evade the higher-tier test? *(slide 5)*. A statute denying Social Security to "women over 70" suggests that if the legislature started with "deny women benefits" and added age to dodge intermediate scrutiny, that evasion should not succeed *(slide 5)*. The deck explicitly flags that Geduldig's framework will return in Skrmetti *(slide 5)*.

### VMI, Quick Hits, and Looking Ahead

**United States v Virginia (1996).** The Virginia Military Institute excluded women from its adversative-method military training *(slide 6)*. Virginia responded by creating VWIL (Virginia Women's Institute for Leadership) at Mary Baldwin College *(slide 6)*. Justice Ginsburg's majority held VWIL a "pale shadow" of VMI -- separate but unequal *(slide 6)*. VMI's defense (most women cannot handle the adversative method) failed because most men cannot handle it either, and the state cannot exclude the women who can *(slide 6)*. Ginsburg added the "exceedingly persuasive justification" gloss to the intermediate scrutiny standard, a formulation the deck flags as slyly elevating the test toward de facto strict scrutiny; because the ERA never came, Ginsburg used the Equal Protection Clause itself as the instrument for sex equality *(slide 6)*.

**Sessions v Morales-Santana (2017).** Intermediate scrutiny extends to immigration law, which had historically been insulated from constitutional scrutiny under the plenary power doctrine *(slide 6)*. Sex-differentiated citizenship transmission rules (favoring unwed citizen mothers over unwed citizen fathers) were struck down *(slide 6)*.

**San Antonio Independent School District v Rodriguez (1973).** Wealth is not a suspect classification; education is not a fundamental right *(slide 6)*. The liberal dream of constitutionalizing economic equality and educational access died here *(slide 6)*.

**City of Cleburne v Cleburne Living Center (1985).** Disability receives only rational basis review, not a heightened tier *(slide 6)*. But the Court applied rational basis with "bite" — the zoning ordinance excluding a group home for people with intellectual disabilities failed even rational basis *(slide 6)*. The deck calls this the tantalizing fourth tier: rational basis rigorously applied can achieve results functionally indistinguishable from intermediate scrutiny without formally establishing a new protected class *(slide 6)*.

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

**The Beer Statistics Problem.** Craig v Boren involves Brennan's subtraction error: 0.18 percent female DUI arrests versus 2 percent male arrests becomes "less than 2% correlation" when the correct comparison is a 10x disproportion *(slide 4)*. Why does the result survive this mathematical problem? Because even a 10x disproportion means 98 percent of men arrested for DUI are not involved in the beer-drinking population targeted by the statute, and the statute makes no individualized determination. Intermediate scrutiny requires substantial relation, and a law that sweeps in 98 percent of nondangerous men to regulate the dangerous 2 percent lacks the fit the standard demands.

**The Intersectionality Tier Question.** Geduldig involves the interaction of sex (intermediate) and pregnancy (no heightened scrutiny) *(slide 5)*. When a later case involves a classification that combines sex with a lower-scrutiny characteristic (age, disability, economic status), which tier applies? If the answer is intent picks the tier, the inquiry becomes whether the lower-scrutiny criterion was added to evade the higher-tier test applicable to the primary characteristic *(slide 5)*. This framework matters greatly for Skrmetti, in which the classification involves sex (intermediate scrutiny) and transgender status (contested tier), and the argument that the sex criterion is derivative of gender identity will drive the outcome.

## Key Takeaways

- Sex classifications receive intermediate scrutiny: important governmental objectives substantially related to achievement by the means chosen.
- Craig v Boren extended EP beyond race and broke free of the Carolene Products discrete-and-insular-minority rationale; the anti-classification symmetry principle means men can challenge laws favoring women.
- Geduldig: pregnancy discrimination is not sex discrimination -- but intent can collapse the distinction.
- VMI's "exceedingly persuasive justification" language may have quietly elevated intermediate scrutiny toward strict scrutiny without formally announcing a doctrinal change.
- The intersectionality tier problem (which scrutiny level governs when multiple classifications from different tiers combine) is unresolved in 2026 and will surface in Skrmetti.
