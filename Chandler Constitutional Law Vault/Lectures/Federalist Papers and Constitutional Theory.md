## Lecture Outline

**The Status of the Federalist Papers as Constitutional Authority**

The Papers were published in New York newspapers during the ratification debates of 1787 to 1788, written by Alexander Hamilton, James Madison, and John Jay under the pen name "Publius." They were advocacy documents written to persuade New York to ratify. They were not the proceedings of the Constitutional Convention (which were kept secret until Madison published his notes in 1840, after all convention delegates had died). They represent the views of three participants, not a consensus of the framers.

Despite these limitations, courts and scholars treat the Papers as uniquely authoritative extrinsic evidence of constitutional meaning. The deck frames this as an open question, "Why privilege the Federalist Papers?", and offers both sides: the Papers may be "the best evidence we have," but the Constitution was ratified at state conventions where delegates may have had their own beliefs, not every argument may have been persuasive to everyone, and "not every issue of meaning was covered" *(slide 13)*. Students are invited to think critically about what interpretive work the Papers actually do in constitutional argument versus what work other sources might do equally well *(slide 13)*.

**Federalist 10: Factions and the Extended Republic (Madison)**

Madison's central problem is faction. The deck summarizes the argument as "we shouldn't worry about large Republics; they fix problems of faction," asks what "faction" is, and notes the perceived need for the argument was that "AOC -> Constitution was creating a large Republic" *(slide 5)*. Removing the causes of faction would require either destroying liberty or imposing uniformity of opinion, both unacceptable, so the solution is to control faction's effects by extending the sphere of the republic.

The deck advances Madison's evidence as "theories about how fewer kooks get power in large Republic" and "diversity in large Republic creates cross-cutting concerns and there can be trades" *(slide 5)*. Madison thus inverts the classical republican preference for small polities: the large republic is better than the small republic precisely because it makes faction harder to coordinate. Slide 6's key passage offers Madison's claim that "the influence of factious leaders may kindle a flame within their particular States, but will be unable to spread a general conflagration through the other States," and the deck's annotation flags this as "speculative" and asks whether it makes sense in the modern era *(slide 6)*.

The deck asks "Does this make any sense in the modern era with transportation and interstate communication?" *(slide 6)*. This page extends that into a modern-political-practice gloss: party organization, gerrymandering, and mass communications have arguably enabled precisely the factionalism Madison hoped geography would suppress -- a list that goes beyond what the deck specifies.

**Federalist 78: Judicial Review and the Nature of Judicial Power (Hamilton)**

The deck summarizes Hamilton's argument as "don't worry about that federal judiciary even with judicial review. It's weak" *(slide 7)*. The perceived need for the argument: "AOC had no federal courts; judicial review takes power away from the people," and "life tenure is a little scary" *(slide 7)*. Hamilton's evidence as enumerated by the deck: "no sword; no purse"; judicial review actually preserves popular sovereignty by stopping legislative abuse; it is normal; and judges "won't frequently be brave enough to buck popular will" *(slide 7)*. Slide 8's key passage gives Hamilton's positive case: courts are "an intermediate body between the people and the legislature, in order, among other things, to keep the latter within the limits assigned to their authority" *(slide 8)*. The deck invites students to "reserve judgment until we see exceptions to judicial review" *(slide 7)* and notes "Israel is now living the issue of Federalist 78" *(slide 8)*.

This page connects Federalist 78 to Marbury v. Madison: Marshall's reasoning closely tracks Hamilton's logic even without citing the Papers. That doctrinal connection is standard but is not made explicit in the deck.

The deck introduces the Anti-Federalist counterpoint through "Brutus," noting "very interesting arguments / But few study them today" -- a framing it labels "winners' history" *(slide 12)*. This page expands on Brutus's substance (the "power of the courts will enable them to mould the government" quotation; the prediction that life-tenured judges would read constitutional provisions broadly) and asks whether the Brutus critique has been validated by history -- substantive moves that go beyond what the deck supplies.

**Federalist 51: Separation of Powers and Ambition (Madison)**

The deck summarizes Madison's argument as "separation of powers complements federalism to prevent oppression" *(slide 10)*. The evidence the deck lists is "logic," "bicamerality helps too," and "an implicit cooperative game theoretic argument about the difficulty of forming stable and powerful coalitions" *(slide 10)*. Slide 11's key passage gives Madison's formula: those who administer each department must be given "the necessary constitutional means and personal motives to resist encroachments of the others … Ambition must be made to counteract ambition" *(slide 11)*. The deck notes Federalist 51 was "Justice O'Connor's favorite Federalist" *(slide 10)* and asks "Do you buy it?", flagging that "the game theory depends on a lot of assumptions" and asking how "states persist[ed] in racial oppression notwithstanding separation of powers" *(slide 10)*.

Modern separation-of-powers doctrine — cases like Youngstown Sheet and Tube Co. v. Sawyer, INS v. Chadha, and Morrison v. Olson — rests on structural arguments about the allocation of power that trace back to the logic Madison articulates here, though the deck itself does not cite those cases. The lecture also notes the tension between Federalist 51's structural optimism and the political reality of party loyalty: if members of Congress are elected from the same party as the President, the structural incentives to resist executive encroachment may be overwhelmed by partisan solidarity.

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

The lecture poses the foundational interpretive question: if Federalist 78 supports broad judicial review, and Brutus No. 1 predicted that such review would give courts unchecked supremacy over the political branches, which prediction has proven correct, and does either document tell us what the Constitution requires today?

Students are asked whether the Papers should be read as (1) authoritative statements of the Constitution's original public meaning, (2) persuasive but non-binding arguments by prominent advocates for ratification, (3) evidence of what sophisticated contemporaries understood the document to mean, or (4) something else entirely. How the answer shapes interpretive methodology is Chandler's core question.

The modern factionalism problem: Madison's Federalist 10 solution to faction was geographic dispersion and the large republic. Today, political parties operate nationally, social media accelerates the spread of passion rather than cooling it, and partisan gerrymandering enables factions to entrench themselves. Does this mean Madison's structural solution has failed, and if so, what follows for constitutional interpretation and design?

## Key Takeaways

- The Federalist Papers are uniquely influential but not uniquely authoritative: they were advocacy documents, not official interpretive materials, and they reflect the views of three authors, not the collective intent of the framers.
- Federalist 10 inverts classical republican theory: the large republic suppresses faction by diluting it, while small republics amplify faction by concentrating it.
- Federalist 78 provides the theoretical foundation for judicial review that Marshall operationalized in Marbury, but also illuminates the least-dangerous-branch premise that critics argue has not survived the Court's modern expansion of power.
- Federalist 51's "ambition counteracts ambition" principle underlies modern structural constitutional law, but its effectiveness depends on members of Congress having incentives that diverge from the President's, which partisan politics may undermine.
- The Anti-Federalist Papers, especially Brutus No. 1, are the essential counterpoint to the Federalist Papers and anticipated many of the structural concerns that motivated later constitutional debates about judicial supremacy, federal power, and individual rights.
