## Lecture Outline

**Gonzales v. Raich (2005) -- Setting Up the Problem**

The DEA seized Monson's private "medical" marijuana because its possession violated the Controlled Substances Act, even though California had legalized the kind of homegrown medical marijuana at issue *(slide 2)*. The deck asks: does California's legalization matter? Would Monson's case be any weaker in Texas? *(slide 2)*. Randy Barnett lost *(slide 2)*. The lecture poses the analytical puzzle of whether there is any real difference between this case and Wickard v. Filburn -- and if not, how do we distinguish Wickard, Lopez, Morrison, and this case? *(slide 3)*

**Possible Distinctions from Wickard**

The deck identifies four potential distinctions: the AAA statute in Wickard exempted "farmers" as small as Filburn, while the CSA goes farther; Filburn did sell some wheat, while the Raich defendants sold no marijuana at all; the record in Wickard showed that home-grown produce affects prices; and California had "detached" medical marijuana from the recreational market *(slide 4)*.

**Stevens's Majority Answer**

Stevens wrote: "[t]he regulation is squarely within Congress' commerce power because production of the commodity meant for home consumption, be it wheat or marijuana, has a substantial effect on supply and demand in the national market for that commodity" *(slide 5)*. Would this support a federal registration system for sibling babysitting? *(slide 5)*. The deck asks whether Congress must create a jurisdictional exception when the regulation "ensnares some purely intrastate activity," and explains the rational basis test *(slide 5)*.

**O'Connor's Dissent -- The Drafting Guide Critique**

O'Connor invoked the Brandeis laboratories theme and the "perverse incentives" created by the Lopez exception *(slide 6)*. She warned: "If the Court is right, then Lopez stands for nothing more than a drafting guide" *(slide 6)*. She spots a "trick" in the level of generality the court uses *(slide 6)*. The deck asks whether O'Connor accepts the rational basis test for determining the scope of congressional power, how much of her dissent is about disagreement on the facts of the medical marijuana market, and whether the Tenth Amendment is a "mere truism" for her *(slide 6)*.

**Scalia's Concurrence -- The Necessary and Proper Angle**

The deck flags Scalia's concurrence as "possibly the opinion he would most like back" *(slide 7)*. It asks how Scalia answers O'Connor's dissent, whether his reliance on McCulloch is comforting, why the fungibility of medical and recreational marijuana matters, and what he says about the "proper" component of "necessary and proper" *(slide 8)*.

**Thomas's Dissent -- The "Proper" Limit**

Thomas dissented vigorously, noting that the challengers were not attacking the CSA broadly but only its extension to homegrown agriculture specifically authorized by state law *(slide 9)*. He attacked the rational basis of labeling marijuana as Schedule I and revived "Proper" as a limit -- "first use of 'Proper' as a limit in a long time [NFIB]" *(slide 9)*. He warned: "There is a danger to concentrating too much, as well as too little, power in the Federal Government" *(slide 9)*. The deck asks what those dangers are in an era of divided government and high partisanship, and what racial and ethnic groups are most affected by prosecution for marijuana violations *(slide 9)*.

**Aftermath: Policy and Social Context**

The deck steps back to ask whether these cases "might be more a referendum on the underlying conduct than on the commerce clause" *(slide 10)*. It asks whether substantive regulation can be fully detached from constitutionality, and how the opioid crisis might have evolved if more states had legalized marijuana and Raich came out the other way *(slide 10)*. Obama-era guidance limited prosecutorial discretion to categories like distribution to minors, revenue to criminal gangs, diversion of legal marijuana to illegal markets, firearms used to protect production, drugged driving, and growing on public lands *(slide 11)*. Congress may condition financial aid on having no drug conviction while receiving loans (Dole), and a Louisiana lawyer was suspended for one year for taking eight ounces of marijuana in payment for defense services *(slide 13)*. The MORE Act, passed by the House in December 2020 and stalled in the Senate, would have removed marijuana from the CSA, imposed a 5% tax, opened SBA loans, and barred denial of federal public benefits or immigration benefits based on cannabis-related conduct *(slide 16)*.

**Taylor v. United States (2016)**

A 7-1 decision asked whether the federal government can punish a robbery on a local drug dealer and, if so, what it must prove beyond a reasonable doubt under the Hobbs Act *(slide 17)*. The options were to prove BARD that the class of activities mentioned in the statute substantially affects interstate commerce, or to prove that the activity engaged in by the victim did *(slide 18)*. The Court held that drug dealing is commerce and interstate commerce, so the government need not prove anything with respect to the particular victim: "Our holding today is limited to cases in which the defendant targets drug dealers for the purpose of stealing drugs or drug proceeds. We do not resolve what the Government must prove to establish Hobbs Act robbery where some other type of business or victim is targeted" *(slide 19)*. Thomas dissented alone, arguing robbery itself is not commerce and Congress may prohibit it only to the extent doing so is "necessary and proper for carrying into Execution" the commerce power *(slide 20)*. He distinguished Raich: "Raich decided only that Congress had a rational basis -- a merely 'conceivable' basis -- for thinking that it needed to regulate that activity as part of an effective regulatory regime. That is far from a finding, beyond a reasonable doubt, that a particular robbery relating to marijuana is an activity that affects interstate commerce" *(slide 20)*. The deck flags that the majority lets Congress criminalize essentially any robbery *(slide 20)*.

The class discussion focuses on the level-of-generality problem. The deck asks whether the class-of-activities rational basis test is principled or whether it renders Lopez/Morrison essentially unenforceable. Questions include: would a federal registration system for sibling babysitting services be constitutional after Raich (both are local, both arguably aggregate to an effect on commerce)? *(slide 5)*. ## Key Takeaways

- Raich extends Wickard's aggregation principle to regulate intrastate activity that is part of a broader regulated market, even absent individual economic activity.
- The rational basis test for "class of activities" makes it difficult to lose a Commerce Clause challenge if Congress phrases the statute to regulate an economic category.
- O'Connor's drafting guide critique is the key doctrinal objection: Lopez and Morrison may impose only a textual discipline, not a substantive limit.
- Scalia's N&P concurrence in Raich pre-figures the N&P debate in NFIB v. Sebelius -- and signals Scalia's willingness to expand federal power through the Necessary and Proper Clause even when individual activity is intrastate.
- Taylor confirms the class-of-activities approach in the Hobbs Act context; Thomas's lone dissent is a recurring note that the proper limit of the Necessary and Proper Clause has not been resolved.
