## Lecture Outline

### The Foundational Rule

The State Action Doctrine limits the 14th Amendment to government actions; private conduct is generally not covered unless one of the recognized exceptions applies *(p. 3)*. The rule cuts two ways: (1) the 14th Amendment applies to state and local government action, and (2) the federal government cannot be held liable under the 14th Amendment for private conduct *(p. 3)*. Constitutional protections apply only when there is state action — action by the government or an entity acting with significant government involvement *(p. 4)*. If the actor is purely private, the Constitution does not apply unless an exception applies *(p. 4)*.

The doctrine was established in the Civil Rights Cases (1883), which struck down the Civil Rights Act of 1875 (prohibiting private racial discrimination in public accommodations) on the ground that Section 1 of the 14th Amendment only restricts state action *(p. 5)*. Justice Bradley: "Individual invasion of individual rights is not the subject matter of the [14th] amendment" *(p. 5)*. Justice Harlan's influential dissent argued that public accommodations were "affected with a public interest" and that Congress had authority to legislate against private discrimination — a view that eventually shaped modern public accommodations law via the Commerce Clause (Heart of Atlanta, Ollie's BBQ) *(p. 5)*. The deck flags the legacy as setting the foundation for modern state-action doctrine and narrowing Reconstruction-era civil rights protections *(p. 5)*.

### Test 1: Public Function

A private entity is treated as a state actor if it performs a function traditionally and exclusively reserved to the state *(p. 6)*.

**State action found:**
- Private company running a town: Marsh v. Alabama *(p. 6)*.
- Private organization conducting elections: Terry v. Adams *(p. 6)*.

**State action not found:**
- Private utility companies regulating service: Jackson v. Metropolitan Edison Co. *(p. 6)*.
- Private shopping malls restricting speech: Hudgens v. NLRB *(p. 6)*.
- Private company operating a public-access TV channel: Manhattan Community Access Corp. v. Halleck (2019) — running TV channels is not traditionally an exclusive state function *(p. 7)*.

**Key principle:** The "traditionally and exclusively" standard is narrow. Heavy regulation, government licensing, or receipt of public funds does not automatically make an entity a state actor under this test.

### Test 2: Entanglement

State action exists if the government is significantly involved in, authorizes, or encourages private conduct *(p. 8)*.

**State action found:**
- Court enforcement of racially restrictive covenants: Shelley v. Kraemer *(p. 8)*.
- State leasing property to a discriminatory private entity: Burton v. Wilmington Parking Authority *(p. 8)*.

**State action not found:**
- State granting liquor licenses to discriminatory clubs: Moose Lodge No. 107 v. Irvis *(p. 8)*.
- NCAA disciplining a coach at a public university: NCAA v. Tarkanian *(p. 8)*.
- State-funded private nursing home discharging patients: Blum v. Yaretsky (1982) — state funding alone is not enough; there must be state coercion or control over the private decision *(p. 9)*.
- State-funded private school firing a teacher: Rendell-Baker v. Kohn (1982) — even extensive state funding does not transform a private entity into a state actor unless the government controls its decisions *(p. 9)*.

### Test 3: Entwinement

State action exists if a private entity is so pervasively entwined with the government that it functions as a state actor *(p. 10)*.

**State action found:**
- Brentwood Academy v. TSSAA (2001) — a high school athletic association made up of public school officials was "entwined" with the state *(p. 10)*.

**State action not found:**
- NCAA regulating university athletics: NCAA v. Tarkanian — NCAA was not entwined with the state *(p. 10)*.

### Test 4: Nexus and Attribution (Jackson v. Metropolitan Edison)

A private entity is a state actor only if there is a close nexus between the state and the challenged action and the private action is fairly attributable to the state *(p. 11)*. No state action where a private utility company terminated service — Jackson v. Metropolitan Edison — heavily regulated, but still private *(p. 11)*. The deck's exam tip: mere state regulation or licensing is not enough *(p. 11)*.

### Government-Created Corporations

If the government creates, owns, and controls a corporation, it may be treated as a state actor *(p. 12)*. Lebron v. National Railroad Passenger Corp. (1995): Amtrak was a state actor because it was created by the federal government and the government retained control over its structure *(p. 12)*. Exception: if the government sells control to private hands, it is no longer a state actor *(p. 12)*.

### No State Duty to Protect: DeShaney

Rule: the government has no affirmative duty to protect individuals from private harm unless it creates the danger or takes someone into custody *(p. 13)*.

DeShaney v. Winnebago County (1989): no state action where a county failed to protect a child from abuse by his father; failure to act is not state action — the government is only responsible if it affirmatively places someone in danger *(p. 13)*.

Two recognized exceptions to the DeShaney rule:
1. **Custody:** if the state takes custody (prisons, foster care), it must protect individuals *(p. 13)*.
2. **State-Created Danger:** when government actions increase the risk of private harm *(p. 16)*. A "Special Relationship" extension — beyond custody, when the state assumes responsibility for individual welfare — is also recognized in some circuits *(p. 16)*.

### 13th Amendment: No State Action Required

Unlike the 5th and 14th Amendments, the 13th Amendment (prohibiting slavery and involuntary servitude) applies to private individuals as well as the government *(p. 14)*. The Civil Rights Cases (1883) themselves acknowledged this distinction: while the 14th Amendment requires state action, the 13th Amendment applies to private conduct because it directly outlaws slavery *(p. 14)*. The 13th Amendment is unique in applying even when no state action exists *(p. 14)*.

### Section 5 of the 14th Amendment and the Congruence-and-Proportionality Test

Section 5: "The Congress shall have power to enforce, by appropriate legislation, the provisions of this article" *(p. 15)*. City of Boerne v. Flores (1997) established the "congruence and proportionality" test: Congress can only remedy or prevent constitutional violations, and legislation must be proportional to the identified harm; this test restricts state action, not the federal government (Gonzales v. O Centro Espirita Beneficente Uniao do Vegetal, 2006) *(p. 15)*. United States v. Morrison (2000) struck down portions of the Violence Against Women Act, holding that Congress cannot use Section 5 to regulate purely private conduct and reinforcing that Section 5 legislation must target state action *(p. 15)*. Key distinction: while the 13th Amendment reaches private conduct, Section 5 powers are limited to addressing state action *(p. 15)*.

### Modern Applications and Edge Cases

The deck flags several modern doctrinal points: private prisons remain state actors even when privatized (West v. Atkins, 1988); courts enforcing private agreements may constitute state action (Shelley v. Kraemer); using state procedures (prejudgment attachments) can convert private action to state action (Lugar v. Edmondson Oil, 1982) *(p. 16)*. Emerging issues include whether tech-platform decisions can be state action (courts generally say no) and the privatization of traditional government functions *(p. 16)*.

### The Five-Point Exam Checklist

The deck's five-point checklist *(p. 17)*:
1. Is the actor a government entity?
2. Is the private actor performing a public function?
3. Is there significant government involvement (entanglement)?
4. Is there entwinement (government and private action indistinguishable)?
5. Is there a close nexus making the action fairly attributable to the state?

If none apply, there is no state action and the Constitution does not apply *(p. 17)*.

### Final Thoughts

Bar Exam Tip: if in doubt, assume private conduct is not state action unless an exception applies *(p. 18)*. The 13th Amendment exception remains the principal carve-out — no state action required, applies to private conduct *(p. 18)*. State action is required for most constitutional claims, but not always *(p. 18)*.

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

**Bar exam framing:** if in doubt, assume private conduct is not state action unless an exception applies *(p. 18)*. The burden is on the party asserting state action to identify which test applies and why the result follows from the controlling case. The most tested traps: (1) state regulation or licensing is not entanglement *(p. 11)*; (2) government funding alone is not entanglement (Blum, Rendell-Baker) *(p. 9)*; (3) the public function test requires the function to be traditionally *and exclusively* governmental — most regulated industries do not qualify *(p. 6)*.

## Key Takeaways

- State action is a threshold requirement for 14th Amendment constitutional claims; private conduct is presumptively outside the Amendment's scope.
- Four overlapping tests: public function, entanglement, entwinement, nexus/attribution.
- The 13th Amendment's prohibition on slavery reaches private conduct -- a critical exception.
- DeShaney establishes no affirmative duty to protect; exceptions for custody and state-created danger apply in limited circumstances.
- Section 5 gives Congress enforcement authority but requires congruence and proportionality -- it cannot reach purely private conduct (Morrison).
