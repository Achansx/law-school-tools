## Professor Emphasis

### From source

The deck organizes the analysis around three overlapping frameworks: constitutional authority, statutory compliance under the WPR, and international law *(slide 22)*. The Constitution is presented as creating "deliberate tension" between Congress (Article I, Section 8's Declare War Clause) and the President (Article II's Commander in Chief Clause) *(slide 3)*. The Prize Cases is flagged as "The Leading Supreme Court Authority on Presidential War Powers" *(slide 5)*, with the Grier majority and Nelson dissent set up to be read against each other *(slides 6-7)*. WPR Section 2(c)'s three lawful triggers (declaration, specific authorization, national emergency from attack) are the statutory anchor and the deck notes that a preemptive Iran strike "does not fit any of these three categories" *(slide 10)*. The justiciability Catch-22 -- before action not ripe, during action political question, after action moot -- is explicitly diagrammed and used to explain why "war powers claims are virtually never adjudicated on the merits" *(slide 17)*. Article 2(4) of the UN Charter prohibits force, Article 51 carves a self-defense exception, and the Caroline Test governs anticipatory self-defense *(slides 19-20)*.

This page treats the Iran hypothetical as the organizing device through which the three frameworks are stress-tested in sequence. The framing of the President's "structural first-mover advantage" -- military action initiated, conducted, and completed before legal remedies arrive -- is editorial synthesis of the deck's distributed observations about appropriations lag, veto-override difficulty, and justiciability Catch-22 (slides 14, 15, 17). The packaging of Grier and Nelson as a paired teaching unit that "each permits and forbids different things" is editorial reading rather than a deck claim.

## Lecture Outline

### I. The Hypothetical Scenario

President Trump orders military strikes against Iran, citing rebuilding of Iran's nuclear weapons program, active support for terrorism against U.S. allies, and imminent threats to Israel and regional partners *(slide 2)*. Targets include nuclear facilities, Iranian military leadership, communications and command/control infrastructure, with civilian casualties resulting *(slide 2)*. The central legal question organizes the rest of the lecture: what legal authority does the President possess for this action? *(slide 2)*.

### II. The Constitutional Framework

The Constitution deliberately divides war powers between Congress and the President *(slide 3)*. Article I, Section 8 gives Congress the power to declare war, grant letters of marque and reprisal, make rules concerning captures, raise and support armies, provide and maintain a navy, and make rules for the regulation of the armed forces *(slide 3)*. Article II, Section 2 makes the President Commander in Chief of the Army and Navy, and of the state militias when called into federal service; the Take Care Clause (Article II, Section 3) adds an additional layer of executive responsibility *(slide 3)*.

Madison's Convention notes are cited for the proposition that the Framers deliberately rejected the original "make war" language to give Congress the power to "declare" war while allowing the President only to "repel sudden attacks" *(slide 4)*. That drafting history is the textual foundation for the congressional-primacy reading.

### III. Competing Constitutional Theories

Under the Congressional Primacy View, the Declare War Clause reserves to Congress the decision to initiate offensive military operations, and the deliberate "make war" to "declare war" substitution shows narrowing rather than a stylistic tweak *(slide 4)*. Under the Executive Primacy View, the Commander in Chief Clause grants the President broad authority over military operations and national defense, and OLC opinions across multiple administrations invoke historical practice -- Presidents have deployed forces abroad without congressional authorization over 200 times since the Founding *(slide 4)*.

### IV. The Prize Cases (1863): The Leading Constitutional Authority

The deck labels The Prize Cases "The Leading Supreme Court Authority on Presidential War Powers" *(slide 5)*. President Lincoln proclaimed a naval blockade of Southern ports in April 1861 -- an act of war under international law -- before Congress ratified his war measures in July 1861; ships captured before July challenged the blockade's validity *(slide 5)*.

Justice Grier's majority (5-4) held that the President "has no power to initiate or declare a war either against a foreign nation or a domestic State," but "if a war be made by invasion of a foreign nation, the President is not only authorized but bound to resist force by force. He does not initiate the war, but is bound to accept the challenge without waiting for any special legislative authority" *(slide 6)*. The proclamation of blockade is itself "official and conclusive evidence to the Court that a state of war existed," and the President "must determine what degree of force the crisis demands" *(slide 6)*.

Justice Nelson's dissent (joined by Chief Justice Taney, Catron, and Clifford) drew a sharper line: the President "does not possess the power under the Constitution to declare war or recognize its existence within the meaning of the law of nations . . . that power belongs exclusively to the Congress of the United States" *(slide 7)*. The President may use domestic-law authority to suppress insurrection and repel invasion, but only Congress can create a state of war in the international legal sense entitling the government to belligerent rights such as blockades and prize captures *(slide 7)*. Before congressional action, the President wages only a "personal war" against rebels *(slide 7)*.

Applied to the Iran hypothetical, The Prize Cases cuts both ways *(slide 8)*. The majority supports presidential authority to treat Iranian threats as a "challenge" the President is "bound to accept" and to determine the necessary degree of force *(slide 8)*. But The Prize Cases involved a response to actual armed attack (Fort Sumter), whereas the Iran hypothetical involves a preemptive/preventive strike against anticipated threats -- the deck stresses the Prize majority itself said the President "does not initiate the war" *(slide 8)*. The critical question: is a preemptive strike against a non-attacking nation equivalent to "repelling invasion" or "accepting a challenge"? *(slide 8)*.

### V. The War Powers Resolution (1973): Statutory Framework

The WPR grew out of the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution (1964) functioning as a "blank check," Truman's Korea deployment without any congressional authorization, and Nixon's Cambodia expansion heightening concerns about an "imperial presidency" *(slide 9)*. Passed over Nixon's veto on November 7, 1973, 50 U.S.C. sections 1541 to 1548 attempted to restore congressional primacy *(slide 9)*. Nixon's veto message warned the WPR would "take away, by a mere legislative act, authorities which the President has properly exercised under the Constitution for almost 200 years" *(slide 9)*.

**WPR Section 2(c): Three Lawful Triggers.** Presidential power to introduce forces into hostilities is exercised only pursuant to (1) a declaration of war (last used December 1941), (2) specific statutory authorization such as the AUMFs of 1991, 2001, and 2002, or (3) a national emergency created by attack upon the United States, its territories or possessions, or its armed forces *(slide 10)*. The deck explicitly notes a preemptive Iran strike "does not fit any of these three categories" -- no attack on U.S. territory or forces, no AUMF, no declaration *(slide 10)*.

**Procedural Provisions.** Section 3 requires consultation "in every possible instance" before introducing forces into hostilities and regular consultation thereafter *(slide 12)*. Section 4 requires a 48-hour report when troops are introduced into hostilities, onto foreign territory equipped for combat, or in substantially enlarged numbers *(slide 12)*. Section 5(b) imposes a 60-day clock: the President must withdraw within 60 days unless Congress declares war, authorizes the force, extends the clock, or is physically unable to meet, extendable to 90 days for safe withdrawal *(slide 12)*.

**Executive Branch Position.** OLC (1984) treats Section 2(c) as "merely a 'declaratory statement of policy'" and "not a legally binding definition of Presidential authority," and treats Section 5(c)'s legislative veto as unconstitutional under INS v. Chadha *(slide 12)*. No President has accepted the WPR's constitutionality, but many have voluntarily complied in practice, reporting "consistent with" rather than "pursuant to" the WPR *(slide 11)*.

**Historical Pattern.** WPR invoked or acknowledged: Lebanon (1982-83), Gulf War I (1991, AUMF), Afghanistan (2001, AUMF), Iraq (2002, AUMF) *(slide 11)*. Possibly ignored or circumvented: Sudan cruise missiles (1998), Kosovo bombing (1999, which exceeded 60 days), Libya (2011, characterized as "not hostilities"), Syria Tomahawks (2017, 2018), Soleimani strike (2020) *(slide 11)*. No formal declaration of war since 1941; the 2001 AUMF has been used for operations in 20+ countries far beyond its original scope; lower courts have found the issue to be a "political question" *(slide 11)*.

### VI. Constitutional Validity of the WPR

The case for constitutionality rests on the Declare War Clause, the Necessary and Proper Clause's authority over procedural framework, the appropriations and army-raising powers, and the Framers' intent on congressional control over war initiation *(slide 13)*. The case against asserts that Section 2(c) unconstitutionally limits inherent Commander in Chief powers, that the 60-day automatic termination intrudes on executive warmaking, that Congress cannot micromanage military operations (Chadha concerns), and that 200+ years of presidential practice has established constitutional custom *(slide 13)*. No court has ever ruled on the WPR's constitutionality *(slide 13)*.

### VII. Other Congressional Checks

Congress retains non-judicial tools to check presidential war-making *(slide 14)*. Power of the purse: Congress can cut off funding, making continued expenditures potentially illegal and creating grounds for impeachment *(slide 14)*. Impeachment: the deck quotes Judge Leavitt in Ex parte Vallandigham -- "the president is guided solely by his own judgment and discretion, and is only amenable for an abuse of his authority by impeachment" *(slide 14)*. House impeaches; Senate convicts.

The Yemen episode (2019) illustrates the limits: Congress voted to deauthorize U.S. military activity in Yemen under WPR Section 5(c), Trump vetoed, and Congress lacked votes to override *(slide 14)*. Trump v. United States (2024) complicates the picture further: absolute immunity for "core" constitutional powers may shield the President from criminal prosecution for war-related decisions, since Commander in Chief authority over military operations may be a "core" executive function *(slide 14)*.

The "too late" problem: congressional remedies typically cannot prevent a President from initiating military action -- they can only respond after the fact, since spending cuts take time, impeachment is lengthy, and military momentum creates pressure to "support the troops" *(slide 15)*.

### VIII. Justiciability Barriers: Why Courts Stay Out

War powers claims are virtually never resolved on the merits because courts deploy every available procedural doctrine to avoid adjudication *(slide 17)*.

Under the political question doctrine articulated in Baker v. Carr (1962), courts will not adjudicate questions where there is textual commitment to a coordinate branch, lack of judicially discoverable standards, impossibility of deciding without an initial non-judicial policy determination, need for unquestioning adherence to a political decision already made, or potential for embarrassment from multifarious pronouncements by various departments *(slide 16)*. War initiation fits multiple Baker factors.

Standing also forecloses most challenges. Campbell v. Clinton (D.C. Cir. 2000) held that individual members of Congress lack standing when they retain legislative remedies -- the power to cut off funding, pass laws forbidding force, or impeach *(slide 17)*.

Ripeness under Dellums v. Bush (D.D.C. 1990) requires a "constitutional impasse": a dispute is not ripe until a majority of Congress asserts its authority and the Executive shows commitment to definitive military action *(slide 17)*. Combined with mootness after the fact, ripeness creates the Catch-22: before action not ripe, during action political question, after action moot *(slide 17)*. The practical result: "war powers claims are virtually never adjudicated on the merits" *(slide 17)*.

**Potential Paths to Judicial Review.** The deck flags four theoretical off-ramps from the Catch-22: (1) a congressional institutional suit where a majority of Congress asserts authority (Dellums hints this might be justiciable); (2) a service member challenge based on personal liberty interests, though usually dismissed on other grounds; (3) a collateral criminal prosecution in which a service member refuses an order and challenges the underlying legality, since criminal defendants have automatic standing; (4) enemy combatant habeas review on the Hamdi v. Rumsfeld model *(slide 18)*. None of these paths has successfully resulted in a merits ruling against presidential war powers *(slide 18)*.

### IX. International Law: The UN Charter Framework

Article 2(4) of the UN Charter prohibits the threat or use of force against the territorial integrity or political independence of any state, with two exceptions: UN Security Council authorization under Chapter VII, and self-defense under Article 51 if an armed attack occurs *(slide 19)*.

**Anticipatory Self-Defense.** The Caroline Test (1837), articulated by Secretary of State Daniel Webster, requires that the necessity of self-defense be "instant, overwhelming, leaving no choice of means, and no moment of deliberation," and that the response be proportionate *(slide 20)*. Two elements: necessity (imminent threat, peaceful alternatives impractical) and proportionality (force limited to neutralizing the threat) *(slide 20)*. The OLC (2002) formulation evolved the imminence factors -- probability of attack, likelihood probability will increase, whether diplomatic alternatives are practical, and magnitude of potential harm -- and asserted that a threat "may be imminent even if distant" if its realization is "certain and inevitable" *(slide 20)*.

**Applied to the Iran Hypothetical.** For legality *(slide 21)*: anticipatory self-defense against WMD threat; Iranian nuclear program creating "certain and inevitable" danger; Iranian terrorism as ongoing armed attack; collective self-defense of allies such as Israel; proportionate response to specific threats. Against legality *(slide 21)*: no armed attack on the United States has occurred; "preventive" (distant threat) versus "preemptive" (imminent); diplomatic alternatives not exhausted; targeting leadership exceeds proportionate response; civilian casualties raise jus in bello concerns.

**Jus in bello.** Even if jus ad bellum is satisfied, the law of armed conflict imposes additional limits *(slide 21)*: distinction (military objectives only; civilians may not be targeted), proportionality (incidental civilian harm not excessive relative to military advantage), necessity (only force required for military objectives), and the contested legality of assassinating leadership.

### X. The Fundamental Tension

The deck closes with the question whether "law can constrain war" or whether the Constitution is not a "suicide pact" that prevents a President from protecting the nation *(slide 23)*. The law remains unsettled precisely because courts have avoided ruling on the merits *(slide 22)*. The President retains a structural first-mover advantage: military action can typically be initiated, conducted, and completed before legal remedies become available.

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

The Iran hypothetical is the organizing device for the entire lecture. Students should be able to work through (1) whether the strike falls within Prize Cases authority (response to attack versus initiation), (2) whether any of the three WPR Section 2(c) triggers are satisfied (no declaration, no AUMF, no attack on U.S. territory), (3) why courts would likely refuse to adjudicate on standing, ripeness, and political question grounds, and (4) whether anticipatory self-defense under Article 51 can be stretched to cover a preemptive strike against a distant nuclear threat.

Additional discussion prompts: the 60-day clock scenario (if the President commences operations without authorization, what happens on day 61, and can Congress actually enforce the WPR); the Caroline Test in the WMD era (has imminence evolved sufficiently to justify strikes against nations merely developing nuclear weapons, and is the OLC 2002 framework defensible); and the Yemen veto problem (if Congress passes a WPR resolution and the President vetoes it, is there any effective check, and what does that reveal about the statute's design). The overarching prompt is the Catch-22 itself: is it a feature or a bug of the constitutional system that war powers claims are essentially non-justiciable, and what mechanisms (if any) effectively check presidential war-making.

## Key Takeaways

1. The Constitution deliberately divides war powers, creating a zone of contested authority between the Commander in Chief power and the Declare War Clause; the text supports both primacy readings.
2. The Prize Cases authorizes presidential response to actual attacks but does not authorize initiating offensive war without congressional authorization; preemption against a non-attacking sovereign is not what Grier wrote about.
3. WPR Section 2(c) identifies three lawful triggers, and a preemptive strike on Iran fits none of them, yet no court has ever enforced this.
4. The WPR's constitutionality remains unsettled; no court has ruled on it, and no President has accepted it as binding on Commander in Chief authority.
5. Congressional checks (funding cutoffs, impeachment) are politically difficult to deploy once military action has begun, and Yemen (2019) illustrates the override problem.
6. Standing, ripeness, and the political question doctrine combine to make war powers claims virtually non-justiciable; the Catch-22 is the practical result.
7. The President has a structural first-mover advantage: military action can typically be initiated, conducted, and completed before legal remedies become available.
8. International law (UN Charter Article 2(4) plus Article 51) requires an armed attack for self-defense; anticipatory self-defense under Article 51 must satisfy the Caroline Test of imminence, necessity, and proportionality, and jus in bello constraints apply independently.
