## Lecture Outline

**The Architecture of Black Subjugation**

The deck inventories the legal mechanisms that sustained racial hierarchy: Prigg v. Pennsylvania (1842) upholding the Fugitive Slave Act; statutes such as the Missouri Compromise permitting admission of slave states; Dred Scott denying citizenship to persons of African descent (even free ones); debt slavery in some Southern States during the late 19th and early 20th centuries; federal housing programs that reinforced private segregation; restrictive definitions of state action; neutering of the privileges and immunities clause; separate but equal; and failure to enforce the amendments and the withdrawal of Union troops after 1876 *(slide 2)*. In 1883, the Supreme Court struck down provisions of CRA 1875 as unconstitutional, asking where Congress gets the power to do anything about private exercises of freedom like deciding who comes to your inn or who gets to sit at your barbecue *(slide 6)*. The deck notes that an expansive understanding of commerce might have helped, but "this isn't really about commerce or increasing GDP," and the era was the Dewitt / Paul / Dagenhart understanding of commerce *(slide 6)*. The privileges and immunities clause was elided because of Slaughterhouse and Cruikshank *(slide 6)*.

**The Civil Rights Act of 1964: Commerce Clause Strategy**

By 1964, the deck inventories what was still unfixed: recalcitrant states; hostility to persons of African descent that did not amount to slavery; denial of commercial services even to those who had money to pay; and restrictive covenants that ran with the land and prevented sale or ownership *(slide 7)*. The deck poses the strategic questions: how does Congress cover itself in enacting CRA 1964, how does CRA 1964 attempt to define interstate commerce for purposes of hotels like Heart of Atlanta, does it require a finding that there is an effect on interstate commerce, and what about for restaurants -- with the answer being "proxies" *(slide 8)*.

**Heart of Atlanta Motel v. United States (1964)**

Clark's opinion distinguished the 1875 Act on the ground that the commerce proxy may not have been a good fit in 1875 but is good now, and noted there was no stare decisis problem because cases at the time didn't really consider the commerce argument *(slide 9)*. Private segregation discourages interstate travel by Negroes, which is commerce, and the deck pointedly asks whether economy-maximization is a valid theory of Congressional authority *(slide 9)*. The test: Congress just needs a rational basis to find that racial discrimination by motels affects commerce, and Congress may choose reasonable and appropriate means to eliminate that evil *(slide 10)*.

**Katzenbach v. McClung (1964)**

Ollie's Barbecue in Birmingham let Black employees serve and Black takeout customers buy, but refused Black sit-down customers *(slide 12)*. The deck describes the deck's own treatment with characteristic Chandler bite ("It's like they thought black people had COVID!") *(slide 12)*. Clark's test: a covered activity exerts substantial economic effect on interstate commerce; an activity is outside the commerce power only if it is completely within a particular state, does not affect other states, and is not necessary to interfere for executing some general power of government -- and the deck asks the rhetorical "Is ANYTHING outside commerce power?", noting the additional rational basis test *(slide 13)*. The conclusive-presumption issue formalizes this: if predictive power is only slightly diminished by simplification (eliminating or consolidating variables), Congress may make the simplification subject to rational basis *(slide 14)*. This is the proxy technique that Rehnquist will later challenge in Lopez (1995) -- an editor's cross-reference not made by the deck itself.

**Douglas and Goldberg Concurrences**

Both concurred but wanted the opinion grounded in Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment, and the deck flags this as worth returning to when reading (a) Eleventh Amendment cases and (b) state action *(slide 11)*. The deck also flags the Thirteenth Amendment "badges and incidents" theory as an alternative *(slide 11)*. This page reads the differing doctrinal bases as mattering for the scope of available remedies in subsequent cases -- an editorial gloss that goes beyond the slide.

**Reasoning About the Commerce Nexus**

The page distinguishes the 1875 Act from CRA 1964 on the ground that the commercial nexus argument was not available in 1875 given the pre-New Deal understanding of commerce but became available after the New Deal expansion *(slide 9)*. Whether this is a doctrinal distinction or post-hoc rationalization depends on how one reads Wickard and McClung -- an editorial framing that goes beyond what the deck supplies.

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

**Rational Basis and the Scope of the Power:** The deck's "Is ANYTHING outside commerce power?" rhetorical is the lecture's main pressure on Clark's rational basis test *(slide 13)*. This page extends the question by comparing it to Breyer's institutional-competence argument in Lopez -- a connection not drawn by the deck.

**The Section 5 Alternative:** The Douglas/Goldberg concurrence slide flags Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment and the Thirteenth Amendment's "badges and incidents" theory as alternative grounds *(slide 11)*. The Slaughterhouse Cases and the Civil Rights Cases' state-action limitation as the proximate cause for the Commerce Clause choice is editorial doctrinal history, not deck content.

**The Conclusive Presumption Problem:** The conclusive-presumption framework -- if predictive power is only slightly diminished by simplification, Congress may simplify subject to rational basis -- is given as a graphical complex-vs-simpler-model contrast *(slide 14)*. Whether Congress is constitutionally permitted to use such categorical proxies, and what limits exist on proxy legislation, is what Lopez's later skepticism about classification schemes that eliminate the jurisdictional element addresses -- an editor's forward link not drawn by these slides.

## Key Takeaways

- The Civil Rights Act of 1964 rested on the Commerce Clause rather than the Reconstruction Amendments because of the state-action limitation on the 14th Amendment established in the Civil Rights Cases (1883).
- Heart of Atlanta and Katzenbach v. McClung held that Congress needs only a rational basis to find that private racial discrimination affects interstate commerce; the aggregation principle applies.
- The "conclusive presumption" technique allows Congress to use categorical proxies (buying food in interstate commerce) rather than particularized effect findings -- a technique later challenged in Lopez.
- Douglas and Goldberg concurrences argued for grounding the holdings in Section 5 and the 13th Amendment, which would have avoided the doctrinal vulnerability that Rehnquist later exploited.
- The question Chandler poses throughout: is commerce the right constitutional basis for civil rights law, or is it a structural workaround for doctrinal failures in the Reconstruction Amendments?
