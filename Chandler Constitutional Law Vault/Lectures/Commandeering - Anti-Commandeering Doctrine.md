# Commandeering - Anti-Commandeering Doctrine

<!-- 2026-05-26: convention rollout (slide anchors + Professor Emphasis split) -->

## Professor Emphasis

### From source

The deck frames the doctrine as a non-textual limit: New York v United States holds that the Tenth Amendment confirms but does not source the limit, since the Tenth is "essentially a tautology," and the slide flags the puzzle of writing an amendment that "weaken[s] the text but then provide[s] the states with even more protection" *(slide 11)*. The O'Connor Accountability Theme passage warns that when the federal government directs states to regulate, state officials bear the brunt of public disapproval while federal officials remain insulated from electoral consequences -- and the deck asks what empirical basis supports that prediction *(slide 12)*. The Anti-Stevens Theme reads federalism as a protection of individuals rather than states-as-abstract-entities, channeling Federalist 51 and Gregory v. Ashcroft's "promise of liberty" framing *(slides 13, 41)*. The competing Stevens Theme treats state-by-state representation in Congress as the principal Framer-chosen safeguard of federalism, making judicial limits unnecessary *(slide 27)*. The deck repeatedly asks "How much does [Rehnquist] accomplish in, as a practical matter, rolling back Garcia?" *(slide 3)*. Three categories of federal action structure the doctrine: direct commandeering of state legislatures (barred by New York), commandeering of state executive officials (barred by Printz), and permissible coercion through conditional spending (Dole) or cooperative federalism schemes (Hodel) *(slide 4)*.

### Editor's framing

This page treats the Garcia/National League of Cities oscillation as the doctrinal pivot for the entire commandeering line and characterizes Blackmun's flip in Garcia as the move that triggered the eventual Rehnquist-court response -- a narrative shape that goes beyond the deck's compressed "Blackmun changes his mind!" *(slide 3)*. The synthesis that the line between coercion and valid inducement runs through the entire course is editorial packaging. The taxonomy of regulatory modes at the end of the outline (criminal/civil, taxation, spending/market entry, conditional spending) draws on the deck's "Modes of federal regulation" slide but is rearranged for narrative continuity *(slide 46)*.

## Lecture Outline

**Background: National League of Cities to Garcia**

Congress has broad power to regulate commerce, but questions arise when states themselves engage in commercial activities such as hiring people *(slide 3)*. National League of Cities v Usery (1976), in a Rehnquist opinion, held that the Tenth Amendment / structure bars Congress from directly regulating states using the commerce power at least as to "attributes of sovereignty" *(slide 3)*. Garcia v San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority (1985) overruled National League of Cities -- "Blackmun changes his mind!" -- and held the Tenth Amendment does not bar Congress from directly regulating states using the commerce power, even for attributes of sovereignty *(slide 3)*. Blackmun bought in to the political structure / Stevens Theme: the political process will protect federalism and the judiciary is seldom needed *(slide 3)*. Rehnquist dissented predicting "this will not stand," and the deck asks how much he ultimately accomplished in rolling Garcia back *(slide 3)*.

**Simple Version of the Anti-Commandeering Rule**

The simple version: the federal government cannot force states to legislate in a certain way (New York v United States); it can coax through conditional spending; it can condition state regulation of areas subject to federal regulation on state regulations being acceptable (Hodel); and it cannot force state officials to aid in federal law enforcement (Printz) -- though states can probably force their own officials to do so *(slide 4)*. Older cases such as Testa v Katt (1947) and FERC v Mississippi (1982) remain in tension with modern doctrine *(slide 4)*.

**Commandeering State Legislatures: New York v United States (1992)**

The Low-Level Radioactive Waste Policy Act let states enter into regional compacts (approved by Congress) that would discriminate against non-compact states *(slide 7)*. The 1986 amendments allowed escalating surcharges against out-of-state waste from 1986 to 1992, exclusion after 1992, and -- if a state lacked a private-party mechanism -- a take-title provision making the state owner of the waste *(slide 8)*. New York began siting its own waste plants but residents of Cortland County (near Ithaca) and Allegany County (north of the PA border) "went NIMBY," so rather than shove radioactive waste down constituents' throats, New York sued *(slide 9)*.

Justice O'Connor held that the feds may not "commandeer," and the deck flags that O'Connor's argument is non-textual -- an attempt to reconcile 200 years of commerce clause jurisprudence *(slide 10)*. Congress may still coax through conditional spending (Dole) or conditional commandeering (Hodel) *(slide 10)*. The deck asks what O'Connor's favorite Federalist paper is *(slide 10)*. The O'Connor accountability theme is explicit: where the Federal Government directs the States to regulate, state officials bear the brunt of public disapproval while federal officials who devised the regulatory program remain insulated from electoral ramifications *(slide 12)*. The Anti-Stevens Theme adds the structural rationale: the Constitution divides authority between federal and state governments for the protection of individuals, and federalism secures to citizens the liberties that derive from the diffusion of sovereign power *(slide 13)*.

White, Stevens, and Blackmun dissented on Garcia political-process grounds: the limitation on the Commerce Clause to protect "the States as States" is one of process rather than result *(slide 14)*. Stevens separately argued from constitutional history that the Articles of Confederation gave the federal government the power to issue commands to the States, that the Constitution enhanced rather than diminished federal power, and that the Tenth Amendment is nothing but a truism -- though he got only one vote *(slide 15)*.

**Commandeering State Executive Officials: Printz v United States (1997)**

The Brady Handgun Violence Prevention Act (1993) required Chief Law Enforcement Officers to conduct background checks on firearm purchasers while a federal system was being developed *(slide 21)*. The Supreme Court held 5-4 that the Act's interim provisions violated the Tenth Amendment: the federal government cannot commandeer state officials to enforce federal regulatory programs *(slide 22)*.

The reasoning rests on three foundations. First, a historical and structural argument: the Constitution establishes dual sovereignty, the Tenth Amendment reaffirms that powers not delegated remain with the states, and historical practice does not support federal commandeering of state officials *(slide 23)*. Second, the anti-commandeering principle picks up the O'Connor accountability theme -- compelling state officers to administer federal laws blurs lines of accountability *(slide 23)*. Third, a practical concern that shifting administrative burdens to states creates inefficiency and undermines accountability *(slide 23)*.

The deck poses a set of pointed questions: how does the majority distinguish early federal imposition of obligations on state courts, what does Scalia do with the First Congress argument, how stable is Scalia's adjudicatory-vs-executive distinction at the state level, what does he do with the Federalist Papers, which Federalist paper is cited most, can the separation-of-powers theory be explained, and who has the better of the argument on the meaning of Federalist 27 *(slide 24)*. The deck flags Scalia's textualism question -- whether Scalia is being textualist and whether he accepts non-textual arguments in Part III -- and includes a forward link comparing Scalia in Printz to Justice Douglas in Griswold *(slide 25)*. Thomas concurred separately to argue that regulation of intrastate handgun sales is not regulation of commerce, to raise unmentioned Second Amendment concerns, and to flag future revisitation *(slide 28)*. The Stevens / Souter / Ginsburg / Breyer dissent's Part II argument is historical, political-theoretical, and textual *(slide 26)*. The deck flags that Breyer and Stevens separately do things that "will drive some people berserk" -- looking to foreign and comparative material *(slide 26)*.

**NCAA v Murphy (2018): Expansion to Prohibitions on Repeals**

PASPA made it unlawful for governmental entities to sponsor, operate, advertise, or promote sports gambling, or to authorize it "by law or compact" *(slide 16)*. After New Jersey's 2012 affirmative-authorization law was struck down by the Third Circuit *(slide 17)*, New Jersey instead repealed much of its prohibition on sports gambling, but only for casinos and racetracks. The Court held that PASPA does prohibit New Jersey from repealing its laws barring sports gambling, because a prohibition on a repeal is no different than requiring a state to make a law *(slide 18)*. Alito's opinion identified three purposes of the anti-commandeering doctrine: preserving liberty, preserving accountability, and preventing the federal government from shifting costs of legislation to the states, concluding that "a more direct affront to a state's sovereignty is not easy to imagine" *(slide 54)*. The majority distinguished prior cases: South Carolina v Baker did not order states to do anything, Reno v Condon applied to states and private citizens alike, Hodel permitted states to comply but did not force them to, and FERC was decided before New York and Printz and was de minimis anyway *(slide 55)*. Ginsburg, Sotomayor, and Breyer dissented, mostly on severability, and reinforced that under Gonzalez v. Raich Congress could regulate sports gambling directly *(slide 56)*.

**Tricky Cases**

Testa v Katt (1947) addressed Congress's emergency post-WW2 price controls, which gave state courts concurrent jurisdiction over disputes thereunder; Rhode Island refused to enforce the "foreign" statute and the Supreme Court (9-0) said it could not refuse *(slide 34)*. The opinion cites Prigg but makes no effort to distinguish it *(slide 34)*. FERC v Mississippi (1982) (Blackmun, J.) addressed PURPA, which required state administrative agencies to adjudicate disputes about compliance and required state-regulated utilities to consider energy-reducing rates *(slide 35)*. The Court upheld the statute on the theory that forced adjudication is just Testa v Katt and that since the feds could have preempted states entirely, requiring procedures is OK (Hodel-style); core sovereignty was not threatened *(slide 36)*. Reno v Condon (1994) (9-0) (Rehnquist, C.J.) upheld the Driver's Privacy Protection Act -- triggered by the stalking death of actress Rebecca Schaeffer whose home address was obtained from California DMV -- because it regulates state DMVs and their personnel alongside the equivalent private conduct *(slide 37)*.

**South Carolina v Baker and Gregory v Ashcroft**

South Carolina v Baker (1988) addressed TEFRA's elimination of the federal income tax exemption for interest earned on state bearer bonds *(slide 39)*. Brennan's opinion applied Garcia's structural-not-substantive limits: the State had not alleged it was deprived of any right to participate in the national political process or singled out in a way that left it politically isolated and powerless *(slide 40)*. The deck flags no commandeering where the state can declare something legal, then change its law when the feds outlaw it, and asks whether Murphy changes this *(slide 40)*. Gregory v Ashcroft (1991) (O'Connor, J.) achieved a saving construction of the ADEA so that it did not cover state judges *(slide 41)*. The deck cites the O'Connor formulation channeling Federalist 51: "In the tension between federal and state power lies the promise of liberty" *(slide 41)*. The White/Stevens dissent restored the Baker/Garcia political-process framing -- the deck asks whether this is the restoration of the Darby Tenth-Amendment-as-truism theory *(slide 42)*.

**Conditional Spending and Modes of Federal Regulation**

The deck taxonomizes federal regulatory tools: making something a federal crime or civil violation (need an enumerated power or N+P; may subject states themselves if evenhanded); imposing a tax (needs to be an actual tax for general welfare; may include income on state-issued obligations under Baker); spending money on stuff (no enumerated power needed; just general welfare plus no Tenth Amendment structural violation); fed entry into the market itself; and conditional spending (withhold money unless the state complies; need only the four Dole conditions plus NFIB non-coercion) *(slide 46)*.

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

**Immigration Commandeering Hypo:** Senator Smith proposes making it a crime for a state law enforcement official not to check an immigration database when arresting a person and to fail to detain them on a "red flag," based on the political calculation that small Western state Senators plus House conservatives and some Democrats can pass it *(slide 29)*. The deck asks whether the dissenters in Printz would permit enforcement *(slide 29)*. The analysis: this is regulating states, not requiring them to regulate others; the thin-distinction worry is resolved because the law regulates more than just states (private parties too), so "formal overbreadth saves federalism" -- this is really Garcia v San Antonio Metropolitan Transit, and the case where federal law regulates ONLY a state is reserved *(slide 30)*.

**Conditional Spending and Commandeering Tension:** The Edward Byrne Memorial Justice Assistance Grant Program provides more than $250 million in federal funding for state and local criminal justice efforts, conditioned on compliance with all other applicable federal laws *(slide 57)*. Under Trump, the administration withheld 100% of funding from New York and other states unless the state certified it would comply with federal law prohibiting restrictions on the communication of citizenship and alien status with federal immigration authorities, provided release dates of incarcerated noncitizens, and afforded federal immigration officers access *(slide 58)*. New York's initial argument was commandeering; the response: the law does not compel state governments to administer any federal regulatory program but only prohibits them from directly restricting voluntary exchange of immigration information *(slide 59)*. The district court concluded that Murphy's reasoning required holding the law facially violative of the Tenth Amendment because the statute prevents states from adopting immigration policies contrary to the federal government's preferred policies *(slide 60)*. The Circuit Court distinguished Murphy on the ground that a commandeering challenge depends on pertinent authority "reserved to the States" -- not so obvious in the immigration context where the federal government holds "broad" and "preeminent" power *(slide 61)*. Plaintiffs could not claim the loss represents so significant a percentage of their annual budget as to cross the line from pressure to coercion -- New York's $8.879 million 2017 Byrne award is less than 0.1% of the state's $152.3 billion budget, a smaller percentage loss than in Dole *(slide 64)*. A circuit conflict has formed *(slide 65)*.

## Key Takeaways

- The anti-commandeering rule is structural and non-textual: the Tenth Amendment confirms the limit but does not source it in text.
- Two categories of commandeering are categorically barred: directing state legislatures to enact federal regulatory programs (New York) and conscripting state executive officials to administer federal law enforcement (Printz).
- The line between impermissible commandeering and permissible cooperative federalism runs through three permitted alternatives: conditional spending (Dole), cooperative regulation where states may opt out by accepting federal direct regulation (Hodel), and direct federal regulation of states and private parties equally (Reno v Condon).
- Murphy extended the doctrine to prohibit Congress from requiring states to maintain criminal prohibitions on the books, treating a mandatory non-repeal as functionally equivalent to a command to legislate.
- The Stevens/Blackmun political-process theory is always available as a dissenting baseline: if Congress represents the states structurally, judicial enforcement of categorical limits is unnecessary and the courts should defer to legislative judgments.
