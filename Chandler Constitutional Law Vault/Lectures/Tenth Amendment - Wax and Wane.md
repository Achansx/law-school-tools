# Tenth Amendment - Wax and Wane

## Professor Emphasis

### From source

The deck's subtitle delivers the thesis up front: "Six Cases, Seven Decades, One Incoherent Doctrine" *(slide 1)*. The doctrinal-journey slide presents the six landmark cases as a pendulum swing -- from commandeering state courts (Testa, 1947) to prohibiting commandeering of state legislatures (Murphy, 2018) *(slide 2)*. The Garcia incoherence is the lecture's spine: Garcia held that the fundamental federalism limitation is "one of process rather than one of result," while New York, Printz, and Murphy each impose substantive, judicially-enforced limits regardless of political-process outcomes -- positions the deck calls "logically incompatible," and that "Garcia was never explicitly overruled, yet its reasoning is contradicted by three subsequent landmark cases" *(slide 10)*. The deck also poses the Testa Problem on its own slide: why can Congress commandeer state judges (Testa) but not state sheriffs (Printz)? Scalia's textual answer points to the Supremacy Clause's mention of "Judges," and the deck flags this as a distinction critics call principled-less *(slide 7)*. The preemption puzzle slide arrays three options -- direct federal ban (valid), state-authorization prohibition (invalid commandeering), DMV-data regulation (valid general applicability) -- and asks why the Constitution requires the heavier hand *(slide 11)*. The three-models slide closes the diagnostic: truism (Darby/Garcia), substantive (New York/Printz/Murphy), and hybrid -- "we operate under all three simultaneously" *(slide 13)*. The script also stresses the script-specific deeper point that Reno's market-participant logic, if taken seriously, would swallow much of what Garcia purported to protect through political process *(p. 6)*.

### Editor's framing

This page treats the lecture as fundamentally about doctrinal incoherence rather than doctrinal evolution, and packages the six cases as a single argument that the Court "operates under all three simultaneously." The framing of the NFIB spending-power point as an "anti-commandeering analog" extends the deck's brief "Can Congress condition funds to achieve what it cannot command?" question into a positive doctrinal claim *(slide 14)*. The Reno-boundary-problem framing and the Murphy-paradox synthesis in the hypotheticals section are page-author packaging rather than language the deck supplies.

## Lecture Outline

### Testa v Katt (1947)

**The Holding.** State courts MUST enforce valid federal claims when they have jurisdiction over similar state-law claims; the Supremacy Clause compels state judicial cooperation *(slide 3)*. State courts are not free to refuse enforcement of petitioners' claims, and federal law is the policy of every state, binding upon its judges *(slide 3)*.

**The Tension.** This case COMMANDS state courts to enforce federal law, yet later cases hold Congress cannot command state legislatures or executives *(slide 3)*. Why can the Constitution conscript judges but not legislators? The lecture identifies this as an unresolved problem -- the Testa Problem -- that Scalia addresses in Printz but does not fully resolve *(slide 7)*.

### Garcia v San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority (1985)

**The Holding (Blackmun, J., 5-4).** Overruling National League of Cities (1976), the Court holds that the "traditional governmental function" test for Tenth Amendment immunity from federal commerce regulation is unworkable *(slide 4)*. Federalism is protected by the political process -- the structure of Congress itself, with senators and representatives answerable to state constituents -- not by judicial limits on federal power *(slide 4)*. As Blackmun put it, the fundamental limitation is one of process rather than one of result *(slide 4)*.

**The Incoherence.** If the political process alone protects federalism, why did the Court recognize substantive anti-commandeering limits in New York and Printz? Garcia says no judicial enforcement; those cases say yes *(slide 10)*. Rehnquist's dissent proved prophetic, predicting that the principle of National League of Cities would in time again command the support of a majority of the Court *(slide 4)*. He was right -- but Garcia was never overruled *(slide 10)*.

### New York v United States (1992)

**The Holding (O'Connor, J., 6-3).** Congress cannot compel states to enact or administer a federal regulatory program *(slide 5)*. The Low-Level Radioactive Waste Policy Amendments Act required states to "take title" to radioactive waste or regulate according to federal standards; the take-title provision -- offering states a choice between two unconstitutional options -- is itself unconstitutional *(slide 5)*.

**Federalism Protects Liberty, Not State Sovereignty.** The Constitution divides authority between federal and state governments for the protection of individuals *(slide 5)*. Anti-commandeering doctrine is not about state government as an end in itself -- it is about preventing the federal government from using state legislatures as instruments to circumvent accountability to voters.

**The Tension with Garcia.** Just seven years after Garcia held the political process is the only federalism safeguard, the Court imposes a substantive, judicially-enforced limit *(slide 5)*. How can both be right? The lecture identifies this as the fundamental doctrinal incoherence *(slide 10)*.

### Printz v United States (1997)

**The Holding (Scalia, J., 5-4).** Congress cannot conscript state executive officers to administer federal programs *(slide 6)*. The Brady Handgun Violence Prevention Act required local chief law enforcement officers (sheriffs) to conduct background checks on handgun purchasers; this is unconstitutional *(slide 6)*. The Federal Government may neither issue directives requiring the States to address particular problems, nor command the States' officers to administer or enforce a federal regulatory program *(slide 6)*.

**Extending New York.** New York prohibited commandeering state legislatures; Printz extends this to state executives, holding that Congress cannot circumvent that prohibition by conscripting the States' officers directly *(slide 6)*. The no-case-by-case weighing: it matters not whether policymaking is involved, and no weighing of burdens or benefits is necessary *(slide 6)*.

**The Testa Problem.** Scalia's answer: the Supremacy Clause's text explicitly mentions "Judges," implying a specific textual duty for state courts but not for other state officials *(slide 7)*. Critics call this a distinction without a principled difference *(slide 7)*. The lecture leaves the Testa tension unresolved as an honest doctrinal gap *(slide 14)*.

### Reno v Condon (2000)

**The Holding (Rehnquist, C.J., unanimous).** The Driver's Privacy Protection Act, which restricts state DMVs from selling personal information from driver's license records, is valid *(slide 8)*. Congress is regulating states as the owners of databases -- as market participants rather than as sovereigns regulating their citizens *(slide 8)*. Generally applicable laws that regulate state conduct along with private conduct avoid the anti-commandeering problem *(slide 8)*.

**The Key Distinction.** Regulating states as market participants is constitutional; commanding states to regulate their citizens is not *(slide 8)*. But is this distinction coherent? States don't choose to enter the DMV market the way private actors choose to enter markets *(slide 8)*. The regulator/proprietor distinction from sovereign immunity law is imported into anti-commandeering analysis *(slide 8)*. The lecture script presses a deeper version of this critique: if state employment in Garcia is also market participation, then Reno's logic, taken seriously, would swallow much of what Garcia purported to protect through political process *(pp. 6-7)*.

**The Unanswered Question.** Where does Reno's exception end? Can Congress regulate any state activity it labels proprietary? The lecture identifies this as one of the doctrine's unresolved boundary problems *(slide 14)*.

### Murphy v NCAA (2018)

**The Holding (Alito, J., 7-2).** The Professional and Amateur Sports Protection Act (PASPA) prohibited states from authorizing sports gambling, and this violates anti-commandeering *(slide 9)*. Congress cannot command states to act AND cannot prohibit states from acting; both are "direct orders to state legislatures" *(slide 9)*. The distinction between compelling and prohibiting is an empty one *(slide 9)*. As Alito put it, it is as if federal officers were installed in state legislative chambers and armed with the authority to stop legislators from voting on offending proposals *(slide 9)*.

**The Extension.** New York + Printz said Congress can't command affirmative state legislative action; Murphy adds that Congress also can't prohibit state legislative action *(slide 9)*. The anti-commandeering principle runs in both directions.

**The Preemption Paradox.** This creates a striking formal result *(slide 11)*:
- Option A: Congress directly bans sports gambling nationwide by making it a federal crime. VALID. Preemption is fine.
- Option B: Congress prohibits states from authorizing sports gambling (PASPA). INVALID. Commandeering.
- Option C: Congress regulates how states handle DMV data (DPPA). VALID. General applicability.

The paradox: Congress can achieve the same practical result (no sports gambling) through Option A but is forbidden from the lighter touch of Option B -- the Constitution requires the heavier hand *(slide 11)*. The deck's slide 12 sharpens this by attacking the accountability rationale on its own terms: PASPA was clearly federal law and voters knew exactly whom to blame -- so the doctrine's main justification (preserving political accountability) does not even hold on the PASPA facts *(slide 12)*.
<!-- 2026-05-26: removed sweeping "hard to defend on any pragmatic theory of federalism" gloss (not in deck or script); replaced with the anchored Slide 12 accountability-rationale critique, which is the specific critique the deck actually makes. -->


**The Accountability Rationale Revisited.** The theory: if Congress commandeers states to implement unpopular policies, voters blame state officials rather than federal legislators, so anti-commandeering ensures voters know whom to hold responsible *(slide 12)*. The critique as applied to PASPA: voters knew perfectly well that PASPA was federal law, and the real effect of Murphy was to enable states to create gambling havens, not to improve political accountability *(slide 12)*.

### The NFIB Spending Power Connection

The lecture briefly notes NFIB v. Sebelius's Medicaid holding as a spending-power analog to anti-commandeering: Congress cannot use coercive conditions to achieve what it cannot command directly *(slide 14)*. This is not formally an anti-commandeering case (conditions are voluntary in form), but the structural concern is similar: federal leverage over state policymaking that effectively removes the state's autonomous choice.

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

**The Garcia-New York Contradiction.** The lecture asks students to reconcile Garcia with New York, Printz, and Murphy *(slide 10)*. The best-available synthesis: Garcia holds that political process is the primary protection for state sovereignty in the context of generally applicable federal regulation (FLSA applied to state employers). The anti-commandeering cases carve out a specific structural limit: Congress cannot use states as instrumentalities of federal governance. But this synthesis requires a line between general regulation and commandeering that is not crisp -- the lecture leaves this as a genuine doctrinal gap *(slide 14)*.

**The Reno Boundary Problem.** If Congress can regulate state DMV databases as market participants, what limits the exception? *(slide 14)* Can Congress pass laws regulating state prisons as market participants in the incarceration business? State universities as market participants in higher education? The lecture asks students to identify what makes the DMV genuinely analogous to a private database operator.

**The Murphy Paradox and Federalism Theory.** The preemption paradox: if the goal of anti-commandeering is to preserve state autonomous policymaking, Murphy's result seems wrong -- PASPA's Option B left states free to prohibit gambling themselves, and Option A (making gambling a federal crime) is more intrusive of state governance than PASPA was *(slide 11)*. Can any principled theory of federalism justify a rule that requires the more nationally intrusive approach?

## Key Takeaways

- Three incompatible models of the Tenth Amendment operate simultaneously: truism (Garcia/Darby -- the Amendment adds no substantive limit), substantive limit (New York/Printz/Murphy -- judicially-enforced anti-commandeering), and hybrid (the current confused state). The Court has never reconciled them *(slide 13)*.
- Garcia's process-federalism theory is never explicitly overruled but is functionally contradicted by three landmark cases. This is a live doctrinal incoherence *(slide 10)*.
- Anti-commandeering runs in both directions: Congress cannot command state legislative action (New York) OR prohibit it (Murphy). Congress cannot commandeer state executives (Printz). But Congress can commandeer state courts (Testa) *(slides 5, 6, 9, 3)*.
- The Testa Problem (judges but not sheriffs) remains the doctrine's most visible structural anomaly *(slides 7, 14)*.
- The preemption paradox -- Congress must impose a heavier federal hand via direct prohibition rather than the lighter touch of prohibiting state authorization -- is evidence that anti-commandeering doctrine's formal logic outstrips its functional justifications *(slide 11)*.
- The spending-power analog (NFIB's Medicaid coercion holding) suggests that the anti-commandeering principle may extend beyond formal commandeering to conditions that are coercive in substance *(slide 14)*.
