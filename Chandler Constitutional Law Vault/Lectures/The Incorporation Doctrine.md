## Lecture Outline

The lecture moves chronologically from the antebellum baseline through the present doctrinal tentacles *(slide 2)*, pausing at each inflection point to ask what the Court did, what it should have done, and what the choice costs.

The antebellum baseline comes from Barron v Baltimore in 1833, where Chief Justice Marshall held that the Bill of Rights applies only to the federal government, not the states *(slide 3)*. The Anti-Federalists who demanded the first ten amendments feared centralized power, so applying them against the states would have inverted their purpose *(slide 3)*. Under Barron a state could violate what would have been a federal constitutional right with no recourse under the first ten amendments *(slide 3)*. The Civil War changed the calculus *(slide 3)*. The Fourteenth Amendment in 1868 contained the Due Process Clause, the Equal Protection Clause, and the Privileges or Immunities Clause, which prohibited states from making or enforcing any law that would abridge the privileges or immunities of citizens of the United States *(slide 3)*. Representative John Bingham, the Amendment's principal drafter, intended this language to apply the Bill of Rights against the states *(slide 3)*.

The Slaughter-House Disaster in 1873 is the lecture's pivot *(slide 4)*. The case had nothing to do with the Bill of Rights -- it involved a Louisiana slaughterhouse monopoly -- but Justice Miller's 5-4 majority drew a fatal distinction between the privileges of state citizenship and those of national citizenship and held that the Fourteenth Amendment protected only the latter *(slide 4)*. The national privileges the majority identified were a near-trivial list: access to seaports, the right to travel to Washington, D.C., protection on the high seas -- rights that mostly existed before the Amendment *(slide 4)*. Miller feared that a broad reading would make the Court "a perpetual censor upon all legislation of the States" *(slide 4)*. Justice Field's dissent warned that the majority had rendered the Clause "a vain and idle enactment, which accomplished nothing" *(slide 4)*. The most natural textual path for applying the Bill of Rights against the states had been gutted *(slide 4)*.

Cruikshank in 1876 slammed the door *(slide 5)*. Arising from the Colfax Massacre, in which armed whites murdered approximately 150 Black citizens in Louisiana, Cruikshank held that the First and Second Amendments operate "as a restriction on the powers of Congress, and not as limitations on the powers of the States" *(slide 5)*. After Slaughterhouse and Cruikshank, the Privileges or Immunities Clause was a dead letter, the Bill of Rights expressly did not bind the states, and any path toward incorporation appeared foreclosed *(slide 5)*.

The back door opens with Chicago Burlington and Quincy Railroad v Chicago in 1897, where the Court held that states cannot take private property without just compensation -- but did not call this incorporation; Harlan characterized just compensation as inherent in due process itself, a subtle reframing achieving a functionally identical result *(slide 6)*. The real breakthrough came in Gitlow v New York in 1925, where the Court casually assumed that freedom of speech and of the press are among the fundamental personal rights and "liberties" protected by the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment *(slide 6)*. The assumption was made almost as an aside, with no discussion of Slaughterhouse or Cruikshank *(slide 6)*. The key move was shifting the vehicle from Privileges or Immunities to Due Process, which let the Court sidestep Slaughterhouse without overruling it *(slide 6)*.

Palko v Connecticut in 1937 provided the theoretical scaffolding *(slide 7)*. Justice Cardozo wrote that the Due Process Clause "may absorb specific privileges or immunities to the extent that neither liberty nor justice would exist without them" *(slide 7)*. The test asked whether a right is "implicit in the concept of ordered liberty" *(slide 7)*. Speech, press, and religion were incorporated under this framework; double jeopardy, at the time, was not *(slide 7)*. The framework was elegant but subjective, and that subjectivity was also its utility -- it let the Court incorporate rights one at a time, at its own pace, without a confrontation with Slaughterhouse *(slide 7)*.

Adamson v California in 1947 staged the great debate *(slide 8)*. Justice Black argued for total incorporation, conducting extensive historical analysis of the framers' intent and urging the Court to overturn Barron directly *(slide 8)*. He criticized selective incorporation as giving the Court "boundless power under 'natural law' periodically to expand and contract constitutional standards" *(slide 8)*. Justice Frankfurter defended selective incorporation, insisting that the overlap between incorporated rights and the Bill of Rights was largely coincidental and that the Bill of Rights provided "merely specific methods … for securing basic rights that are inherent in man's nature" *(slide 8)*.

The Warren Court revolution refined the test in Duncan v Louisiana in 1968, asking whether a right is "fundamental to the American scheme of justice" *(slide 9)*. The shift from abstract philosophy to American legal traditions favored incorporation *(slide 9)*. A key principle emerged: once incorporated, rights apply with full federal strength, with no watered-down state versions *(slide 9)*. The deck catalogs the Warren Court's incorporation sweep -- Mapp (1961), Gideon (1963), Malloy (1964), Duncan (1968) *(slide 9)*.

The doctrinal magic, the deck emphasizes, works through four moves *(slide 10)*: clause shifting from Privileges or Immunities to Due Process, fundamental-rights framing that treats protections as inherent in due process rather than as direct Bill of Rights application, selective application one right at a time, and the silent treatment under which most incorporation decisions never mention Slaughterhouse or Cruikshank.

Two tentacles extend from the Due Process choice *(slide 12, 13)*. The first is substantive due process: using the Due Process Clause for incorporation creates a deep linkage to the idea that due process protects not merely procedural fairness but substantive rights so fundamental that no process can justify their deprivation *(slide 12)*. The same theoretical move powers both incorporation and unenumerated rights -- Lochner, Griswold, and Obergefell *(slide 12)*. If substantive due process is illegitimate, as originalist critics argue, then so, in theory, is the entire incorporation edifice *(slide 12)*. Justice Thomas's McDonald concurrence makes exactly this point: "I cannot accept a theory of constitutional interpretation that rests on such tenuous footing" *(slide 12)*. The second tentacle is a possible revival of Privileges or Immunities -- the Clause speaks directly to "privileges" and "immunities" of citizens, language that maps naturally onto the Bill of Rights, while the Due Process Clause speaks to "process," and using it to protect substance requires the conceptual leap of substantive due process *(slide 13)*. Thomas's McDonald concurrence offered an extensive originalist reappraisal, and in Timbs v Indiana (2019) both Thomas and Gorsuch urged reconsideration *(slide 13)*. But what the Clause would protect beyond the Bill of Rights is the uncertainty that may explain judicial hesitation *(slide 13)*.

McDonald v City of Chicago in 2010 is the lecture's modern centerpiece *(slide 14)*. After Heller (2008) recognized an individual Second Amendment right against the federal government, Otis McDonald challenged Chicago's handgun ban *(slide 14)*. Alito's plurality applied the standard selective incorporation framework, finding the right to bear arms "fundamental to our scheme of ordered liberty" and "deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition," and incorporated it through Due Process *(slide 14)*. Thomas's concurrence agreed with the result but rejected the Due Process methodology entirely, arguing for incorporation through Privileges or Immunities and directly criticizing Slaughterhouse *(slide 14)*. Thomas essentially acknowledged what the Court had avoided for over a century: that incorporation was a dramatic reversal achieved through doctrinal sleight of hand *(slide 14)*.

The lecture closes by flagging open questions *(slide 15)*: Will the Court overrule Slaughterhouse? What about unincorporated provisions such as grand jury indictment, civil jury trial, and excessive bail? What happens to substantive due process if incorporation moves to Privileges or Immunities? And after incorporation, how do substantive content tests (Bruen's history-and-tradition test, Kennedy v Bremerton) continue to evolve? The conclusion: incorporation through Due Process is, at bottom, a way of chipping away at Slaughterhouse without formally overruling it *(slide 16)*.

## Second Amendment Doctrinal Arc (Heller → McDonald → Bruen → Rahimi)

The four modern Second Amendment cases in the vault trace a complete doctrinal arc. Each case answers a question the preceding case either left open or exposed. Distilled from the four case briefs, without new research:

| Case | Scope question resolved | Doctrinal move | Methodology | Vote | Standard-of-review posture |
|------|-------------------------|----------------|-------------|------|----------------------------|
| [[Cases/District of Columbia v Heller (2008)\|Heller (2008)]] | Does the Second Amendment protect an individual right unconnected to militia service? (Federal context.) | Recognizes individual right to possess and carry weapons for self-defense, centered on the home. Lists "presumptively lawful" regulations without theorizing them. | Originalist text-and-history parsing of prefatory and operative clauses. | 5-4 (Scalia) | Rejects rational basis and Breyer's interest-balancing; declines to specify strict or intermediate scrutiny. |
| [[Cases/McDonald v City of Chicago (2010)\|McDonald (2010)]] | Does Heller's right bind the States? | Incorporates the Heller right against the States. Leaves the right as defined by Heller; adds no new scope. | Plurality: selective incorporation through Due Process (ordered liberty + deeply rooted). Thomas concurrence: Privileges or Immunities revival; would overrule Slaughter-House and Cruikshank. | 5-4 judgment; 4-Justice plurality on Due Process. Thomas is the fifth vote on P-or-I. | Does not specify a standard of review; carries over Heller's unresolved standard question. |
| [[Cases/New York State Rifle and Pistol Association v Bruen (2022)\|Bruen (2022)]] | Does the right extend outside the home? What is the standard of review? | Extends right to public carry for self-defense. Replaces the two-step framework with text-history-and-tradition. Strikes down may-issue (proper-cause) licensing; preserves shall-issue. | Step 1: challenger shows plain text covers conduct. Step 2: government must produce a relevantly similar historical analogue (the "how" and "why" metrics). | 6-3 (Thomas) | Rejects means-end scrutiny entirely ("one step too many"). No strict or intermediate scrutiny in 2A cases. |
| [[Cases/United States v Rahimi (2024)\|Rahimi (2024)]] | Can Bruen's framework accommodate targeted modern regulation, or does it require a founding-era twin? | Upholds § 922(g)(8) temporary disarmament of persons subject to qualifying domestic-violence protective orders. Clarifies Bruen's "analogue" requirement. | Analogical reasoning against surety laws and going-armed laws. Shared principles, not identical mechanics. Doctrinal stabilizers: temporariness and judicial process. | 8-1 (Roberts); Thomas solo dissent | Maintains Bruen's rejection of means-end scrutiny; Thomas accuses majority of diluting the test into a floor that any modern regulation can clear. |

**What each case unlocks.** Heller opens the individual-right question at the federal level. McDonald closes the incorporation question but not the scope or standard questions. Bruen closes the scope (public carry) and standard (text-history-and-tradition) questions but exposes an administrability problem. Rahimi calibrates the administrability problem by distinguishing analogue from twin, while introducing its own new ambiguity about how loose the analogy may be (which is the point of Thomas's solo dissent).

**Why the arc matters on an exam.** The natural prompt is a modern firearms regulation challenged under the Second Amendment. The answer walks through: (1) the text covers the conduct (Heller on "keep and bear," Bruen on public carry), (2) the right binds the regulator (federal: Heller; state: McDonald), (3) the government must show a relevantly similar historical analogue (Bruen framework), (4) temporariness and judicial process may serve as doctrinal stabilizers (Rahimi), (5) the level-of-generality question is the contested pressure point (Bruen dissent, Rahimi dissent).

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

The lecture presses three analytical pressure points. First, if the Court were to overrule Slaughterhouse today and move incorporation to Privileges or Immunities, what would change substantively versus doctrinally? *(slide 15)* The honest answer may be that most incorporated rights stay incorporated but the theoretical foundation becomes more textually defensible, with the cost of potentially protecting unenumerated rights the Court has not yet identified *(slide 15)*. Second, if substantive due process falls, does incorporation fall with it? *(slide 15)* This page treats the linkage as a load-bearing wall of modern doctrine -- editorial sharpening of the deck's softer "deepest tensions" language *(slide 12)*. Third, what about the still-unincorporated provisions -- grand jury indictment under the Fifth Amendment, civil jury trial under the Seventh, and excessive bail under the Eighth? *(slides 11, 15)* Would the modern "fundamental to the American scheme of justice" test *(slide 9)* yield different results today than when Palko and Hurtado were decided? The deck treats these as genuinely open *(slide 15)*.

## Key Takeaways

- Incorporation is achieved through the Due Process Clause because the Privileges or Immunities Clause was rendered a dead letter by Slaughter-House in 1873, not because Due Process is the textually natural home for Bill of Rights protections against the States.
- The Palko test (implicit in the concept of ordered liberty) and the Duncan refinement (fundamental to the American scheme of justice) both perform the work of selective incorporation, one right at a time.
- Once incorporated, a right applies against the States with full federal strength; there are no watered-down state versions.
- McDonald is the doctrinally revealing modern case because Thomas's concurrence openly names what the Court has spent over a century avoiding: that incorporation is a dramatic reversal achieved through clause-shifting, fundamental-rights framing, selective application, and silent treatment of Slaughter-House.
- The Due Process route ties incorporation to substantive due process. If one falls, the other becomes harder to defend. This is a load-bearing tension in modern constitutional law and an exam-likely synthesis point.
- A Privileges or Immunities revival has at least two votes (Thomas, Gorsuch) and intellectual sympathy from others, but the uncertainty about what the Clause would protect beyond the Bill of Rights explains judicial hesitation.
