The deck is built as a five-section walk through what Marshall created beyond judicial review, opening with the claim that Marbury is "perhaps the most generative single opinion in American constitutional law" *(p. 2)*. The political-question track traces Marshall's distinction between "political powers" and "rights of individuals," questions "in their nature political" that "cannot be made in court," and the executive's discretionary acts in foreign affairs and military decisions remaining unreviewable, before moving to the modern Baker v. ## Lecture Outline

### I. Marbury's Ministerial/Discretionary Distinction

*Marbury* distinguished between two categories of executive acts *(p. 4)*:
- **Ministerial acts**: specific duties imposed by law that leave no room for executive discretion; courts can provide remedies when there is a "specific duty assigned by law" *(p. 4)*. (Example: delivering Marbury's commission once duly signed and sealed.)
- **Discretionary acts**: exercises of executive judgment or political discretion entrusted to the executive; for these the executive is "accountable only to his country in his political character" *(p. 4)*.

Marshall announced this distinction to explain why courts could in principle compel delivery of a commission (ministerial) while leaving foreign policy and removal decisions (discretionary) to the executive. The distinction has since structured every analysis of executive duty: *Kendall v Stokes* (compelling Postmaster); *Train v City of New York* (impoundment); and the entire Bivens line all turn on whether the duty is ministerial or discretionary.

### II. The Political Question Doctrine

*Marbury* noted that some questions are "in their nature political" and cannot be made in court, with the executive's discretionary acts in foreign affairs and military decisions remaining unreviewable *(p. 3)*. *Baker v Carr* (1962) gave this idea a comprehensive test, with the deck flagging two of the six factors specifically *(p. 3)*:

1. A textually demonstrable constitutional commitment of the issue to a coordinate political department; *(p. 3)*
2. A lack of judicially discoverable and manageable standards for resolving the issue; *(p. 3)*
3. The impossibility of deciding without an initial policy determination of a kind clearly for nonjudicial discretion;
4. The impossibility of independent resolution without expressing a lack of the respect due coordinate branches;
5. An unusual need for unquestioning adherence to a political decision already made;
6. The potentiality of embarrassment from multifarious pronouncements by various departments on one question.

The deck flags partisan gerrymandering (Rucho 2019), foreign affairs, and impeachment as modern applications *(p. 3)*. *Zivotofsky v Clinton* (2012) -- not on the slides but standard refinement -- holds the passport-birthplace dispute justiciable as a question of statutory interpretation rather than one committed to executive discretion, so the political question doctrine does not apply as a blanket bar to foreign affairs.

### III. Presidential Immunity and Constitutional Remedies

**Nixon v Fitzgerald (1982):** Absolute presidential immunity from civil damages liability for official acts *(p. 4)*. The unique constitutional position of the President -- the only officer subject to impeachment, the only officer directly accountable to the entire nation -- justifies absolute (not merely qualified) immunity for acts within the outer perimeter of official duties. This immunity is not a gift but a structural necessity: if Presidents faced personal liability for every official decision, the chilling effect on the exercise of constitutional discretion would be severe.

**Trump v United States (2024):** Criminal immunity for official acts, citing Marbury's framework *(p. 4)*.

**Qualified immunity:** Officials performing discretionary functions get qualified immunity, a doctrinal cousin of the ministerial/discretionary distinction *(p. 4)*.

**Bivens v Six Unknown Named Agents (1971):** The deck quotes Marshall's "government of laws, and not of men" principle in full and identifies Bivens as the implied-rights-of-action progeny -- federal officers who violate the Fourth Amendment may be sued directly under the Constitution for damages, even without an authorizing statute *(p. 5)*.

**The Bivens trajectory:** The modern Court "frequently recognizes rights without remedies," and the deck names *Egbert v Boule* (2022) as the contemporary lockdown -- Bivens is rarely extended to new contexts *(p. 5)*. The doctrinal arc this page traces (Chappell v Wallace 1983, Bush v Lucas 1983, Schweiker v Chilicky 1988, Ziglar v Abbasi 2017) is editor-supplied detail; the deck names only Bivens and Egbert.

### IV. Separation of Powers as Enforceable Law

*Marbury*'s deepest legacy is that structural constitutional provisions create enforceable legal boundaries, not merely political arrangements among branches, and that every word must have operative effect *(p. 6)*. The deck names INS v Chadha (1983) invalidating the legislative veto, Clinton v City of New York (1998) striking down the line-item veto, and Seila Law v CFPB (2020) invalidating removal restrictions as modern applications, with the deck's claim that all rest on Marshall's insistence that structure creates judicially enforceable boundaries *(p. 6)*.

The deck adds a constitutional-supremacy track: the Constitution is "superior paramount law, unchangeable by ordinary means," hierarchically above statutory law, binding all government actors *(p. 7)*. The interpretive-methodology track gives three Marshall-derived rules: every word and clause must have operative effect; provisions must be read together in harmonious interpretation; the methodology is invoked by both originalists and living constitutionalists *(p. 7)*.

## Key Takeaways

- *Marbury*'s legacy extends far beyond judicial review: it gave us the ministerial/discretionary distinction, the germ of the political question doctrine, a framework for constitutional remedies, and the principle that separation of powers is judicially enforceable law.
- Ministerial/discretionary: courts can compel ministerial acts (no discretion left); courts stay out of discretionary executive judgments.
- Political question doctrine (*Baker v Carr*): six factors; not a blanket "political = non-justiciable" rule -- it requires specific analysis.
- Presidential immunity (*Nixon v Fitzgerald*): absolute for official acts; structural necessity, not personal privilege. *Trump v United States* (2024) extended and refined this.
- *Bivens*: created in 1971; now functionally limited to its original context. Courts are extremely reluctant to extend Bivens to new factual patterns.
- Separation of powers: judicially cognizable and enforceable against all three branches.
