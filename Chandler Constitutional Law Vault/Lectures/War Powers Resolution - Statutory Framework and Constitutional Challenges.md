## Lecture Outline

### Purpose and Policy: Section 2 (50 U.S.C. § 1541)

The WPR's stated purpose is "to fulfill the intent of the framers of the Constitution... [and] insure collective judgment" between Congress and the President in decisions to deploy military force (p. 2). The founders assigned the power to declare war to Congress (Art. I, § 8, cl. 11) while making the President Commander-in-Chief (Art. II, § 2). The WPR attempts to restore Congress's constitutional role after decades of presidential military action without formal declarations of war (Korea, Vietnam).

**Legal bases for military deployment under Section 2:** The WPR identifies three lawful triggers: (1) a formal declaration of war; (2) statutory authorization, such as an Authorization for the Use of Military Force (AUMF); and (3) a national emergency, such as an attack on the United States (p. 2).

### Presidential Consultation: Section 3 (50 U.S.C. § 1542)

Section 3 requires the President, "in every possible instance," to consult with Congress before introducing armed forces into hostilities or imminent hostilities (p. 3). This consultation requirement applies before any deployment, not merely after. However, the phrase "in every possible instance" has been read by presidents to permit deployment without consultation in fast-moving operational contexts. More importantly, Section 3 carries **no judicial enforcement mechanism**: courts have uniformly dismissed WPR consultation claims as political questions (see Dellums v. Bush, 752 F. Supp. 1141 (D.D.C. 1990)) (p. 3).

### Reporting Requirements: Section 4 (50 U.S.C. § 1543)

Section 4 requires the President to submit a written report to Congress within 48 hours whenever armed forces are introduced into hostilities or imminent hostilities, into a foreign country equipped for combat, or in a substantial enlargement of forces abroad (p. 4). The critical consequence: a Section 4(a)(1) report (hostilities) triggers the 60-day clock under Section 5(b) (p. 4). Presidents have consistently submitted reports "consistent with" the WPR rather than explicitly under Section 4(a)(1), thereby avoiding the triggering of the 60-day clock -- a practice Congress has contested but courts have declined to adjudicate.

### The 60-Day Clock: Section 5(b) (50 U.S.C. § 1544(b))

Section 5(b) provides the WPR's core enforcement mechanism: once the clock is triggered, "within 60 calendar days... the President shall terminate" the use of armed forces (p. 5) unless Congress has (1) declared war, (2) enacted specific statutory authorization, (3) extended the deadline by law, or (4) is physically unable to meet because of an armed attack. The President must also terminate forces earlier if Congress directs by concurrent resolution.

**The Chadha Problem:** The concurrent resolution mechanism (Congress directing termination by concurrent resolution without presenting the resolution to the President for signature or veto) is a classic legislative veto -- the same mechanism the Court held unconstitutional in INS v. Chadha, 462 U.S. 919 (1983) (p. 5). After Chadha, the congressional-concurrent-resolution termination mechanism in Section 5(c) is constitutionally inoperative. Congress must pass a joint resolution (presented to the President) to lawfully direct termination before the 60-day clock expires.

### Constitutional Challenges to the WPR

**Executive Branch position:** Every President from Nixon forward has taken the position that the WPR's restrictions on the Commander-in-Chief power (Art. II, § 2) are unconstitutional (p. 6). The core argument: Article II vests the Commander-in-Chief function in the President, and Congress cannot use ordinary legislation to restrict that function. Presidents comply with WPR reporting obligations as a matter of comity, not legal compulsion.

**Congressional defense:** Congress argues that the Declare War Clause (Art. I, § 8) gives Congress the authority to define the conditions under which the executive may deploy forces (p. 6). The WPR is an exercise of that authority: Congress is not abolishing the Commander-in-Chief function but specifying the lawful triggers for its exercise.

**Judicial avoidance:** Lower courts continue to dismiss WPR enforcement suits, including Smith v. Obama, 2016 WL 680625 (D.D.C. 2016) (p. 6). In Dellums v. Bush (1990), a federal district court dismissed a WPR challenge to preparations for the Gulf War (p. 3). Lower courts have likewise declined to reach the merits of WPR claims in challenges to operations in Libya, Yemen, and Somalia.

**Zivotofsky v. Clinton (2012):** The Supreme Court narrowed the political question doctrine, holding that statutory disputes are justiciable (p. 6). On this page's reading, Zivotofsky signals that courts cannot use the political question doctrine as a blanket shield for all executive-congressional foreign affairs conflicts, though it did not resolve WPR disputes directly.

### The WPR's Practical Ineffectiveness

In practice, the WPR has not successfully constrained presidential military action:

- Presidents have deployed forces in Libya (2011), Syria (2013, 2017), Yemen (ongoing), Somalia, and elsewhere without triggering the 60-day clock by reporting under Section 4(a)(1).
- Congress has passed post-hoc AUMFs (2001, 2002) but has not affirmatively terminated any military action under Section 5(b) after a presidential refusal to comply.
- The 60-day clock has never been definitively adjudicated in court.
- The legislative veto termination mechanism (Section 5(c)) is constitutionally disabled after Chadha.

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

Exam framing: Given a presidential military action and Congress's response, trace the WPR's procedures. Key questions: Did the President report under Section 4(a)(1) (triggering the 60-day clock) or under a less specific provision? If the clock runs, can Congress terminate? After Chadha, only a joint resolution -- presented to the President for signature or subject to veto -- can lawfully direct termination. What happens if the President vetoes the joint resolution and Congress cannot override? The WPR's architecture breaks down at precisely this point.

## Key Takeaways

- The WPR's three-trigger structure (declaration, AUMF, national emergency) reflects the founders' design of collective judgment but lacks effective enforcement.
- Section 3's consultation requirement is unenforceable: courts dismiss WPR suits as political questions.
- The 60-day clock (Section 5(b)) is the core mechanism, but presidents can sidestep it by declining to report under Section 4(a)(1).
- INS v. Chadha disables the concurrent-resolution termination mechanism (Section 5(c)), leaving Congress without a fast, non-veto-able termination tool.
- Every president has denied the WPR's constitutionality; the Court has never ruled on the merits.
- Zivotofsky (2012) signals that at least some WPR disputes might be justiciable, but lower courts continue to dismiss these cases.
