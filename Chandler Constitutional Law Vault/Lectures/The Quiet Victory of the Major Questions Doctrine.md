# The Quiet Victory of the Major Questions Doctrine

<!-- 2026-05-26: convention rollout (slide anchors + Professor Emphasis split) -->

## Professor Emphasis

### From source

The deck is organized as a five-act case study in Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump (2026), the Supreme Court's decision invalidating President Trump's IEEPA tariffs *(slide 2)*. The deck's central thesis is the "quiet victory" -- the MQD has won not through a majority endorsing a clear-statement constitutional rule, but by becoming so embedded in the reasoning of even its opponents that it operates as a background constraint on statutory construction regardless of whether a majority invokes it by name; six Justices reason this way, only three will say so *(slides 24, 40)*. The deck uses a flashback structure -- each act of the Learning Resources analysis is interrupted by flashbacks to foundational cases: McCulloch for the taxing power *(slide 8)*, Gibbons for tariffs as taxes *(slide 9)*, West Virginia for MQD's origin *(slide 14)*, Hampton for the intelligible principle test *(slide 26)*, Schechter for nondelegation's high-water mark *(slide 27)*, Youngstown and Dames & Moore for the foreign affairs framework *(slides 34, 36)*. The deck stresses three incompatible MQD theories that converge in easy cases and diverge in hard ones: Gorsuch's clear-statement rule, Barrett's textualism-in-context, and Kagan's "straight-up statutory construction" *(slide 18)*. The deck calls out Gorsuch's catalog of Kagan's prior dissents to argue she is applying the doctrine without saying so *(slide 21)*. The deck also presses the ideological scramble: every prior MQD application was a conservative majority against a Democratic administration; Learning Resources scrambled that narrative *(slides 15, 23)*. MQD is framed as "Nondelegation 2.0" -- it fills the institutional gap formal nondelegation cannot *(slide 29)*. The quiet narrowing of Curtiss-Wright, Dames & Moore, and Youngstown is accomplished within the textual holding all six Justices joined *(slide 38)*.

### Editor's framing

This page treats the lecture as fundamentally about how precedent is migrated and recharacterized, not just what the rules are -- a teaching frame the deck supports through its repeated "Teaching Point" callouts on slides 8 and 36 but does not state as a unifying theme. The packaging of MQD's three theories as having "the label debate matter most in the cases that reach the Supreme Court" sharpens the deck's "easy cases / hard cases" formulation *(slide 22)*. The "Quiet Victory" framing as a thesis about how interpretive norms prevail through opponents' reasoning is the deck's own self-described central thesis *(slide 24)*.

## Lecture Outline

**ACT I: The Crisis — IEEPA and the IEEPA Tariffs**
Early 2025: President Trump declared national emergencies over drug trafficking and trade deficits and invoked IEEPA to impose 25% tariffs on Canada and Mexico, and 10% on China -- later raised to 145% on China *(slide 4)*. By December 2025, $129 billion had been collected from 301,000+ importers across 34 million customs entries *(slide 4)*. The Supreme Court granted certiorari, consolidated cases, and in 2026 affirmed invalidation 6-3 *(slide 4)*.

The statutory question: the entire government case rested on two words separated by sixteen others -- "regulate . . . importation" in 50 U.S.C. § 1702(a)(1)(B) *(slide 5)*. Does the power to "regulate . . . importation" include the power to impose tariffs? *(slide 5)*

Before that question can be answered, a premise must be established: tariffs are not ordinary regulations -- they are exercises of the taxing power *(slide 6)*. The distinction between what tariffs do (regulate commerce) and what the power to impose them is (a branch of the taxing power) is the hinge on which the entire case turns *(slide 6)*.

**ACT II: The Textual Holding — "Regulate" Does Not Include the Power to Tax**
The majority used two 19th-century Marshall Court cases as flashbacks:

*McCulloch v. Maryland* (1819) -- the power to tax is the power to destroy *(slide 8)*. Roberts repurposed Marshall's characterization: if the taxing power is this potent, Congress would not delegate it through ambiguous language; a federalism holding becomes a separation-of-powers tool *(slide 8)*.

*Gibbons v. Ogden* (1824) -- Marshall had distinguished the Commerce Clause from the taxing power, noting tariffs were "very clearly a branch of the taxing power" despite their regulatory effect on commerce *(slide 9)*. The majority treats this as a constitutional fact: tariffs are taxes; taxes belong to Congress; Congress doesn't delegate its most important powers vaguely; "regulate" cannot mean "tax" *(slide 9)*.

The chain *(slide 9)*: Tariffs are taxes (Gibbons, 1824) → The taxing power is uniquely Congress's (McCulloch, 1819; Nicol v. Ames, 1899) → Congress doesn't delegate its most important powers through vague language (West Virginia, 2022) → "Regulate" cannot mean "tax."

The majority reinforced this with five textual arguments *(slide 10)*: (1) ordinary meaning of "regulate" is to fix, establish, or control -- not to tax; (2) when Congress delegates tariff authority elsewhere, it uses "duty" and imposes caps, durational limits, and procedural prerequisites -- IEEPA has none; (3) constitutional avoidance -- if "regulate importation" includes taxing, "regulate exportation" would too, but Article I, § 9 forbids export taxes; (4) noscitur a sociis -- nine verbs × eleven objects = 99 possible actions, none involving raising revenue; (5) Algonquin distinguished -- Section 232 said "duties" and gave sweeping discretion, while IEEPA says neither.

All six majority Justices joined this textual analysis, including Kagan, Sotomayor, and Jackson *(slide 10)*. The textual holding is 6-0: "Regulate" does not include the power to tax *(slide 11)*. The case could have ended here, and three Justices thought it should *(slide 11)*.

**ACT III: The Major Questions Doctrine — A Plurality That Casts a Long Shadow**
Roberts (joined by Gorsuch and Barrett) went further: when the executive claims authority of vast economic or political significance, courts require clear congressional authorization *(slide 13)*. The diagnostic criteria: vast economic or political significance, no historical precedent, ambiguous or "backwater" statutory basis, transformative policy implications *(slide 13)*.

*Flashback to West Virginia v. EPA* (2022): The EPA claimed authority under a seldom-used Clean Air Act provision to restructure the nation's energy grid; the Court held 6-3 that claims of this economic and political significance require clear congressional authorization, not ambiguous language *(slide 14)*.

*Flashback to Biden v. Nebraska* (2023): "Waive or modify" in the HEROES Act did not clearly authorize $430 billion in student loan cancellation; MQD extended to executive spending *(slide 15)*. The pattern before Learning Resources: every prior MQD application had been a 6-3 conservative majority against a Democratic administration *(slide 15)*.

The plurality's MQD analysis treats Learning Resources as the first time MQD applied to delegation of Congress's own enumerated fiscal powers, with staggering economic stakes (trillions in revenue, $15 trillion in trade agreements), and no historical precedent in IEEPA's half-century *(slide 16)*. The cross-party application -- the three consistent MQD supporters (Roberts, Gorsuch, Barrett) applying it against a Republican president while Kavanaugh, Thomas, and Alito dissented -- may enhance the doctrine's perceived legitimacy *(slide 23)*.

The plurality rejected two exceptions *(slide 17)*: (1) the emergency exception -- the Government argued IEEPA's whole point is discretion for unforeseen emergencies; the plurality responded that emergency powers tend to kindle emergencies and that dozens of IEEPA emergencies remain ongoing, including the first, declared over four decades ago. (2) The foreign-affairs exception -- because the tariff power belongs to Congress alone during peacetime (as the Government itself conceded), foreign affairs considerations do not make delegation through vague language more likely.

**The Three Theories of MQD** *(slide 18)*:
- *Gorsuch*: a freestanding clear-statement rule grounded in common-law agency and charter interpretation -- an external constraint on statutory meaning, the strongest version *(slides 18, 19)*. The deck critiques this as grounding a modern doctrine in 17th-century English corporate law *(slide 19)*.
- *Barrett*: textualism in context -- reading statutes in their full constitutional context. Gorsuch's version is "a judicial flex" in tension with textualism *(slides 18, 20)*.
- *Kagan*: "straight-up statutory construction" -- MQD is unnecessary surplusage; ordinary interpretation produces the same result *(slide 18)*. The deck's "convergence problem": Gorsuch catalogs Kagan's prior dissents, where she interpreted "safe and healthful working conditions," "regulations to prevent spread of disease," and "waive or modify" broadly enough to authorize vaccine mandates, eviction moratoriums, and loan cancellation -- but here treats "regulate . . . importation" narrowly *(slide 21)*. Kagan is applying the MQD; she just won't say so *(slide 21)*.

The label matters in hard cases *(slide 22)*. The approaches converge in easy cases and diverge in hard ones, and the hard cases are the ones that reach the Supreme Court *(slide 22)*. Two hypos illustrate: a semiconductor embargo under IEEPA's "prohibit . . . importation" (Kagan: text authorizes; MQD-as-clear-statement: extraordinary economic significance might require more) and a Section 301 tariff at IEEPA scale (Kagan: if statutory prerequisites met, authorized; MQD: unprecedented scope might trigger heightened scrutiny) *(slide 22)*.

**The Quiet Victory** *(slides 24, 40)*: MQD has won not through a majority endorsing a clear-statement rule, but by becoming so embedded in the reasoning of even its opponents that it operates as a background constraint regardless of whether a majority will invoke it by name -- six Justices reason this way; only three will say so.

**ACT IV: The Nondelegation Fracture — Thomas vs. Gorsuch**
*Flashback to Hampton* (1928): The intelligible principle test -- Congress may delegate legislative authority if it provides an "intelligible principle" guiding the executive's exercise of that authority *(slide 26)*. The test has governed delegation challenges since 1928; the Court has not struck down a statute on nondelegation grounds since 1935 *(slide 26)*. Note the irony: Hampton was itself a tariff case *(slide 26)*.

*Flashback to Schechter Poultry* (1935): The NIRA delegation -- presidential power to approve "codes of fair competition" for entire industries with virtually no constraints -- struck down unanimously *(slide 27)*. 91 years without a single statute struck down on nondelegation grounds since *(slide 27)*.

*Flashback to Gundy* (2019): SORNA delegated to the Attorney General authority to decide whether sex-offender registration applied to pre-Act offenders *(slide 28)*. A prior Supreme Court interpretation of SORNA was used to supply the intelligible principle the statute itself lacked -- if a judicial interpretation can serve as the intelligible principle, the test is essentially unfalsifiable *(slide 28)*. Gorsuch's dissent (joined by Roberts and Thomas) proposed replacing the test entirely, creating anticipation of a five-Justice nondelegation revival *(slide 28)*.

In Learning Resources, the anticipated coalition did not materialize *(slide 31)*. Thomas and Gorsuch disagreed fundamentally about nondelegation's scope:
- *Thomas*: Only "core legislative power" -- rules about life, liberty, or property -- is nondelegable; other Article I powers (war, tariffs, borrowing, commerce, coinage) are former Crown prerogatives that Congress may freely delegate *(slide 30)*. The deck frames this as authorizing Congress to create "an elected monarch for the purposes of war, commerce, and finance," constrained only when individual rights are directly at stake *(slide 30)*.
- *Gorsuch*: Article I, § 1 vests "all legislative Powers" in Congress without distinguishing core from non-core *(slide 31)*. The Founders described powers Thomas would classify as "non-legislative" -- tariff power, the power to raise armies, the power to coin money -- as legislative powers *(slide 31)*. Even the Second Congress rejected on nondelegation grounds a proposal giving the President unfettered power to establish postal routes -- a power Thomas would classify as non-legislative *(slide 31)*.

Thomas attracted no additional votes; neither did Gorsuch's robust nondelegation *(slide 31)*. Kavanaugh -- whose vote was thought to be the fifth -- dissented on textual grounds without reaching nondelegation *(slide 32)*. The intelligible principle test survives not because anyone defends it, but because no coalition can form around an alternative *(slide 32)*.

MQD as "Nondelegation 2.0" *(slide 29)*: Where Nondelegation 1.0 (constitutional invalidation) strikes down the entire statute, was last used in 1935, and cannot attract five votes, MQD operates through statutory interpretation, narrows the claimed authority without invalidating the statute, has been active and growing since 2021, and commands six functional votes in Learning Resources. MQD fills the gap that formal nondelegation cannot *(slide 29)*.

**ACT V: Foreign Affairs and the Youngstown Framework**
*Flashback to Youngstown* (1952): Jackson's three-category framework *(slide 34)*. Both sides in Learning Resources invoked it: Kavanaugh placed the case in Category 1 (President acts with congressional authorization, power at its zenith); the majority placed it in Category 2 or 3 (authorization doesn't exist) *(slide 34)*. The framework presupposes agreement on whether authorization exists -- the very question in dispute *(slide 34)*. Youngstown provides the stage set but not the script: the real work is done by textual analysis and, for three Justices, the MQD *(slide 35)*.

*Flashback to Dames & Moore* (1981): The Court blessed presidential action under IEEPA suspending claims against Iran during the hostage crisis, widely cited for four decades as authority for executive flexibility in foreign affairs emergencies *(slide 36)*. Roberts's recharacterization: the 1981 Court actually held that "the terms of IEEPA do not authorize" the President's action, and upheld the claims suspension only because of independent executive-agreement authority *(slide 36)*. A case that for decades supported broad executive power is transformed into a case about IEEPA's limits depending on which passages you emphasize -- narrowing by recharacterization *(slide 36)*.

*Flashback to Curtiss-Wright* (1936): Justice Sutherland described the President as the "sole organ of the federal government in the field of international relations" and suggested that "strict limitation upon congressional delegations" should not apply to "external affairs" *(slide 37)*. Kavanaugh cited Curtiss-Wright twelve times in dissent *(slide 37)*. The majority did not overrule Curtiss-Wright; Gorsuch read it narrowly, treating the actual holding as a carefully bounded delegation -- prohibiting arms sales to specific countries in a specific conflict after a specific factual finding -- and the sweeping "sole organ" language as nonbinding dicta *(slide 37)*. Curtiss-Wright's utility for future executive power claims is diminished without the doctrinal upheaval of an overruling *(slide 37)*.

Summary of the quiet narrowing *(slide 38)*: Curtiss-Wright (sole organ dicta treated as nonbinding); Dames & Moore (recharacterized as about IEEPA's limits, not its breadth); Youngstown (categories apply but don't decide; textual analysis does the work). All accomplished within the textual holding all six Justices joined -- stronger institutional support than the plurality's constitutional overlay.

**The Paradox** *(slide 39)*: Learning Resources establishes that "regulate" does not include the power to tax (6-0); there is no emergency exception to MQD (plurality, uncontradicted); no foreign affairs exception (plurality, uncontradicted); Curtiss-Wright's broad dicta are dicta; Dames & Moore is about IEEPA's limits; and MQD operates as a background norm even without the label. What it does not establish: the theoretical foundation of MQD (three incompatible theories); the scope or revival of the nondelegation doctrine; a replacement for the intelligible principle test; the metes and bounds of IEEPA beyond tariffs; whether MQD applies to future IEEPA actions that aren't taxes; whether Barrett's or Gorsuch's theory will prevail.

## Cases Discussed

### [[Cases/Learning Resources Inc v Trump (2026)]] (2026)
IEEPA tariffs invalidated. "Regulate . . . importation" does not include the power to impose tariffs—tariffs are taxes, not regulations. Unanimous on this textual ground. MQD applied by Roberts, Gorsuch, and Barrett plurality. Thomas and Gorsuch disagreed on nondelegation. Curtiss-Wright and Dames & Moore quietly narrowed.

### [[West Virginia v. EPA]] (2022)
MQD gets a name. EPA claimed authority to restructure the nation's energy grid under a seldom-used Clean Air Act provision. 6-3 conservative majority applied MQD. Established diagnostic criteria: vast economic stakes, no historical precedent, statutory "backwater," transformative implications.

### [[Cases/Biden v Nebraska (2023)]] (2023)
MQD extended to executive spending. "Waive or modify" in the HEROES Act did not authorize $430 billion in student loan cancellation. Part of the pre-Learning Resources pattern of MQD applied against Democratic administrations.

### [[Gundy v. United States]] (2019)
SORNA sex-offender registration delegation upheld using a prior Court interpretation of the statute as the "intelligible principle." Exposed the essentially unfalsifiable nature of the intelligible principle test. Gorsuch's dissent signaled potential five-Justice nondelegation revival that never materialized.

### [[J.W. Hampton Jr. & Co. v. United States]] (1928)
Established the intelligible principle test for evaluating delegations of legislative authority. A tariff statute delegating rate-adjustment authority upheld. The test has governed—but not been applied rigorously—since 1928.

### [[A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States]] (1935)
The last statute struck down on nondelegation grounds. NIRA codes of fair competition delegation with virtually no constraints invalidated. 91 years without a single statute struck down for nondelegation since.

### [[Cases/Youngstown Sheet and Tube Co v Sawyer (1952)|Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer]] (1952)
Jackson's three-category framework for evaluating presidential power claims. Categories apply in Learning Resources but don't decide the outcome—the threshold question (does statutory authorization exist?) must be resolved by textual analysis first.

### [[Dames & Moore v. Regan]] (1981)
Executive suspension of claims against Iran under IEEPA upheld—conventionally cited for presidential flexibility in foreign affairs. Roberts recharacterized it in Learning Resources as holding the action was not authorized by IEEPA text; it was upheld on independent executive-agreement grounds.

### [[United States v. Curtiss-Wright Export Corp.]] (1936)
"Sole organ" doctrine for presidential foreign affairs authority. Sweeping Sutherland dicta about presidential power in external affairs. Learning Resources treats the broad language as nonbinding; the actual holding was a narrowly bounded delegation.

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

**Does the Label Matter?** The deck poses hypotheticals to test whether Kagan's approach and Gorsuch's approach actually diverge *(slide 22)*: (1) President bans all semiconductor imports from China -- "prohibit . . . importation" is expressly in IEEPA, not a tax; Kagan: text authorizes it; MQD-as-clear-statement: extraordinary economic significance might require more than textual fit. (2) President reimposes IEEPA-scale tariffs under Section 301, which does authorize tariffs; Kagan: if statutory prerequisites met, tariffs are authorized; MQD: unprecedented scope might trigger heightened scrutiny even under a tariff-specific statute. The label debate matters most in precisely the cases that reach the Supreme Court *(slide 22)*.

**The Ideological Scramble.** Before Learning Resources, critics called MQD a conservative weapon against progressive regulation *(slide 23)*. Learning Resources scrambled that narrative: the three consistent MQD supporters (Roberts, Gorsuch, Barrett) applied the doctrine against a Republican President while Kavanaugh, Thomas, and Alito dissented *(slide 23)*. Does this cross-party application enhance the doctrine's legitimacy?

**Thomas's "Elected Monarch" Problem.** Thomas would allow Congress to delegate war power, tariff power, borrowing power, and foreign commerce power to the President -- creating, in the deck's framing, "an elected monarch for the purposes of war, commerce, and finance" *(slide 30)*. Is Thomas's distinction between "core legislative power" and former Crown prerogatives doctrinally coherent? Does Article I, § 1's "all legislative Powers" language defeat it? *(slide 31)*

**The Nondelegation Paradox.** The intelligible principle test survives not because anyone defends it, but because no coalition can form around an alternative *(slide 32)*. Is a doctrine that persists through inertia rather than endorsement legitimate?

## Key Takeaways

1. **"Regulate" does not include the power to tax.** This is a 6-0 holding supported by ordinary statutory interpretation tools: ordinary meaning, congressional practice, constitutional avoidance, noscitur a sociis, and case-specific distinctions. It does not depend on MQD or any contested constitutional overlay.

2. **The major questions doctrine has won quietly.** Not through a majority endorsing a clear-statement rule, but by embedding itself in the reasoning of even its opponents. Kagan applies MQD reasoning while refusing the label. Six Justices reason this way; three will say so. This is how interpretive norms typically prevail.

3. **Three incompatible theories of MQD coexist.** Gorsuch (freestanding constitutional clear-statement rule), Barrett (textualism in context), and Kagan (ordinary statutory construction) converge in easy cases and diverge in hard ones. Lower courts need a framework; the hard cases reveal which theory matters.

4. **The anticipated nondelegation revival did not materialize.** Thomas and Gorsuch disagreed fundamentally about the scope of nondelegable legislative power. Kavanaugh declined to reach the question. The intelligible principle test survives through the failure of alternatives, not through affirmative endorsement. MQD fills the institutional gap nondelegation was meant to fill through a more workable mechanism.

5. **Curtiss-Wright and Dames & Moore have been quietly narrowed.** The "sole organ" dicta in Curtiss-Wright are treated as nonbinding; the actual holding was narrow. Dames & Moore is recharacterized as a case about IEEPA's limits, not its breadth. These moves were accomplished within the textual holding all six Justices joined—stronger support than a plurality's constitutional overlay.

6. **Learning Resources settles less than it seems.** The 6-0 textual holding is durable. The plurality's MQD analysis—three incompatible theories, uncontradicted but not endorsed by three Justices—casts a long shadow without fully resolving the doctrine's theoretical foundation. The nondelegation fracture remains unresolved. The "metes and bounds" of IEEPA beyond tariffs are unsettled.
