# The Reconstruction Civil Rights Statutes

## Professor Emphasis

### From source

The deck maps the constitutional architecture of Reconstruction legislation as a six-statute arc from 1865 to 1875 *(slide 2)*. Each statute is presented with an explicit constitutional-tension callout: the 1866 Act was predicated solely on the Thirteenth Amendment, with "constitutionality doubtful" because it defined citizenship and guaranteed broad civil rights far beyond abolition *(slides 2, 3)*. The 1870 Enforcement Act re-enacted the 1866 Act on the Fourteenth Amendment's firmer ground and added aggressive anti-KKK provisions targeting private conduct -- the most aggressive assertion of federal power over private conduct, whose constitutionality was precarious *(slides 4, 5)*. The 1875 Civil Rights Act directly regulated private innkeepers, railroads, and theaters with no "under color of law" requirement *(slide 6)*. The Judiciary Act of 1875, enacted two days after the Civil Rights Act, created general federal question jurisdiction -- the missing piece of the jurisdictional architecture, because the Judiciary Act of 1789 had conspicuously omitted general federal question jurisdiction despite Article III's authorization *(slide 7)*. The deck stages the undoing as a three-step sequence on its final slide: Cruikshank (1876) holds that § 6 of the 1870 Act does not reach private conduct; the Civil Rights Cases (1883) strike down §§ 1-2 of the 1875 Act; and an 81-year silence follows until the 1964 Civil Rights Act -- this time predicated on the Commerce Clause, not the Fourteenth Amendment *(slide 8)*.

### Editor's framing

This page treats each enactment as a response to the doctrinal or political inadequacy of its predecessor -- a thesis the deck's arc supports but does not state in those terms. The framing of the 1866-to-1870 sequence as a "constitutional solution" tracks the deck's "The Problem / The Paradox / The Solution" callout on slide 3. The packaging of the 1964 Civil Rights Act's Commerce Clause grounding as "a deliberate doctrinal detour around the Civil Rights Cases" is editorial sharpening of the deck's neutrally-stated "this time predicated on the Commerce Clause, not the 14th Amendment" *(slide 8)*. The cross-references to Heart of Atlanta, Katzenbach, Jones v. Alfred H. Mayer, and Griffin v. Breckenridge are editorial connections the deck itself does not make.

## Lecture Outline

### I. The Constitutional Arc: 1865–1875

The deck presents the arc as six developments across 1865-1875 *(slide 2)*:

| Year | Development | Constitutional Significance |
|------|-------------|----------------------------|
| 1865 | Thirteenth Amendment | Abolishes slavery; §2 grants Congress enforcement power — but how far does it reach? |
| 1866 | Civil Rights Act | Defines birthright citizenship; guarantees equal civil rights; predicated solely on 13th Amendment; constitutionality doubtful |
| 1868 | Fourteenth Amendment | Ratified; provides the constitutional foundation the 1866 Act lacked: citizenship, equal protection, §5 enforcement |
| 1870 | Enforcement Act | Re-enacts 1866 Act under 14th Amendment; adds voting protections (15th Amendment) and aggressive anti-KKK provisions targeting private conduct |
| 1875 | Civil Rights Act | Extends protection to public accommodations; directly regulates private innkeepers, railroads, theaters; struck down in Civil Rights Cases (1883) |
| 1875 | Judiciary Act | Creates general federal question jurisdiction — ensures federal claims always have a federal forum |

### II. Civil Rights Act of 1866 — 14 Stat. 27
- Predicated on §2 of the Thirteenth Amendment *(slide 3)*
- **§1**: birthright citizenship -- all persons born in the U.S. are citizens; equal rights to contract, sue, hold property, and equal benefit of all laws *(slide 3)*
- **§2**: criminal penalties -- any person acting "under color of" law who deprives inhabitants of these rights: misdemeanor, up to $1,000 fine or 1 year imprisonment *(slide 3)*
- **§9**: military enforcement -- President authorized to employ land, naval, or militia forces *(slide 3)*

#### Constitutional Tension *(slide 3)*
- The Problem: the Thirteenth Amendment abolishes slavery, but § 1 defines citizenship and guarantees broad civil rights far beyond abolition.
- The Paradox: the Thirteenth Amendment reaches private conduct (slaveholding), yet § 2 targets state actors ("under color of law"); Congress's power to regulate state officials arguably required the Fourteenth Amendment.
- The Solution: the Fourteenth Amendment (1868) provided the constitutional foundation, and the 1870 Act re-enacted the 1866 Act on that firmer ground.

### III. Enforcement Act of 1870 — 16 Stat. 140
- Predicated on the Fifteenth Amendment *(slide 4)*

#### Voting Provisions *(slide 4)*
- **§§ 1-3**: equal opportunity to vote without distinction of race; targets state officers who administer voting prerequisites -- state action / firm constitutional ground.
- **§ 4**: criminalizes "any person" who by force, bribery, threats, or intimidation hinders voting -- reaches private conduct, constitutionally uncertain.
- **§ 5**: targets employers and landlords who threaten loss of employment, eviction, or lease non-renewal to suppress votes -- purely private actors, constitutionally vulnerable.

#### Anti-KKK Provisions *(slide 5)*
- **§ 6**: the core anti-KKK provision criminalizing conspiracies and disguised intimidation of citizens to prevent or hinder the free exercise of rights granted or secured by the Constitution or laws of the United States, with felony imprisonment up to 10 years and ineligibility for office *(slide 5)*.
- Constitutional problem: targets private conspiracies -- the most aggressive assertion of federal power over private conduct, with constitutionality later confirmed as precarious in United States v. Cruikshank, 92 U.S. 542 (1876) *(slide 5)*.

#### Re-enactment Provision *(slide 5)*
- **§§ 16-18**: the 1866 Act's equal-rights guarantees re-enacted under the Fourteenth Amendment's § 5 enforcement power, resolving the constitutional doubt that had hung over the Act since passage *(slide 5)*.

### IV. Civil Rights Act of 1875 — 18 Stat. 335
- The Public Accommodations Act *(slide 6)*

#### Core Provisions *(slide 6)*
- Entitlement provision: all persons within the jurisdiction of the United States are entitled to the full and equal enjoyment of the accommodations, advantages, facilities, and privileges of inns, public conveyances on land or water, theaters, and other places of public amusement *(slide 6)*.
- **§§ 1-2**: directly regulated private innkeepers, railroads, theaters; civil liability + criminal penalties; no "under color of law" requirement -- a direct regulation of private conduct *(slide 6)*.
- **§ 3**: exclusive federal court jurisdiction over all violations; part of the piecemeal construction of federal question jurisdiction *(slide 6)*.
- **§ 4**: no racial disqualification from jury service in federal or state courts; survived constitutional challenge in Strauder v. West Virginia (1880) *(slide 6)*.

### V. Judiciary Act of 1875 — 18 Stat. 470 (Enacted March 3, 1875)
- Enacted two days after the Civil Rights Act of 1875; completed the jurisdictional architecture *(slide 7)*.

#### The Missing Piece *(slide 7)*
- Before 1875, federal courts could hear federal-law cases only when Congress had granted jurisdiction over specific subject matter (patent, bankruptcy, civil rights enforcement) *(slide 7)*.
- The Judiciary Act of 1789 had conspicuously omitted general federal question jurisdiction despite Article III's authorization *(slide 7)*.
- **§ 1**: circuit courts received original, concurrent jurisdiction over all civil suits exceeding $500 arising under the Constitution or laws of the United States, or treaties -- concurrent with state courts, not exclusive *(slide 7)*.
- **§ 2**: removal jurisdiction -- defendants in state court could remove federal-question cases to federal circuit court; the mirror image of § 1 *(slide 7)*.

### VI. The Undoing: State Action Doctrine and Its Consequences

#### United States v. Cruikshank (1876) *(slide 8)*
- Section 6 of the 1870 Act (anti-KKK conspiracy) does not reach private conduct *(slide 8)*.
- The Fourteenth Amendment prohibits only state action; private conspiracies against Black citizens are beyond federal congressional power to reach via the Fourteenth Amendment's § 5 enforcement authority *(slide 8)*.
- The backdrop: the Colfax Massacre (April 1873) -- white supremacist paramilitaries killed approximately 150 Black men in Louisiana; only a few defendants convicted; Cruikshank reversed those convictions.

#### Civil Rights Cases (1883) *(slide 8)*
- Sections 1-2 of the 1875 Act struck down *(slide 8)*.
- Congress cannot regulate private discrimination in public accommodations under the Fourteenth Amendment, which reaches only state action *(slide 8)*.
- The Thirteenth Amendment's "badges of slavery" doctrine does not extend to refusal of inn or theater service *(slide 8)*.
- Justice Harlan's dissent: the majority's distinction between direct state discrimination and private discrimination is formalistic; railroads and inns operate under state-granted franchises and public licenses.

#### The Long Silence: 1883–1964 *(slide 8)*
- No federal civil rights legislation for 81 years *(slide 8)*.
- Public accommodations would not be federally guaranteed again until the Civil Rights Act of 1964 -- this time predicated on the Commerce Clause, not the Fourteenth Amendment *(slide 8)*, following Heart of Atlanta Motel v. United States and Katzenbach v. McClung.

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

**The 1866 Paradox.** The Civil Rights Act of 1866 was predicated on the Thirteenth Amendment (which reaches private conduct) but used the "under color of law" requirement in its criminal penalty provisions (which reaches only state actors) *(slide 3)*. Was Congress already contemplating that it needed the Fourteenth Amendment's state-action framework, or was this a drafting error?

**The Private Conduct Problem.** Section 6 of the 1870 Enforcement Act targets private conspiracies to deprive citizens of constitutional rights *(slide 5)*. Cruikshank struck it down because the Fourteenth Amendment reaches only state action *(slide 8)*. But the Thirteenth Amendment reaches private actors. Could Congress have predicated Section 6 on the Thirteenth Amendment instead? What doctrinal move would be required?

**The 81-Year Gap.** The Civil Rights Cases in 1883 struck down the 1875 Act *(slide 8)*. Congress did not pass another public accommodations law until 1964 -- and then used the Commerce Clause rather than § 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment *(slide 8)*. Why? What does this tell us about the practical effect of the state action doctrine on federal civil rights enforcement capacity?

**The Jury Non-Discrimination Provision.** Section 4 of the 1875 Act, prohibiting racial disqualification from jury service, was upheld in Strauder (1880) *(slide 6)*. The public accommodations provisions (§§ 1-2) were struck down in the Civil Rights Cases (1883) *(slide 8)*. What distinguishes jury service from access to inns and theaters for state action purposes? The answer involves the state's role in selecting jurors versus private proprietors' decisions.

## Key Takeaways

1. The Reconstruction statutes form a sequence in which each enactment responded to the constitutional inadequacy of its predecessor: the 1866 Act lacked a firm constitutional foundation; the 1870 Act re-enacted it under the Fourteenth Amendment while pushing into private conduct; the 1875 Act tested the outer limit of federal power over private discrimination.
2. The constitutional tension is structural: the Thirteenth Amendment reaches private conduct but authorizes only narrow slavery-related legislation; the Fourteenth Amendment authorizes broad civil rights legislation but only against state action.
3. Section 6 of the 1870 Act represents the most aggressive assertion of federal power over private conduct in the Reconstruction era; Cruikshank (1876) struck it down, effectively immunizing private white supremacist violence from federal prosecution.
4. The Civil Rights Cases (1883) locked in the state action doctrine for 81 years; when Congress finally returned to public accommodations in 1964, it used the Commerce Clause rather than the Fourteenth Amendment — a deliberate doctrinal detour around the Civil Rights Cases.
5. The Judiciary Act of 1875, enacted two days after the Civil Rights Act, created general federal question jurisdiction for the first time — completing the architectural precondition for systematic federal enforcement of constitutional rights.
