## Professor Emphasis

### From source

The deck is titled "Using the Commerce Clause to Achieve Civil Rights" (slide 1), and that is what it is — a focused unit on Heart of Atlanta and Katzenbach v. McClung as the Warren Court's commerce-power answer to the failure of the Reconstruction Amendments to do the work of dismantling private segregation. The deck opens with the long arc of "Black subjugation" and the legal architecture of subjugation, naming Prigg v. Pennsylvania, the Missouri Compromise, Dred Scott, debt slavery, federal housing programs that reinforced segregation, restrictive state-action doctrine, the neutering of Privileges and Immunities, separate but equal, and the failure to enforce amendments after the 1876 withdrawal of Union troops (slide 2). It then recites the operative texts of the Thirteenth and Fourteenth Amendments with their respective enforcement clauses (slides 3-4) and walks through the doomed Civil Rights Acts of 1866 and 1875, flagging the doubt whether the anti-slavery provisions of the Thirteenth Amendment supported the 1866 Act (slide 5). The deck pivots on the 1883 Civil Rights Cases, which struck down provisions of the 1875 Act and asked where Congress would get the power to regulate private exercises of freedom like deciding who comes to your inn — the deck's gloss is that an expansive understanding of commerce might have helped, but this was not really about commerce or increasing GDP and the Court was operating in a Dewitt/Paul/Dagenhart era of commerce doctrine (slide 6). The slide also flags the deck's own ellipsis around the Privileges or Immunities Clause and asks students to note links to Slaughterhouse and Cruikshank (slide 6). The 1964 setting is sketched as recalcitrant states plus hostility, denial of commercial services, and restrictive covenants (slide 7). The CRA1964 deals with hotels by attempting to define interstate commerce on its face and uses proxies for restaurants without requiring a particularized finding of effect on interstate commerce (slide 8). Heart of Atlanta distinguishes the 1875 Act on the ground that the commerce proxy may not have fit then but does now, there is no stare decisis problem because the earlier cases did not really consider the commerce argument, and private segregation discourages interstate travel by Black travelers — which is commerce (slide 9). The operative test is rational basis: Congress needs only a rational basis to find that racial discrimination by motels affects commerce, and may choose reasonable and appropriate means (slide 10). Douglas and Goldberg in concurrence would ground the opinion in Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment, which the deck flags as material to revisit when reading Eleventh-Amendment, state-action, and Thirteenth-Amendment "badges and incidents" cases (slide 11). Katzenbach v. McClung (Clark, J.) covers a barbecue rather than a hotel, and the deck inventories the Ollie's McClung facts in the deck's blunt rhetorical register — Black employees fine, Black takeout customers OK, Black sit-down customers not OK (slide 12). Clark's test asks whether the activity exerts a substantial economic effect on interstate commerce and treats activity as outside the commerce power only if completely within a particular state and not affecting other states and not necessary to interfere with for purposes of executing some general power — leading the deck to ask "is anything outside commerce power?" under a rational-basis layer (slide 13). The deck closes on the "conclusive presumption" issue: if the predictive power of a model is only slightly diminished by simplification, including elimination or consolidation of variables, Congress may simplify subject only to a rational basis test (slide 14).

## The Reconstruction Setup

The deck spends five slides setting up why the Commerce Clause becomes the civil-rights vehicle of choice by 1964. Slavery was reinforced not only by private conduct but by legal architecture — Prigg v. Pennsylvania upholding the Fugitive Slave Act, Missouri-Compromise admissions of slave states, Dred Scott denying citizenship to free persons of African descent, post-Reconstruction debt slavery in the South, and federal housing programs that reinforced private segregation (slide 2). The list explicitly extends through restrictive state-action doctrine, the neutering of Privileges and Immunities, separate but equal, the failure to enforce the amendments, and the withdrawal of Union troops after 1876 (slide 2).

The Thirteenth Amendment's operative text and Section 2 enforcement clause are laid out verbatim (slide 3), as are the Fourteenth Amendment's citizenship, Due Process, and Equal Protection clauses with their Section 5 enforcement power (slide 4). The Civil Rights Act of 1866 declared all persons born in the United States citizens and guaranteed the same right "to make and enforce contracts, to sue, be parties, and give evidence, inherit, purchase, lease, sell, hold, and convey real and personal property, and to full and equal benefit of all laws" as "is enjoyed by white citizens" — but with doubt whether the anti-slavery provisions reached that far (slide 5). The Civil Rights Act of 1875 went further, entitling all persons within United States jurisdiction to "the full and equal enjoyment" of inns, public conveyances, theatres, and other places of public amusement (slide 5).

The 1883 Civil Rights Cases struck down provisions of the 1875 Act, asking where Congress derived the power to regulate private decisions like who comes to your inn or sits at your barbecue (slide 6). The deck's gloss is that an expansive commerce understanding might have helped — but the case was not really about commerce or maximizing GDP, and the Court was operating in a Dewitt / Paul v. Virginia / Hammer v. Dagenhart era of cramped commerce doctrine (slide 6). The deck explicitly flags the ellipsis around the Privileges or Immunities Clause and instructs students to put Slaughterhouse and Cruikshank links into their notes — these are the cases that hollowed out the Fourteenth Amendment's alternative path (slide 6).

By 1964, the deck inventories what remained: recalcitrant states that did not honor Supreme Court interpretations of the Thirteenth-through-Fifteenth Amendments, hostility to persons of African descent (and others) that did not technically amount to slavery, denial of commercial services even to those who had the money to pay, and restrictive covenants running with the land that prevented sale or ownership (slide 7).

## The CRA of 1964 and Heart of Atlanta

The deck asks how Congress "covers itself" in enacting CRA 1964 (slide 8). For hotels like Heart of Atlanta, CRA 1964 attempts to define interstate commerce on its face, without requiring a particularized finding of effect (slide 8). For restaurants, the statute uses proxies — the deck flags proxies as the operative drafting move and previews the conclusive-presumption discussion that closes the lecture (slide 8).

Heart of Atlanta's reasoning distinguishes the 1883 Civil Rights Cases on three grounds the deck specifies: the commerce-clause proxy may not have been a good fit in 1875 but is a good fit now, there is no stare decisis problem because the earlier cases did not really consider the commerce argument, and private segregation discourages interstate travel by Black travelers — which is itself commerce (slide 9). The deck asks the framing question it then leaves open: is economy-maximization a valid theory of congressional authority? (slide 9). The deck's test is rational basis: Congress just needs a rational basis to find that racial discrimination by motels affects commerce, and Congress may choose reasonable and appropriate means to eliminate that evil (slide 10).

Douglas and Goldberg would have grounded the opinion in Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment rather than the Commerce Clause (slide 11). The deck asks why this would make a difference and flags three later contexts where the answer matters: Eleventh-Amendment cases, state-action doctrine, and the Thirteenth-Amendment "badges and incidents" theory (slide 11).

## Katzenbach v. McClung

McClung is the companion case decided the same Term: a Birmingham barbecue rather than an Atlanta motel (slide 12). The deck inventories Ollie's McClung's segregation policy in the deck's blunt register — Black employees were fine, Black takeout customers were OK, Black sit-down customers were not — followed by a question about what CRA 1964 actually said about when restaurants were acting in interstate commerce (slide 12). The deck does not pretend neutrality about Ollie's policy: it analogizes the sit-down refusal to treating Black customers as if they carried a contagion (slide 12).

Clark's test asks whether the activity "exerts a substantial economic effect on interstate commerce" and excludes activity from commerce power only if completely within a particular state, not affecting other states, and not necessary to interfere with for the purpose of executing some general power of government — at which point the deck asks: is anything outside commerce power? (slide 13). The test is overlaid with rational basis (slide 13).

## The "Conclusive Presumption" Closer

The deck's closing slide on the conclusive presumption is the unit's analytical payoff. If the predictive power of a model is only slightly diminished by simplification — including elimination or consolidation of variables — Congress may simplify, at least in some areas, subject only to a rational basis test (slide 14). The slide contrasts a complex model that is perfectly predictive against a simpler model that is imperfectly predictive (slide 14). This is the doctrinal license for proxies: Congress can use restaurant-receives-food-in-commerce as a stand-in for restaurant-affects-commerce without having to prove the latter in every case, because the predictive cost of the simplification is small enough to clear rational basis.

## Key Takeaways

- The Warren Court used the Commerce Clause as the civil-rights vehicle because the Fourteenth Amendment's Section 5 power had been narrowed by the 1883 Civil Rights Cases and the Slaughterhouse / Cruikshank line (slides 5-6).
- CRA 1964 was drafted with belt-and-suspenders: a facial commerce definition for hotels and statutory proxies for restaurants (slide 8).
- The operative test in both Heart of Atlanta and McClung is rational basis layered onto an aggregate substantial-economic-effect inquiry (slides 10, 13).
- The deck's closing analytical move is the conclusive-presumption / model-simplification idea: Congress may use proxies under rational basis when the predictive cost of simplification is small (slide 14).
- Douglas and Goldberg's Section-5 concurrences in Heart of Atlanta foreshadow later debates about Eleventh-Amendment immunity, state-action doctrine, and Thirteenth-Amendment "badges and incidents" enforcement (slide 11).
