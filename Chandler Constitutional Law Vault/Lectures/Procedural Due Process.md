## Professor Emphasis

### From source

The deck frames PDP as material students have seen before -- in Civil Procedure (Mullane on notice, Fuentes v. Shevin on prejudgment attachment, personal jurisdiction), Criminal Law (vagueness), and Property (Goldberg v. Kelly) *(slide 2)*. Mathews v. Eldridge (1976) is taught as the governing framework: the three factors are (1) the private interest affected; (2) the risk of erroneous deprivation through the procedures used, plus the probable value of additional safeguards; (3) the Government's interest, including fiscal and administrative burdens *(slide 8)*. The deck makes the framework decision-theoretic: the higher the cost of obtaining accuracy, the easier it is for government to use less accurate procedures; the higher the cost of a false negative to the individual, the harder; the higher the cost of a false positive to the government, the easier *(slide 11)*. Two threshold requirements gate Mathews: a constitutional interest in life, liberty, or property (including "new property" like tenure revocation or some welfare benefits) *(slide 9)*; and PDP "does not apply to creation of the law, only to the application of the law to you" *(slide 12)*. The "Friendly List" catalogs ten procedural protections *(slide 10)*. The DHS v. Thuraissigiam (2020) unit introduces big questions about non-citizens' constitutional rights, statutory limits on habeas, and the role of historical analysis *(slide 16)*.

## Lecture Outline

**Foundations and Prior Exposure**

Procedural due process appears across the law school curriculum: in Civil Procedure (Mullane on notice; Fuentes v. Shevin on prejudgment attachment; "sort of/kind of personal jurisdiction"), in Criminal Law (vagueness doctrine), and briefly in Property (Goldberg v. Kelly) *(slide 2)*. The Con Law lecture situates these as applications of a unified framework.

**Constitutional Interests: Life, Liberty, Property**

The Mathews analysis requires a threshold finding that the government action affects a constitutionally protected interest -- it must qualify as life, liberty, or property, including "new property" like tenure revocation or possibly some welfare benefits *(slide 9)*. Goldberg v. Kelly (1970) held that welfare benefits, once granted, constitute property interests protected by due process. Without identifying a protected life, liberty, or property interest, the Mathews balancing inquiry does not begin.

**Application Only: The Legislative-Adjudicative Distinction**

A critical structural limit: PDP "does not apply to creation of the law / Only to the application of the law to you" *(slide 12)*. When a legislature enacts a general law -- even one that drastically changes legal rights -- no PDP hearing is required. Only when the government applies the law to a particular person, depriving them of an identified interest, does the process owed arise.

**The Mathews Framework**

Mathews v. Eldridge asked whether the Due Process Clause requires an opportunity for an evidentiary hearing *prior* to termination of Social Security disability benefit payments *(slide 6)*. The deck lays out the trade-off: a prior hearing means no interim period during which some people are wrongly denied benefits, but it also slows cut-off for the many cases where the government determination is correct, and pre-determination hearings may be the most costly to operate *(slide 7)*. Powell's majority articulated the three-factor balancing test *(slide 8)*: (1) the private interest that will be affected by the official action; (2) the risk of an erroneous deprivation through the procedures used, and the probable value of additional or substitute procedural safeguards; (3) the Government's interest, including the function involved and the fiscal and administrative burdens of additional safeguards.

The "Friendly List" identifies the spectrum of procedural protections *(slide 10)*: an unbiased tribunal; notice of the proposed action and the grounds asserted; opportunity to present reasons against; the right to present evidence and call witnesses; the right to know opposing evidence; the right to cross-examine adverse witnesses; a decision based exclusively on evidence presented; opportunity to be represented by counsel; a record of the evidence; and written findings of fact and reasons. The Mathews calculus determines which subset is constitutionally required in any given context.

**DHS v. Thuraissigiam (2020): PDP at the Border**

Thuraissigiam, a Sri Lankan, entered from Mexico and was detained before getting 25 yards into the U.S.; he was brought before an asylum officer for a "credible fear" interview, and the deck notes he wanted habeas review to make claims that might conceivably qualify for asylum (Tamil Tiger membership) *(slide 18)*. The big issues *(slide 16)*: what rights non-citizens have under the constitution; whether unlawful presence matters; how we interpret the Constitution when rights are limited to those recognized "historically"; the constitutionality of 8 USC 1252(e)(2) precluding habeas on the underlying merits of an asylum claim; whether this was an unconstitutional suspension of habeas; and whether it violates procedural due process.

Alito (6-3) found no PDP violation and no unconstitutional suspension of habeas, in a "very history-focused and very specific" opinion *(slide 19)*. Building on Nishimura Ekiu v. United States (1892), the deck notes immigration authorities can find facts and make legal determinations -- the "Plenary power" + "finality" tradition *(slide 20)*. Alito's formulation: "While aliens who have established connections in this country have due process rights in deportation proceedings, the Court long ago held that Congress is entitled to set the conditions for an alien's lawful entry into this country and that, as a result, an alien at the threshold of initial entry cannot claim any greater rights under the Due Process Clause" -- Thuraissigiam was apprehended just 25 yards from the border and "has no entitlement to procedural rights other than those afforded by statute" *(slide 21)*. The deck flags the "legal fiction of non-presence": physical presence immediately upon entry does not constitute "entry" *(slide 22)*.

On habeas, Alito treated the statute as not "really a suspension," looking only to 1789 precedents because that's what respondent invoked, while also examining post-1789 precedents as "belt and suspenders" -- a move the deck flags as in tension with Boumediene *(slide 24)*. Breyer was "troubled by breadth of Alito here": "Don't need to decide scope of PDP and I regard this decision as limited to very brief entry and not very deep" *(slide 23)*. Sotomayor and Kagan were concerned about reliance on history, specificity, and treatment of aliens -- with the deck noting "We will see again in Glucksberg / Griswold" *(slide 25)*.

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

**Speeding Ticket Hypo:** X is stopped for speeding (42 in a 35 mph zone) during a rainstorm based on a radar gun and demands a trial with expert witnesses on radar gun accuracy and meteorological conditions *(slide 13)*. Arguments for the state: the private interest (a fine or points) is modest; the risk of erroneous deprivation is low given established radar technology; the marginal value of expert testimony is minimal; the government's administrative interest in processing thousands of traffic citations efficiently is high. Arguments for X: even if individually modest, cumulative impact on driving records and insurance is significant; the radar-in-rain issue is genuinely contested factually.

**Algorithmic Admission Hypo:** UCLA hypothetically uses an algorithm scoring applicants on SAT/ACT, grades, curriculum, peer-school performance, and machine scoring of the personal statement; Applicant Y is denied admission based on the score, complains the machine downgraded her "brilliant (but short) Haiku" personal statement and ignored her improving grade trajectory *(slide 14)*. The Mathews analysis requires identifying the constitutional interest, the risk of error in algorithmic scoring versus human review, the marginal value of individual review for edge cases, and the government's interest in efficient processing of thousands of applications.

**Prison Solitary Hypo:** Jones faces a month in solitary for a prison fight, claims particular sensitivity to sensory deprivation, and wants review from someone outside the prison system to determine that Smith was at fault and Jones used only minimal force in self-defense; the prison says it will decide internally and Jones cannot bring in evidence on fault and sensitivity *(slide 15)*. Mathews: the liberty interest in avoiding solitary confinement is significant; the risk of error in informal internal review is real; the value of outside review and evidence about culpability and medical sensitivity is probative; the government has legitimate interests in efficient prison discipline and security.

**Thuraissigiam Follow-Ons:** What if the immigration official expressed hostility to the applicant's racial/religious heritage? What if the official had been intoxicated at the time of decision? What if the official had never, in 500 cases, done anything other than order expedited removal? *(slide 21)*. The deck uses these to probe whether the categorical "no PDP rights at entry" rule survives obvious procedural defect.

## Key Takeaways

- Procedural due process requires identifying a protected life, liberty, or property interest before balancing begins; "new property" entitlements (welfare, tenure) qualify.
- The Mathews three-factor balancing test -- private interest, risk of erroneous deprivation, government interest -- determines what process is constitutionally required and produces context-specific outcomes.
- PDP applies only to adjudicative application of law to individuals, not to legislative creation of law.
- The Friendly List marks the outer boundary of procedural protections; the Mathews calculus determines which are required in a given context.
- Aliens at the threshold of initial entry have no PDP rights beyond those Congress provides (Thuraissigiam); Breyer limits the holding; the historical methodology is contested (Sotomayor).
