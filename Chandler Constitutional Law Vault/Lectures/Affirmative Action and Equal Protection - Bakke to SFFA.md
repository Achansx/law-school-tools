## Professor Emphasis

### From source

The deck opens by laying out the two competing theories that organize the doctrinal arc: anti-subordination (the EP Clause exists to lift up historically oppressed groups) versus anti-classification (the EP Clause forbids government from classifying people by race, full stop; discrimination against whites is the same as discrimination against blacks) (slide 2). It then frames four possible rationales for racial classification — specific reparations, general reparations, utilitarian/diversity, and the core tension between the two theories — flagging that these theories will be tested across five decades of doctrine (slide 2). Chandler stresses Powell's key intellectual move in Bakke — strict scrutiny for ALL racial classifications, with the Constitution protecting individuals rather than groups (slide 5). The deck emphasizes the empirical weakness of the diversity rationale's causal chain (slides 8-9) and the self-segregation / affinity-housing contradiction (slide 9). The deck stresses the 25-year sunset language as a "loaded gun" that SFFA pulls 20 years later (slide 19) and the transparency paradox flagged by Ginsburg's Gratz dissent (slide 21). ## Lecture Outline

### The Competing Theories

**Anti-subordination.** The Equal Protection Clause exists to remedy the subordination of historically oppressed groups (slide 2). Under this theory, race-conscious programs that lift up minorities are not only permissible but may be required — they serve the Clause's core purpose. Discrimination against whites and discrimination against Blacks are not the same constitutional wrong because they do not reflect the same history of subordination.

**Anti-classification.** The Equal Protection Clause forbids the government from classifying people by race, period (slide 2). The individual is the unit of constitutional protection, not the group. This is the principle Justice Harlan articulated in dissent in Plessy ("Our Constitution is color-blind"), which becomes the majority position in SFFA (slide 29).

**Why might government classify by race?** The deck enumerates the candidate rationales (slide 2): (1) specific reparations — the institution itself discriminated and seeks to undo that harm; (2) general reparations — American society broadly discriminated (slavery, Jim Crow, redlining), so government should compensate affected groups; (3) utilitarian / diversity — racial mixing yields better education, peace, leadership, and economic outcomes. The diversity rationale is the only one to survive to SFFA.

### Regents of the University of California v Bakke (1978)

**The Facts.** UC Davis Medical School reserved 16 of 100 seats for minority applicants (Black, Mexican-American, Asian, American Indian) in a separate track with a lower GPA threshold (2.5 versus the regular cutoff) (slide 4). White applicants were completely excluded from competing for those 16 seats. Allan Bakke, a white male, was rejected in 1973 and 1974 despite benchmark scores of 468/500 (slide 4). In 1973, four special admissions seats remained unfilled yet Bakke was never offered the opportunity to compete for them (slide 4).

**The 4-1-4 Split.** No majority opinion (slide 11). Four Justices (Brennan, White, Marshall, Blackmun) would have upheld the Davis program as a valid remedy for past societal discrimination (slide 11). Four Justices (Stevens + 3) would have struck it down on Title VI statutory grounds without reaching the Constitution (slide 11). Powell stood alone in the center (slide 11).

**Powell's Key Move: Strict Scrutiny for ALL Racial Classifications.** Racial and ethnic classifications of any sort are inherently suspect — whether they burden minorities or majorities (slide 5). "The guarantee of equal protection cannot mean one thing when applied to one individual and something else when applied to a person of another color" (slide 5). This is the anti-classification principle: the Constitution protects individuals, not groups (slide 5). Discrimination against whites IS the same as discrimination against Blacks under the EP Clause (slide 5).

**Reparations Rationales Rejected.** Specific reparations require institutional findings of past discrimination — UC Davis never found itself to have discriminated (slide 6). General reparations ("societal discrimination") are too amorphous to justify racial quotas: "That amorphous injury reaching endlessly into the past is too broad to justify racial classifications" (slide 6).

**The Diversity Rationale Survives.** Powell finds diversity a compelling interest rooted in academic freedom and the First Amendment (slide 7). Race is ONE element among many contributing to a diverse student body — geographic origin, work experience, background (slide 7). The quota fails because white applicants are insulated from competing for 16 seats (slide 10). The Harvard Model — race as a "plus factor" in holistic review where all applicants compete for every seat — is constitutional (slide 10).

**The Irony.** Under this framework, the strongest beneficiaries of the diversity rationale are majority students, not the minority students admitted (slide 7). The reason to admit minority students becomes the improvement of the educational experience for everyone, including whites (slide 7).

**The Empirical Problem.** The causal evidence for diversity producing better learning is remarkably weak (slide 8). Self-reported perceptions dominate the literature — students say they feel they learned more, but correlation between self-perception and actual learning is poor (slide 8). The required causal chain — racial identity → distinct experiences → unique cognitive perspectives → richer discussion → better learning — is unproven at every link (slide 8). The "rich Black kid" problem: a Black student from Exeter and a white student from Exeter may share more analytical frameworks than either shares with a first-generation student from rural Appalachia (slide 8). If the goal is cognitive diversity, SES and geographic diversity likely deliver more per unit of admissions preference (slide 8). Self-segregation in dining halls and affinity housing undercuts the mechanism the theory depends on (slide 9).

**The Temporal Vision.** Powell implicitly treats affirmative action as transitional: diversity is compelling at this moment in history, but racial classifications "cannot be justified as matters of perpetual necessity" (slide 11). This seeds O'Connor's 25-year sunset in Grutter and SFFA's reckoning (slide 11).

### Adarand Constructors Inc v Pena (1995)

**The Facts.** A federal highway contracting program gave financial bonuses for hiring minority-owned subcontractors (slide 14). Adarand Constructors (white-owned) lost a guardrail subcontract to a less competitive minority-certified firm (slide 14). Before Adarand, federal racial classifications received only intermediate scrutiny — "important objective" plus "substantially related" (slide 15).

**Three Principles.** The Court announces Skepticism (any racial preference must receive the most searching judicial examination), Consistency (standard of review does not depend on the race of those burdened or benefited), and Congruence (Equal Protection analysis is the same under the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments — federal and state governments are held to the same standard) (slide 14). Overrules Metro Broadcasting (1990), which had applied intermediate scrutiny to federal classifications (slide 14).

**The Significance Beyond Education.** After Adarand, ALL governmental racial classifications — federal, state, local — must survive strict scrutiny (slide 15). Adarand + Bakke together: the anti-classification principle governs everywhere, with diversity as a narrow educational escape hatch (slide 15). Outside education, what compelling interest justifies racial preferences in highway contracts, employment, or government contracting? (slide 15)

### Grutter v Bollinger and Gratz v Bollinger (2003)

**Grutter (the Law School, 5-4, O'Connor writing).** Barbara Grutter, a white applicant with a 3.8 GPA and 161 LSAT, was rejected from U. Michigan Law School (slide 17). Had she been a minority, she would have had a much higher probability of admission — the equivalent of 12 LSAT points (slide 17). The Law School used holistic review with race as one factor among many, seeking a "critical mass" of minority students — no quota, no fixed points, individually reviewed files (slide 17). Minority enrollment varied from 13.5% to 20.1% between 1993-1998 — fluctuation framed as evidence of genuine individualized review (slide 17). Holding: the diversity rationale is a compelling interest (endorses Powell's Bakke rationale as governing law); the Law School's holistic program survives strict scrutiny (slide 17).

**O'Connor's 25-Year Sunset.** "We expect that 25 years from now, the use of racial preferences in university admissions will no longer be necessary" (slide 19). This sunset becomes a loaded gun pointed at the diversity rationale — SFFA pulls the trigger in 2023, only 20 years later (slide 19).

**The Skeptic's View.** Statistical evidence showed minority enrollment tracked the applicant pool closely — suspiciously close to racial balancing (slide 18). One could predict law school admission in Grutter with 86% accuracy just from LSAT, UGPA, and race (slide 23). If the output is indistinguishable from a formula, the "holistic" label may not save it (slide 23).

**Gratz (the Undergraduate Program, unconstitutional, Rehnquist writing).** Michigan undergrad used a 150-point selection index with 20 automatic points for underrepresented minority status — equivalent to a perfect SAT score (slide 20). Unconstitutional because the bonus is predetermined, categorical, and mechanical: no individual assessment of what that particular applicant contributes to diversity (slide 20). A non-Black applicant could never receive those 20 points — the system forecloses competition for that portion of the scale (slide 20).

**The Transparency Paradox.** Justice Ginsburg in dissent: "If honesty is the best policy, surely Michigan's accurately described, fully disclosed program is preferable to achieving similar numbers through winks, nods, and disguises" (slide 21). Gratz punishes the undergraduate program for being transparent about what it does, while Grutter rewards the law school for dressing the same functional outcome in holistic language (slide 21). Is the constitutional difference between them form or substance? (slide 21)

### Schuette v Coalition to Defend Affirmative Action (2014)

After Grutter upheld holistic affirmative action, Michigan voters passed Proposal 2 in 2006 — a state constitutional amendment banning racial preferences in public university admissions (slide 24). The EP Clause does NOT require affirmative action, even where diversity might be a compelling interest; voters may ban it (slide 24). Diversity may be a compelling interest sufficient to JUSTIFY a race-conscious program — but it is not a compelling interest that COMPELS one (slide 24). The Sotomayor dissent: "The way to stop discrimination on the basis of race is to speak openly and candidly on the subject of race" (slide 24). Roberts had already answered in Parents Involved: "The way to stop discrimination on the basis of race is to stop discriminating on the basis of race" (slide 24).

### Students for Fair Admissions v Harvard (2023)

**How Harvard Actually Used Race.** First readers assigned scores in six categories (academic, extracurricular, athletic, school support, personal, overall); race entered at every stage (slide 26). Regional subcommittees discussed applicants by race; the goal was "no dramatic drop-off" from prior year's minority numbers (slide 26). The "Lop" (final winnowing) used only four data points: legacy status, athlete status, financial aid, and race (slide 26). Harvard's legacy preference (ALDC: athletes, legacies, Dean's list, children of faculty) overwhelmingly benefited white applicants — a direct product of Harvard's historically white student body (slide 26). The First Circuit found that Harvard's race consideration led to an 11.1% decrease in Asian American admissions (slide 26).

**The Diversity Rationale Unravels.** Harvard's stated interests — training future leaders, preparing graduates for a pluralistic society, better education through diversity, producing new knowledge from diverse outlooks — are not judicially manageable standards (slide 27). How does a court measure whether leaders have been trained or new knowledge produced? (slide 27) The interests are "unavoidably intertwined with racial balancing" (slide 27). Roberts invokes Rucho v. Common Cause: some standards are simply too incoherent for courts to police (slide 27).

**Race as Negative and Racial Stereotyping.** Admissions are zero-sum: a benefit to some applicants necessarily disadvantages others (slide 28). Harvard's own data showed that race consideration resulted in fewer Asian American AND white students admitted (slide 28). The programs rest on the premise that "a Black student can usually bring something that a white person cannot offer" — this IS stereotyping (slide 28). Race categories are overbroad; grouping all "Asians" or all "Hispanics" ignores vast internal diversity (slide 28). Identity should not be reduced to the color of skin (slide 28).

**No End Point.** Harvard admitted no sunset date and the same admissions approach had been used for 50 years (slide 29). UNC "has not set forth a proposed time period" to end race-conscious admissions (slide 29). O'Connor's 25-year sunset arrives and neither university has moved toward it (slide 29).

**Anti-Classification Wins.** "Our Constitution is color-blind" — Justice Harlan's Plessy dissent, now adopted by the majority (slide 29). Thomas concurrence: "Every time the government places citizens on racial registers and makes race relevant to burdens or benefits, it demeans us all" (slide 29). The anti-subordination reading of the EP Clause is definitively rejected for purposes of higher education admissions (slide 29).

**What Remains: The Essay Passage.** "Nothing prohibits universities from considering an applicant's discussion of how race affected the applicant's life, so long as that discussion is concretely tied to a quality of character or unique ability" (slide 30). A student who overcame racial discrimination may receive a benefit tied to courage and determination (slide 30). The anti-circumvention warning: "Universities may not simply establish through application essays or other means the operation of a racial classification that would do indirectly what they may not do directly" (slide 30).

**The Military Academy Exception.** The Court explicitly reserved the question of race-based admissions at military academies — no academy was a party and no lower court had addressed it (slide 31). The national security rationale: a pipeline of officers who are both highly qualified and racially diverse is essential to military readiness, applying not just at service academies but at civilian universities with ROTC programs (slide 31). If diversity is compelling for the military, why not for police departments, the FBI, or the diplomatic corps? (slide 31)

### The Marshall Legacy Scholarship Hypothetical (IQS)

Franklin State University (a state law school committed to training lawyers to serve underserved communities) hosts the Marshall Legacy Scholarship Program established by three private law firms (patent, maritime, corporate tax), offering $15,000 annually (slide 34). The essay prompt asks applicants to describe "hardships faced growing up" and interest in patent, maritime, or tax law (slide 34). Scholarships are named after three prominent minority alumni (slide 34). The firms' stated rationale: "Patent law, maritime law, and corporate tax have been far too homogeneous for far too long" (slide 34). Results: 18 of 20 recipients (90%) are racial minorities, though only 50% of applicants are minorities (slide 34).

**The State Action Question.** The Law School advertises the program, hosts the annual ceremony (with the Dean introducing recipients), and provides free conference-room space — but does not select recipients, sit on the committee, handle funds, or have a contractual relationship (slide 35). Burton v. Wilmington — state "insinuated itself into a position of interdependence" — versus Rendell-Baker v. Kohn — funding and regulation alone are insufficient; state must control the specific challenged conduct (slide 35).

**Discriminatory Purpose or Disparate Impact?** Washington v. Davis requires both discriminatory purpose AND discriminatory effect (slide 36). Evidence suggesting purpose under Arlington Heights: 90% minority rate from a 50% pool, scholarships named exclusively after minority alumni, and the practice-area disconnect — why do patent/maritime/tax firms care about hardships growing up? (slide 36). Evidence against: facially neutral essay prompt, two non-minority recipients, and 90% is not Yick Wo's 100%-versus-0% pattern (slide 36).

**The SFFA Anti-Circumvention Problem.** Is the "hardship" essay functioning as a racial proxy — exactly what SFFA warns against? (slide 37). The diversity rationale for the firms (they want to diversify their specialties) may be genuine or pretextual (slide 37). The counterargument: SFFA permits applicants to discuss how race shaped their lives, tied to character — a hardship essay is arguably that (slide 37).

**Counseling the Dean.** Recommended steps to reduce legal exposure (slide 38): formalize the relationship through an MOU requiring race-neutral selection criteria — demonstrated resilience, intellectual interest, and concrete career plans — not race; require contemporaneous records of deliberations and selection reasons; stop characterizing the program as fulfilling the Law School's own diversity mission, because this deepens state-action nexus AND suggests race-conscious purpose; describe it instead as an independent initiative the Law School supports because it cultivates expertise in underrepresented practice areas. Do NOT advise total withdrawal — the Law School has legitimate interests in supporting professional development scholarships (slide 38).

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

The Marshall Legacy Scholarship hypothetical (IQS) is the primary class application. The key analytical questions are: (1) Is the Law School a state actor with respect to this specific conduct, or merely a host? (2) Was there discriminatory purpose, and can the 90%/50% disparity plus the practice-area disconnect establish purpose without direct evidence? (3) Does the "hardship" essay operate as a racial proxy in violation of SFFA's anti-circumvention principle, or is it genuinely tied to character in the way SFFA permits?

Professor Chandler also asks students to consider whether, if the causal evidence for the diversity rationale is as weak as the lecture suggests, SFFA was actually right to strike it down -- not just on anti-classification grounds, but because the empirical predicate of the compelling interest was never adequately established. If the government cannot actually show that diversity in higher education produces better learning outcomes (as distinct from attitudinal changes), was the interest ever genuinely compelling?

## Key Takeaways

- Anti-classification defeats anti-subordination, definitively, in SFFA. The EP Clause does not recognize a difference between discrimination against whites and discrimination against Blacks for purposes of strict scrutiny.
- The diversity rationale was empirically fragile from the beginning: its causal chain (racial identity → distinct perspectives → better learning) was never rigorously established, and the self-segregation problem undermined the mechanism the theory depended on.
- The transparency paradox (Gratz penalizes candor; Grutter rewards opacity) is the doctrine's most intellectually troubling feature. Post-SFFA, the anti-circumvention principle attempts to prevent the same result, but essay-based proxies will be difficult to police.
- O'Connor's 25-year sunset -- planted in 2003, fired 20 years early in 2023 -- was the doctrinal mechanism that enabled SFFA: universities could not show they were working toward a race-blind future.
- Adarand's strict-scrutiny-everywhere principle means no government entity can rely on intermediate scrutiny for racial classifications outside education. The diversity rationale is specific to universities.
- The military academy exception is a live wedge: if national security can justify race-conscious admissions, the Grutter "leadership training" rationale may survive in national security contexts even after SFFA.
