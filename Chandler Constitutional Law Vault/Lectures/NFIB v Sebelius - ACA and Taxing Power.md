# NFIB v Sebelius - ACA and Taxing Power

## Professor Emphasis

### From source

The deck flags at least two constitutional issues, plus statutory interpretation and severability *(slide 6)*. On the individual mandate, Roberts argues the naked mandate is unprecedented and that "Power to regulate pre-existing commerce is not a power to create it"; the integrated mandate fails because even if necessary, it is not "Proper" *(slide 9)*. The deck flags this as a "huge ruling" and offers the editorial gloss that the "proper" jurisprudence may be modern Lochnerism or City-of-Boernism *(slide 9)*. The "broccoli argument" is the dramatic device the deck uses to challenge the mandate's Commerce Clause justification, with a parody statute slide *(slides 9-10)*. The saving-construction-as-tax move is presented with skepticism: can something Congress labels as a "tax" actually be a penalty? *(slide 11)*. The five factors Roberts uses to label the payment a tax are listed: less than insurance, Congress's tolerance for non-compliance, taxing-power preferred over Commerce-Clause power, no scienter requirement, and IRS collection *(slide 14)*. On the spending question, Roberts channels Federalist 45 and asks what percent of its budget the average state would lose by refusing to expand Medicaid *(slide 23)*. Scalia's prophecy that §5000A imposes "not a simple tax but a mandate to which a penalty is attached" is flagged as the seed of the later Texas v. California lawsuit *(slide 20)*.

### Editor's framing

This page treats NFIB as the single most important modern enumerated-powers decision -- a synthesis the deck implies through its scale but does not state. The packaging of the case as having "three centers of gravity" (Commerce Clause limit, Taxing Power saving construction, Spending Clause coerciveness) is editorial structure. The framing of NFIB as "the first post-New Deal decision to treat the Commerce Clause as having a doctrinal limit that bites on a major piece of federal legislation, and the first ever to strike a spending-condition as unconstitutionally coercive" sharpens what the deck only implies. The doctrinal-placement analysis at the end (NFIB sits at the intersection of three arcs, every holding traces back to the Marshall Court) is editorial synthesis beyond the deck.

## Lecture Outline

The lecture begins with political and legislative context. The ACA requires many people in the United States to either have government-approved forms of health insurance or pay a "penalty" -- the greater of a flat amount or a percentage of income, capped by the national average price of a certain kind of health insurance policy *(slide 2)*. The mandate is part of a complex regulatory scheme that prohibits insurers from charging different rates based on medical factors, raising adverse-selection problems Congress sought to mitigate *(slide 2)*. The ACA also reforms Medicaid: states that want continued federal match must expand Medicaid to cover persons earning up to 1.33 (or 1.38) of FPL, including childless adults *(slides 3-4)*. Various states note that even 10% of a large number is a large number *(slide 4)*. Randy Barnett develops the argument that the individual mandate is unconstitutional and is mostly laughed at by academics *(slide 5)*.

The case presents at least two constitutional issues: power under the Commerce Clause plus N&P (or under the Taxing Power) to enact the individual mandate, and whether the Medicaid conditional spending requirement is coercive; there is also a statutory interpretation issue (Tax Anti-Injunction Act) and a severability issue *(slide 6)*.

On the Commerce Clause question, Chief Justice Roberts (joined by four dissenters on this point) holds that the mandate is not a regulation of commerce. The deck summarizes Roberts on the naked mandate: it is unprecedented to force people not in commerce to buy something, and the power to regulate pre-existing commerce is not a power to create it *(slide 9)*. On the integrated mandate, the structural argument based on McCulloch and Printz applies, and even if the mandate is necessary, it is not "Proper" *(slide 9)*. As Chief Justice Roberts put it, "The power to regulate commerce presupposes the existence of commercial activity to be regulated" ([NFIB lines=276](https://app.midpage.ai/document/national-federation-of-independent-business-809122?utm_source=mcp&lines=276)). To read the clause otherwise would convert Congress's authority into a general police power: if Congress can compel purchase of health insurance because everyone will eventually use health care, then Congress can compel purchase of any product because everyone is potentially a market participant. This is the broccoli hypothetical *(slides 9-10)*.

On the Taxing Power, Roberts (joined by the four liberal justices) holds that the mandate is functionally a tax and therefore constitutional. The deck enumerates the factors: the payment will be less than the price of insurance and doesn't look like a penalty; Congress must have thought it tolerable that millions would pay; giving Congress taxing power is less problematic than Commerce Clause power; there is no scienter requirement; and it is collected via the IRS *(slide 14)*. The deck also flags Roberts's reassurances: we already have lots of tax incentives, the Court promises to detect penalties disguised as taxes if it sees them, and taxes aren't so bad as regulation *(slide 14)*. The principle: if a provision can be reasonably construed to fall within Congress's enumerated powers, the Court should so construe it.

On the Medicaid expansion, a seven-justice coalition holds that the Spending Clause is violated by the coerciveness of the condition. Roberts channels Federalist 45 *(slide 23)*. The deck asks what percent of its budget the average state would lose by failure to expand Medicaid, and whether Roberts establishes a test *(slide 23)*.<!-- corrected 2026-05-26: removed unsupported "roughly 10% of the average state budget" figure; not in slides, and Medicaid is typically a much larger (~20–30%) share of state spending --> The remedy is severability: the expansion itself is preserved, but states that decline the expansion keep their existing Medicaid funding.

The lecture closes with doctrinal projection. NFIB revives the Commerce Clause as a meaningful limit in a way Lopez and Morrison could not, because the ACA is the biggest Commerce Clause legislation since the New Deal. The Taxing Power holding expands that route for future legislation while confining its use as a constitutional workaround. The Spending Clause holding is the first time in American history that a federal spending condition is held unconstitutionally coercive, and it opens a new line of litigation against conditional-funding schemes. Congress later repealed the individual mandate in 2017, expanded ACA subsidies in 2021 and 2022 (set to expire at the end of 2025), and the program has grown more expensive but enrollment is at an all-time high *(slide 26)*. Texas argued the entire ACA is unconstitutional after repeal of the mandate, but the Supreme Court held Texas lacked standing in California v. Texas (2021) *(slide 26)*.

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

**Hypothetical 1 (Commerce Clause, same side as NFIB): EV purchase mandate.** Congress enacts a law requiring every adult to purchase a new electric vehicle at least once every fifteen years, with a cash penalty for non-compliance, relying expressly on the Commerce Clause. Reasoning: the mandate fails under NFIB's activity-inactivity line. The Commerce Clause authorizes regulation of existing commercial activity but does not authorize Congress to compel entry into a market that the individual would otherwise stay out of. As Roberts framed the limit, "The power to regulate commerce presupposes the existence of commercial activity to be regulated" ([NFIB lines=276](https://app.midpage.ai/document/national-federation-of-independent-business-809122?utm_source=mcp&lines=276)). The broccoli logic applies: if Congress can compel purchase of vehicles because everyone needs transportation, it can compel purchase of anything anyone might eventually need. NFIB shuts the door on this style of Commerce Clause legislation.

**Hypothetical 2 (Taxing Power workaround, same side as NFIB): Tax on non-purchase of EVs.** Congress instead imposes a tax on anyone who does not purchase an electric vehicle during each fifteen-year period, structured on the ACA model: the amount is reported on the federal income-tax return, calibrated by income and household size, capped at the average cost of a modest EV, collected by the IRS, and carries no criminal or scienter consequences. Reasoning: under NFIB's functional-tax analysis, this is likely upheld under the Taxing Power. The three Roberts factors line up: tax-return reporting, income-calibrated magnitude, non-punitive operation. Congress's label does not control if the provision functions as a tax. The hypo clarifies Roberts's saving construction: the same policy result that fails under the Commerce Clause can succeed under the Taxing Power if it is drafted as a tax rather than as a regulatory command backed by a penalty.

**Hypothetical 3 (Spending Clause coerciveness, same side as NFIB): Highway-funding leverage for climate policy.** Congress conditions all existing federal highway funds on a state's adoption of stringent carbon-neutrality policies, threatening to cut off roughly 30% of each state's transportation budget for non-compliance. Reasoning: apply the South Dakota v Dole four-factor test as modified by NFIB's coerciveness overlay. The condition is unconstitutionally coercive because it leverages existing funds at a scale that leaves states no practical choice. NFIB treated the Medicaid leverage — a very large share of state spending — as crossing into compulsion; cutting 30% of the transportation budget is on the same side of the line.<!-- corrected 2026-05-26: removed "10%-of-state-budget" figure; not in slides --> The condition would likely be struck as coercive under the NFIB gloss on Dole, though the severability remedy (states that decline keep existing funds) is the likely disposition.

**Hypothetical 4 (Spending Clause, opposite side of NFIB): Small new program with no existing leverage.** Congress creates a new, modest federal grant program for rural broadband expansion and conditions receipt of those grant funds on state adoption of specific broadband-deployment standards. No existing state funding is threatened. Reasoning: under the Dole factors and NFIB's coerciveness analysis, this is classic permissible conditional spending. The state has a real choice because declining the new funds costs nothing the state currently receives. NFIB's coerciveness holding is keyed specifically to threatened loss of existing substantial funding, not to any spending condition. The hypo clarifies the outer bound of NFIB: it is a coerciveness limit, not a general prohibition on conditional spending.

**Hypothetical 5 (Fence-sitter, combining all three doctrines): Federal preventive-care purchase program.** Congress creates a mixed scheme. Part A requires every employer above a certain size to purchase a federally-defined preventive-care bundle for employees (Commerce Clause predicate). Part B offers an income-based tax credit for individual purchase of the same bundle (Taxing Power predicate). Part C conditions existing federal Medicaid funds on state adoption of a parallel preventive-care requirement (Spending Clause predicate). Reasoning: this hypo forces students to separate the three doctrinal inquiries NFIB governs. Part A faces the Commerce Clause activity-inactivity line and the question whether employer-level regulation is distinguishable from individual mandates. Part B is likely upheld under the Taxing Power if structured as a genuine tax credit. Part C depends on the magnitude of Medicaid leverage and whether the expansion is a modification of the existing program or a new condition on existing funds (the NFIB plurality's key distinction). The hypo shows why NFIB is the most important modern enumerated-powers decision: it breaks one legislative act into three separate constitutional inquiries and supplies the test for each.

## Key Takeaways

- The Commerce Clause authorizes regulation of existing commercial activity, not the creation of commerce by compelling purchase. The activity-inactivity line is now doctrine.
- The Taxing Power can sustain what the Commerce Clause cannot, if the provision operates as a tax (reported on a return, income-calibrated, non-punitive magnitude) and a reasonable construction places it within the power.
- The Spending Clause is not unlimited. Conditions attached to federal funding cross from persuasion into coercion when they threaten loss of existing funds at a scale that leaves states no practical choice.
- The Medicaid coerciveness holding is the first time a federal spending condition has ever been struck as unconstitutionally coercive, and it creates a new doctrinal limit grafted onto Dole.
- Roberts's decisive vote on each issue reflects a cross-ideological coalition: the Commerce Clause holding with the conservatives, the Taxing Power with the liberals, the Spending Clause with a cross-ideological majority.
- NFIB is the modern continuation of the Gibbons/Wickard/Lopez/Morrison/Raich Commerce Clause arc and the first decision to give the among-the-states limit real bite against major legislation.

- [[Topics/Commerce Clause|Commerce Clause]]: the modern endpoint of the Topic's doctrinal arc. - [[Topics/Federalism|Federalism]]: NFIB is a modern anchor of federalism doctrine. It simultaneously tightens three different limits (Commerce Clause activity-inactivity, Taxing Power characterization, Spending Clause coerciveness), each of which operates to preserve state policy space against federal encroachment.
- [[Topics/Necessary and Proper Clause|Necessary and Proper Clause]]: Roberts's opinion addresses and rejects the argument that the individual mandate is a necessary and proper adjunct to the guaranteed-issue and community-rating reforms. The Necessary and Proper Clause cannot bootstrap a power Congress otherwise lacks; it is a means clause, not an independent substantive power. Students should read this alongside McCulloch to see the limit the Clause does (and does not) carry.
- [[Topics/Implied Powers|Implied Powers]]: NFIB is an important counterpoint to the McCulloch tradition of broad implied-power reading. The Commerce Clause holding makes clear that even a broadly read enumerated power has outer textual limits.
- [[Lectures/Gibbons v Ogden - Commerce Clause Foundations|Gibbons v Ogden - Commerce Clause Foundations]]: the source. NFIB reaffirms and operationalizes Marshall's among-the-states limit. The lecture pair reads as a single arc: Gibbons (functional definition plus limit) to NFIB (limit bites on a major federal statute).
- [[Lectures/Enumerated Powers - New Deal|Enumerated Powers - New Deal]]: the mid-arc connection. The New Deal Commerce Clause cases (Jones and Laughlin, Wickard) pushed the doctrine to its broadest point; NFIB is the first post-New Deal decision to identify a doctrinal limit that bites on major legislation.
- [[Cases/McCulloch v Maryland (1819)|McCulloch]]: the interpretive predecessor for the Necessary and Proper and Taxing Power analyses. Marshall's Taxing-Power discussion in McCulloch (the power to tax includes the power to destroy) supplies background for understanding what the Taxing Power can and cannot do in NFIB.

**Doctrinal placement.** NFIB sits at the intersection of three doctrinal arcs. On the Commerce Clause, it is the fourth case in the modern limit series (Lopez 1995, Morrison 2000, Raich 2005, NFIB 2012) and the first to strike at a piece of federal legislation of national scale. Roberts's activity-inactivity line is a principle, not merely a factual distinction: it generalizes the Gibbons premise that regulation presupposes a thing being regulated. On the Taxing Power, it is the modern affirmation that Congress can achieve through a functionally-calibrated tax what it cannot achieve through a regulatory command; the practical-operation test sets up a saving construction that has become a template for post-NFIB legislation. On the Spending Clause, it is the first decision ever to hold a federal spending condition unconstitutionally coercive, grafting a new coerciveness limit onto the four-factor Dole test and measuring coerciveness by the fraction of a state's existing budget at risk. The three holdings together make NFIB the most consequential enumerated-powers decision since the New Deal, and they explain why the case has become the doctrinal centerpiece of modern Commerce Clause, Taxing Power, and conditional-spending analysis. Every one of the three holdings traces back to the Marshall Court: the Commerce Clause limit to Gibbons's among-the-states reservation, the Taxing Power saving construction to McCulloch-era power reading, and the Spending Clause coerciveness limit to the general principle that federal power must leave states a real choice.
