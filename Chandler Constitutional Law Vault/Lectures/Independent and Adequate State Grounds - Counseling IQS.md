## Lecture Outline

### I. Independent and Adequate State Grounds Doctrine

**The baseline rule:** The Supreme Court's appellate jurisdiction over state court decisions extends only to "federal questions" -- questions of federal law (constitutional, statutory, treaty). When a state court ruling rests entirely on an independent and adequate state law ground, the federal question is irrelevant: even if SCOTUS reversed on the federal question, the judgment would stand on the state ground. SCOTUS declines jurisdiction over such cases because any ruling would be advisory.

**What makes a state ground "independent"?** The state law ground must not depend on, or be tied to, the federal question. If the state court's ruling on the state law question itself required interpreting or applying federal law, the state ground is not independent.

**What makes it "adequate"?** The state ground must be sufficient to support the judgment -- the case would come out the same way even if the federal question were resolved in the plaintiff's favor.

### II. Michigan v Long (1983): The Presumption

***Michigan v Long* (1983):** Officers stopped Long for reckless driving, searched the passenger compartment of his car after seeing a knife, and found marijuana. The Michigan Supreme Court suppressed the evidence, citing both the Fourth Amendment and the Michigan Constitution's analogous search-and-seizure provision. The state court's opinion did not clearly distinguish between the two grounds.

**Holding (O'Connor, J.):** When a state court decision "fairly appears to rest primarily on federal law, or to be interwoven with the federal law, and when the adequacy and independence of any possible state law ground is not clear from the face of the opinion," the Supreme Court will presume the decision rested on federal grounds and take jurisdiction to review the federal question.

**The safe harbor for states:** If the state court wants to insulate its ruling from SCOTUS review, it must clearly and expressly state that the decision rests on bona fide, separate, and adequate state grounds. An ambiguous opinion invites federal review.

**Rationale:** If SCOTUS could not review state court decisions interpreting federal law, state courts would be the final word on the meaning of the Federal Constitution -- an unacceptable fragmentation of federal law.

**Brennan dissent:** Argued the presumption should run the other way -- toward state autonomy. When a state court cites both state and federal grounds, the Court should presume the state ground is independent and adequate, not invite itself in.

### III. The Counseling Exercise: Two Fictional Texas Cases

#### Case A: Ex Parte Rodriguez (Texas Court of Criminal Appeals)

**Facts (hypothetical):** Rodriguez, convicted of possession, challenges (1) a warrantless entry into his home by officers acting on reasonable suspicion that he was unlawfully present in the United States, and (2) the State's failure to disclose key witness statements until the eve of trial *(p. 1)*. On the search, the Texas Court of Criminal Appeals holds the warrantless home entry was *permissible* under the Fourth Amendment, reasoning that the federal interest in controlling national borders plus the transient nature of immigration status creates an exception to the usual warrant requirement when officers have reasonable suspicion of unlawful presence *(p. 1)*. On the discovery claim, the court holds the State violated Article 39.14 of the Texas Code of Criminal Procedure by withholding witness statements until the eve of trial, prejudicing the defense *(p. 1)*. The court reverses on the state discovery ground alone; the Fourth Amendment ruling went *against* Rodriguez *(p. 1)*.

The cert prompt asks: will the U.S. Supreme Court hear this case to decide whether a "suspected illegal immigrant" exception exists to the Fourth Amendment warrant requirement *(p. 1)*.

**IASG analysis:**
- Federal question present: whether there is a "suspected illegal immigrant" exception to the Fourth Amendment warrant requirement (decided *against* Rodriguez by the Texas court).
- State ground present: Article 39.14 discovery violation -- a Texas Code of Criminal Procedure provision wholly distinct from the Fourth Amendment, requiring timely disclosure of witness statements.
- Is the state ground independent? Yes -- Article 39.14 is a state procedural-discovery rule; the analysis does not depend on, or borrow from, Fourth Amendment doctrine.
- Is the state ground adequate? Yes -- the Texas court reversed *solely* on the Article 39.14 violation. The judgment (reversal) stands on that ground regardless of how the Fourth Amendment question is resolved.
- *Michigan v Long* presumption: not triggered. The opinion is not ambiguous; it expressly separates the federal Fourth Amendment holding (for the State) from the state discovery holding (for Rodriguez, and the basis of reversal).

**Counseling advice to Rodriguez (the would-be cert petitioner on the Fourth Amendment question):** Cert is futile on these facts. Rodriguez *lost* the Fourth Amendment question but *won* the case on the Article 39.14 ground. Even if SCOTUS granted cert and rejected the "suspected illegal immigrant" exception, the judgment of reversal would still stand on the independent and adequate state discovery ground -- any federal ruling would be advisory. SCOTUS should decline jurisdiction under IASG.

#### Case B: First Texas Church of God (Texas Supreme Court)

**Facts (hypothetical):** During a surging avian flu epidemic, the City of Austin issued an order on indoor gatherings limiting attendance to 50 persons; the rule applied equally to all types of assemblies -- secular and religious alike *(p. 2)*. The First Texas Church of God challenged the order, contending its scriptural interpretation (Book of Numbers 11:16) requires worship in gatherings of over 70 people, so the cap effectively prohibits its worship *(p. 2)*. The Texas Supreme Court rules for the church on two independent grounds:
- *Federal:* The First Amendment, as recently reaffirmed in *Roman Catholic Diocese of Brooklyn v. Cuomo*, demands that regulations impacting religious exercise be treated at least as favorably as comparable secular activity; the order disparately burdens the church, triggers strict scrutiny, and fails because the City has not demonstrated a compelling interest narrowly tailored to justify such a severe restriction *(pp. 2-3)*. A footnote rejects Austin's attempt to distinguish *Cuomo* as a crabbed reading of the First Amendment *(p. 2)*.
- *State:* Texas Constitution Article I § 6 declares that all men have a natural and indefeasible right to worship Almighty God according to the dictates of their own consciences, and that no human authority ought to control or interfere with the rights of conscience in matters of religion; the court reads § 6 to embody principles akin to the First Amendment's Free Exercise Clause and to demand at least equal treatment for religious exercise compared to secular activities *(p. 3)*. Texas Constitution Article I § 6a, adopted in response to pandemic-era restrictions, provides that the state or a political subdivision may not enact, adopt, or issue an order that prohibits or limits religious services by a religious organization -- a provision the court reads as directly forbidding the very type of blanket prohibition the City imposed *(p. 3)*.

Judgment reversed and remanded with instructions to dissolve the injunction *(p. 3)*. The cert prompt asks whether SCOTUS will hear the case to decide whether the Texas Supreme Court's expansive reading of *Roman Catholic Diocese v. Cuomo* was correct *(p. 3)*.

**IASG analysis:**
- Federal question present: whether the Texas Supreme Court correctly read *Cuomo* to require strict scrutiny of the Austin order.
- State ground present: Texas Constitution Art. I § 6 and § 6a -- two state constitutional provisions, with § 6a in particular operating as a categorical textual prohibition that does not depend on federal Free Exercise doctrine.
- Is the state ground independent? Yes -- § 6a is a Texas-specific text adopted in response to pandemic restrictions; § 6 has its own interpretive footing in the Texas Constitution even as the opinion notes it "embod[ies] principles akin to" the First Amendment.
- Is the state ground adequate? Yes -- the opinion concludes that "no matter how it is considered, the City's shutdown order cannot stand under the religious freedom guarantees of the Texas Constitution" *(p. 3)*. The state grounds alone support reversal.
- *Michigan v Long* presumption: arguably not triggered as to § 6a, which is plainly independent and adequate; closer as to § 6, given its avowed parallel to the First Amendment.

**Counseling advice to the City of Austin (the would-be cert petitioner):** Cert is unlikely to be worthwhile. Even if SCOTUS reversed on the federal question by narrowing *Cuomo*, the judgment in favor of the church would still stand on Article I § 6a, an independent and adequate state ground that flatly forbids the kind of blanket prohibition Austin enacted. The City's most plausible (though uphill) IASG argument is that § 6's analysis was interwoven with the federal framework -- but § 6a is a stand-alone textual bar that doesn't borrow from federal Free Exercise doctrine. If the state grounds are truly independent and adequate, a SCOTUS reversal on *Cuomo* would be advisory.

### IV. Practical Takeaways for Counseling

1. After an adverse state court ruling, always check: does the opinion cite both state and federal grounds?
2. If mixed: apply *Michigan v Long* -- is the federal law interwoven, or is the state ground clearly stated and self-sufficient?
3. Advising a cert petitioner: cert is available (under *Long*'s presumption) unless the state ground is unambiguously independent and adequate.
4. Advising a cert respondent (defending the judgment): argue the state ground is clearly independent and adequate; urge SCOTUS to decline jurisdiction.
5. The state court can always insulate its ruling from SCOTUS review by clearly expressing that its decision rests on independent state grounds -- this is a tool that state courts can use intentionally to develop independent state constitutional law.

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

The exercise is built around the two fictional Texas cases analyzed above (Ex Parte Rodriguez and First Texas Church of God). Additional discussion questions likely include:

- Rodriguez illustrates a *split* opinion -- federal claim lost, state claim won, and reversal driven by the state ground. Does that posture make IASG analysis easier or harder than a single-ground opinion?
- First Texas Church of God pairs a federal holding under *Cuomo* with two distinct state provisions (Art. I § 6 and § 6a). When a state court relies on multiple state grounds with differing independence from federal doctrine, must each be independent and adequate, or does one clean state ground suffice?
- Can a state court use IASG doctrine strategically -- citing state law grounds to avoid SCOTUS review of a ruling the state court knows conflicts with federal precedent?
- After *Michigan v Long*, what should a state court lawyer advise a client who wins at the state level and wants to prevent federal review?

## Key Takeaways

- IASG doctrine is a jurisdictional limit on SCOTUS appellate power: if a state court judgment rests on independent and adequate state law grounds, there is no federal question to review.
- *Michigan v Long* (1983): when state court opinions are ambiguous, SCOTUS presumes federal grounds and takes jurisdiction. The state court must clearly and expressly state independent state grounds to avoid federal review.
- Counseling function: after an adverse state ruling, analyze whether the opinion rests on (1) purely federal grounds (cert possible), (2) independent and adequate state grounds (cert futile), or (3) ambiguous mix (cert possible under *Long*'s presumption).
- The doctrine has strategic implications: state courts can develop independent state constitutional law and insulate it from SCOTUS review by expressly disclaiming federal reliance.
- Exam tip: IASG is a prerequisite question before reaching the merits of any federal constitutional claim that a state court has decided.
