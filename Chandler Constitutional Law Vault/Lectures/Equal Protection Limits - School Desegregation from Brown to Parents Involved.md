# Equal Protection Limits - School Desegregation from Brown to Parents Involved

<!-- 2026-05-26: convention rollout (slide anchors + Professor Emphasis split) -->

## Professor Emphasis

### From source

The deck presents the school desegregation arc explicitly as expansion (Brown → Alexander, 1954-1968), high water (Swann, Keyes, 1969-1973), the turn (Milliken, 1974), and retreat (Parents Involved, 2007), with vote counts as the structural story: Brown (9-0) → Cooper (9-0) → Griffin (9-0) → Green (9-0) → Alexander (per curiam) → Swann (9-0) → Keyes (7-1) → Milliken (5-4) → Parents Involved (5-4) *(slide 32)*. The deck names three unresolved tensions: rights vs. remedies; intent vs. effect (the Washington v. Davis intent requirement extends Keyes's purpose-not-effect rule and makes modern segregation very hard to challenge); and colorblindness vs. race-consciousness (Roberts: "the way to stop discrimination on the basis of race is to stop discriminating on the basis of race"; Breyer: "ignoring race in a society structured by racial hierarchy perpetuates inequality") *(slides 29, 33)*. The deck closes with the "big question": "Can the Equal Protection Clause -- as currently interpreted -- meaningfully address school segregation?" *(slide 35)*. Washington v. Davis is treated as the decisive doctrinal turn that completed the Court's retreat from the Warren Court's expansive equal protection vision *(slides 23-27)*. Each case's "Why Law Was Unsettled" box frames the open constitutional question the deck is teaching the case as resolving.

### Editor's framing

This page tracks the deck's expansion/high-water/turn/retreat chronology faithfully and synthesizes the three tensions and the vote-count arc that the deck makes explicit on Slides 32-33. The framing of the doctrinal "tools removed one by one" closely follows the deck's Slide 30 "Closing of the Circle" enumeration. The cross-reference to SFFA v. Harvard extends the deck's Slide 30 SFFA mention into the broader course arc.

## Lecture Outline

### I. Before Brown: The Legal Landscape
- Plessy v. Ferguson (1896) established "separate but equal" as constitutional doctrine; by 1954, seventeen states plus D.C. mandated segregated schools by law; four more permitted it *(slide 2)*
- Separate was never equal: Black schools received a fraction of per-pupil spending; dilapidated buildings; outdated or absent textbooks; no transportation *(slide 2)*
- The NAACP's strategy under Charles Hamilton Houston and Thurgood Marshall targeted the gap; earlier victories in Sweatt and McLaurin chipped at graduate schools but left K-12 untouched *(slide 2)*
- Brown consolidated cases from Kansas, South Carolina, Virginia, Delaware, and D.C.; Linda Brown walked past a white school to reach her segregated school *(slide 2)*

### II. Brown v. Board of Education (1954) — 9-0 — Warren, C.J.
- Holding: in the field of public education the doctrine of "separate but equal" has no place; separate educational facilities are inherently unequal; Plessy overruled as applied to public education *(slide 3)*
- Psychological harm: to separate children of similar age and qualifications solely because of their race generates a feeling of inferiority as to their status in the community that may affect their hearts and minds in a way unlikely ever to be undone *(slide 3)*
- Warren's strategy: he delayed reargument to build unanimity and personally lobbied resistant justices; the deck reads the 9-0 result as engineered, not inevitable *(slide 3)*
- Doctrinal shift: racial classification in education triggers heightened scrutiny, but the remedy and timeline were left for Brown II *(slide 3)*

### III. Brown II and "All Deliberate Speed" (1955)
- Implementation placed in the hands of local school authorities under federal district court supervision -- desegregate "with all deliberate speed" -- a compromise the deck reads as a license for indefinite delay *(slide 4)*
- A decade later, fewer than 2% of Black children in the South attended integrated schools; in five Deep South states the figure was essentially zero *(slide 4)*
- Massive Resistance as a coordinated legal-political program:
  - Southern Manifesto (1956): nineteen senators and eighty-two representatives pledged "all lawful means" to reverse Brown *(slide 4)*
  - Interposition: states claimed authority to interpose between federal courts and citizens; Virginia adopted a formal resolution; courts uniformly rejected the doctrine *(slide 4)*
  - The toolkit: pupil placement laws, school closure statutes, tuition grants for private academies, defunding of integrated schools *(slide 4)*

### IV. Little Rock (1957) and Cooper v. Aaron (1958)
- The Little Rock Nine; Governor Orval Faubus deployed the Arkansas National Guard to physically block entry; Elizabeth Eckford, arriving alone, was surrounded by a screaming mob -- an image broadcast worldwide *(slide 5)*
- Eisenhower federalized the National Guard and deployed 1,000 soldiers of the 101st Airborne Division -- the first time since Reconstruction that a president sent federal troops South to protect Black citizens' constitutional rights *(slide 5)*
- The Cold War dimension: the Soviet Union used images of the crisis in propaganda and the State Department reported damage to American prestige abroad *(slide 5)*
- Faubus closed all Little Rock public high schools for the 1958-59 school year -- "the Lost Year" -- rather than permit further integration *(slide 5)*
- Cooper v. Aaron (1958), 9-0, signed individually by all nine justices *(slide 6)*:
  - States are bound by Supreme Court interpretations of the Constitution; no state legislator or executive or judicial officer can "war against the Constitution" without violating the oath of office; public disorder engineered by state officials cannot justify postponing constitutional rights *(slide 6)*
  - Reaffirms Marbury: the federal judiciary is "supreme in the exposition of the law of the Constitution" *(slide 6)*
  - Cooper killed interposition as a legal theory; resistance shifted to subtler tactics -- school closures, pupil placement acts, tuition grants for private academies -- setting the stage for Griffin and Green *(slide 6)*

### V. Griffin v. Prince Edward County (1964) — 9-0 — Black, J.
- Prince Edward County, Virginia closed its entire public school system in 1959 rather than integrate; closures lasted five years (1959-1964); white children attended private "academies" funded by state tuition grants and county tax credits; Black children largely denied formal education *(slide 7)*
- Virginia's statewide campaign produced school closures in Norfolk, Charlottesville, and Warren County (1958-59), alongside Little Rock's Lost Year *(slide 7)*
- Holding: closing public schools while funding private segregated alternatives denies equal protection; the record "could not be clearer" that closure occurred "for one reason, and one reason only" -- to ensure that white and colored children would not go to the same school *(slide 8)*
- Extraordinary remedy: the time for mere "deliberate speed" has run out, and federal courts may order local officials to levy taxes to reopen and operate a public school system -- an extraordinary intrusion into local fiscal authority *(slide 8)*
- Doctrinal significance: Griffin closed the exit strategy; a state cannot escape Brown by abandoning public education; the case hinted at an affirmative obligation to equalize educational opportunity, not merely eliminate segregation *(slide 8)*

### VI. Green v. County School Board of New Kent County (1968) — 9-0 — Brennan, J.
- New Kent County, Virginia -- 4,500 residents, roughly equal Black and white populations, no residential segregation; two schools (New Kent on the east side white; Watkins on the west side Black); twenty-one buses deliberately crisscrossed the county to maintain racial separation *(slide 9)*
- After 14 years of noncompliance, the board adopted a "freedom of choice" plan in 1965; three years later, not a single white student chose Watkins and 85% of Black students still attended the all-Black school *(slide 9)*
- Holding: school boards operating former dual systems bear an affirmative duty to "take whatever steps might be necessary to convert to a unitary system in which racial discrimination would be eliminated root and branch"; freedom of choice is merely a means -- if it fails, other means must be used *(slide 10)*
- Green factors: student composition, faculty integration, staff assignment, transportation, extracurricular activities and facilities *(slide 10)*
- The burden: school boards must come forward with a plan that "promises realistically to work, and promises realistically to work now" *(slide 10)*
- Doctrinal shift: Green begins to transform Brown from a negative right (freedom from segregation) into an affirmative duty (obligation to integrate); Southern school integration surged from 32% (1968) to 79% (1971), the highest point in American history -- with the deck's parenthetical that this will not persist *(slide 10)*

### VII. Alexander v. Holmes County (1969) — Per Curiam — Unanimous
- Fifteen years after Brown, thirty-three Mississippi school districts still operated dual systems; the Nixon Justice Department requested a delay arguing "administrative confusion"; the Fifth Circuit agreed; Black parents appealed *(slide 11)*
- Holding: "continued operation of segregated schools under a standard of allowing 'all deliberate speed' for desegregation is no longer constitutionally permissible"; districts must "begin immediately to operate as unitary school systems" *(slide 11)*
- "The obligation of every school district is to terminate dual school systems at once and to operate now and hereafter only unitary schools" *(slide 11)*
- Aftermath: Alexander finally killed "all deliberate speed"; combined with Green's affirmative duty and Title VI's funding leverage, it drove the most rapid period of school integration in American history (1969-1972) *(slide 11)*

### VIII. Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg (1971) — 9-0 — Burger, C.J.
- 84,000 students, 29% Black (24,000); despite a decade of nominal compliance, 14,000 Black students remained concentrated in twenty-one schools that were 99%+ Black; "neighborhood school" assignments tracked decades of segregated housing *(slide 12)*
- District Judge James McMillan appointed Dr. John Finger, whose plan included elementary students bused from city to suburbs, older students bused from suburbs to city, mathematical ratios, and noncontiguous attendance zones; the Fourth Circuit approved part but rejected the elementary plan as too burdensome *(slide 12)*
- Holding: district courts have broad equitable authority to remedy de jure segregation; permissible tools include mathematical ratios as starting points, noncontiguous attendance zones, pairing and grouping of schools, and busing -- unless the time or distance of travel is so great as to risk the health of children or significantly impinge on the educational process *(slide 13)*
- Key limitation: applies only to districts with a history of de jure segregation; where no such history existed, "it might well be desirable to assign pupils to schools nearest their homes" *(slide 13)*
- Swann was the last unanimous desegregation decision and the high-water mark of federal remedial authority; it triggered fierce political backlash including the Boston busing crisis (1974-76); white flight to suburbs accelerated, setting the stage for Milliken *(slide 13)*

### IX. After Swann: Backlash and White Flight
- Boston (1974-76): court-ordered busing between Roxbury and South Boston sparked violence; buses were stoned, students attacked *(slide 14)*
- Nixon proposed a moratorium on new busing orders in 1972; Congress passed the Equal Educational Opportunities Act (1974) discouraging busing as a remedy *(slide 14)*
- White flight paradox: desegregation within city districts often accelerated resegregation across district lines; by 1987, Detroit public schools were approximately 90% Black while surrounding suburbs remained overwhelmingly white; today the white population in Detroit schools is 2.5% *(slide 14)*
- Housing → Schools → Housing feedback loop: decades of racial zoning, restrictive covenants, FHA redlining, and exclusionary land-use regulations sorted populations; school assignment followed neighborhood lines; families who could afford to moved, deepening residential segregation *(slide 14)*

### X. Keyes v. School District No. 1 (1973) — 7-1 — Brennan, J.
- Denver had never operated a legally mandated dual school system, but the Park Hill neighborhood was heavily segregated and school board actions had intentionally maintained that segregation -- gerrymandered attendance zones, strategic school construction in segregated areas, excessive use of mobile classrooms to avoid transfers *(slide 15)*
- The De Jure/De Facto Puzzle: prior cases all involved Southern states with explicit segregation statutes (de jure); Denver's segregation arose from government actions without a formal statute (what defenders called de facto) *(slide 15)*
- The deck flags this as the first desegregation case from outside the South: if segregation in Northern and Western cities was constitutionally remediable, the scope of Brown expanded from a regional correction to a national transformation *(slide 15)*
- Holding: intentionally segregative school board actions in a "meaningful portion" of a system create a presumption that segregation elsewhere in the district is also intentional; the burden shifts to school authorities to prove their actions as to other segregated schools were not likewise motivated by a segregative intent *(slide 16)*
- The differentiating factor between de jure and de facto segregation is "purpose or intent to segregate" *(slide 16)*
- Keyes collapsed the de jure/de facto distinction in practice; the intent requirement (rather than discriminatory effect) would prove a double-edged sword -- the deck flags forward links to *Washington v. Davis* (1976) and *Arlington Heights* (1977) *(slide 16)*
- The deck calls Keyes one of the last "Warren-court-flavored" decisions *(slide 16)*

### XI. Washington v. Davis (1976) — The Intent Requirement Generalized
- Two Black applicants to the D.C. Metropolitan Police Department were rejected after failing Test 21, a federal civil-service exam measuring verbal ability, vocabulary, and reading comprehension (minimum 40 of 80) *(slide 24)*
- Black applicants failed at 4x the white rate; 44% of new recruits since 1969 were Black -- roughly proportional to the area's Black population *(slide 24)*
- Respondents sought to import the *Griggs v. Duke Power* (1971) Title VII disparate-impact standard into constitutional law *(slide 24)*
- Holding: a law is not unconstitutional solely because it has a racially disproportionate impact; the Equal Protection Clause prohibits only intentional discrimination *(slide 25)*
- Disproportionate impact is admissible as evidence from which intent may be inferred; total or seriously disproportionate exclusion may for all practical purposes demonstrate unconstitutionality where the discrimination is very difficult to explain on nonracial grounds *(slide 25)*
- Title VII vs. constitutional standard: under Griggs, once a facially neutral practice has disparate impact the burden shifts to the employer to prove job-relatedness -- a statutory remedy Congress chose to go further than the Constitution requires *(slide 25)*
- Legacy: the intent requirement under the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments remains settled, binding law -- never overruled, never seriously questioned; *Personnel Administrator v. Feeney* (1979) extended it to sex discrimination *(slide 26)*
- Doctrinal turn: the Warren Court (1953-69) expanded constitutional protections aggressively; *Washington v. Davis* marks a decisive turn; the Burger Court drew a firm line -- the Constitution addresses purpose, not effects *(slide 26)*
- What if the dissent had won: the Court warned that a disparate-impact constitutional rule would "raise serious questions about, and perhaps invalidate, a whole range of tax, welfare, public service, regulatory, and licensing statutes"; in an age of data, virtually any facially neutral government policy can be shown to fall more heavily on some group *(slide 27)*

### XII. The Housing-School Segregation Nexus
- *Buchanan v. Warley* (1917) struck down explicit racial zoning, but exclusionary zoning (lot sizes, single-family requirements) achieved the same result indirectly *(slide 17)*
- *Shelley v. Kraemer* (1948) barred judicial enforcement of racially restrictive covenants, but the covenants' residential sorting persisted for decades *(slide 17)*
- FHA redlining: the Federal Housing Administration systematically refused to insure mortgages in Black or mixed neighborhoods; HOLC "residential security maps" became templates for private lending discrimination *(slide 17)*
- Feedback loop: housing segregation → school segregation → further housing segregation; school quality tracked racial composition; families chose neighborhoods based on school assignments; real estate agents steered buyers by race *(slide 17)*
- Legal consequence: after *Milliken*, courts could not reach across district boundaries unless plaintiffs proved the suburban district itself had engaged in intentional segregation -- a near-impossible burden when segregation was caused by federal housing policy *(slide 17)*

### XIII. Milliken v. Bradley (1974) — 5-4 — Burger, C.J.
- By the early 1970s, Detroit's public schools were approximately 64% Black and climbing; surrounding suburban districts were overwhelmingly white; decades of FHA redlining, discriminatory lending, and racially restrictive covenants had created a metropolitan area starkly divided by race along school district lines; a Detroit-only remedy would produce nominally integrated schools in a district that was already majority-Black -- integration in name only *(slide 18)*
- The district court ordered a metropolitan remedy encompassing fifty-three suburban school districts; the Sixth Circuit affirmed *(slide 18)*
- Holding: the scope of the remedy must match the scope of the violation; before interdistrict relief, it must first be shown that there has been a constitutional violation within one district that produces a significant segregative effect in another district; because the suburban districts had not been shown to have caused Detroit's segregation, they could not be conscripted into the remedy *(slide 19)*
- Dissents *(slide 19)*:
  - Marshall: unless our children begin to learn together, there is little hope that our people will ever learn to live together; the majority allows the State to benefit from its own wrongdoing by confining the remedy to a district the State had made demographically impossible to integrate
  - White: Michigan bore responsibility through legislative boundary-drawing and oversight
- The first Supreme Court decision to restrict desegregation remedies; the 5-4 split signaled the end of the Court's consensus on integration *(slide 19)*
- Practical effect: Detroit Public Schools by 1987 were approximately 90% Black with enrollment plummeting -- precisely the dynamic Marshall's dissent predicted *(slide 20)*
- *Milliken II* (1977) allowed district courts to direct city and state to pay extra to remedy consequences of intentional segregation *(slide 20)*
- Structural legacy: district lines became the architecture of segregation; moving to a different school district became the legal escape valve from integration *(slide 20)*

### XIV. Parents Involved in Community Schools v. Seattle (2007) — 5-4 — Roberts, C.J.
- Seattle had never operated a de jure segregated system and had never been under a desegregation decree, but by 2000 some schools were becoming racially isolated due to housing patterns; the district voluntarily used race as a "tiebreaker" when schools were oversubscribed *(slide 28)*
- Jefferson County, Kentucky had a history of explicit segregation; after dissolving its desegregation decree in 2000 (having achieved unitary status), it voluntarily adopted a race-conscious plan to maintain the integration it had achieved; PICS sued *(slide 28)*
- Holding: voluntary race-conscious assignment plans in both Seattle and Louisville were unconstitutional; the plans used individual racial classifications not narrowly tailored to a compelling interest; "racial balancing" for its own sake is patently unconstitutional *(slide 29)*
- The fractured majority *(slide 29)*:
  - Roberts plurality (four): colorblind Constitution; racial classification is almost never permissible -- "the way to stop discrimination on the basis of race is to stop discriminating on the basis of race"
  - Kennedy concurrence (one): agreed plans were unconstitutional but endorsed race-conscious measures that do not classify individual students -- e.g., strategic school siting, drawing attendance zones with demographics in mind. **Kennedy's concurrence, not Roberts' plurality, is the controlling opinion** *(slide 29)*
- Breyer's dissent: "It is not often in the law that so few have so quickly changed so much" -- the plurality abandoned Brown's promise of integrated education *(slide 29)*
- After *SFFA v. Harvard* (2023): the Court's invalidation of race-conscious college admissions further narrowed the doctrinal space for using race to promote integration at any level of education *(slide 30)*

### XV. The Doctrinal Arc: Expansion and Retreat
- 1954-1968 (Expansion): Brown (prohibition) → Cooper (supremacy) → Griffin (no exit) → Green (affirmative duty) → Alexander (now) *(slide 32)*
- 1969-1973 (High Water): Swann (broad remedies) → Keyes (intent + Northern reach); all decisions unanimous or near-unanimous *(slide 32)*
- 1974 (The Turn): Milliken (5-4); first restriction on remedies; district boundaries become walls; white flight gets legal protection *(slide 32)*
- 2007 (Retreat): Parents Involved (5-4); voluntary integration struck down; colorblindness ascendant *(slide 32)*
- Vote counts tell the story: Brown (9-0) → Cooper (9-0) → Griffin (9-0) → Green (9-0) → Alexander (per curiam) → Swann (9-0) → Keyes (7-1) → Milliken (5-4) → Parents Involved (5-4) *(slide 32)*

### XVI. After Parents Involved: The Kennedy Window
- The deck reads the tools as removed one by one: Green's affirmative duty + Parents Involved's prohibition on voluntary racial classification + Milliken's bar on interdistrict remedies + Keyes's intent requirement *(slide 30)*
- Kennedy's window -- districts can still site new schools strategically, draw attendance zones with demographics in mind, use targeted recruitment, and track racial data for monitoring, but they cannot use individual students' race in assignment decisions *(slide 30)*

### XVII. Three Unresolved Tensions
1. **Rights vs. Remedies**: Brown established the right to attend a non-segregated school, but the remedial framework has narrowed steadily -- "all deliberate speed" permitted delay, Milliken confined remedies to single districts, Parents Involved prohibited voluntary classification; a right without an effective remedy is aspirational at best *(slide 33)*
2. **Intent vs. Effect**: Keyes required proof of segregative intent; *Washington v. Davis* (1976) generalized this -- facially neutral policies with disparate racial impact violate equal protection only if discriminatory purpose is shown; the intent requirement makes it extremely difficult to challenge modern segregation where actions occurred decades ago and current officials can plausibly disclaim discriminatory purpose *(slide 33)*
3. **Colorblindness vs. Race-Consciousness**: Roberts: stop discriminating by race to end racial discrimination; Breyer: ignoring race in a society structured by racial hierarchy perpetuates inequality -- the fundamental divide in modern equal protection doctrine that extends far beyond education *(slide 33)*

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

**Was Milliken Rightly Decided?**: If the state of Michigan created the conditions of segregation through housing policy, redlining oversight, and boundary-drawing, should the remedy be confined by district lines the state drew? Marshall argues the majority allows the state to benefit from its own wrongdoing. Burger responds that the suburban districts themselves did no wrong. Who is right?

**The Colorblindness Debate**: Roberts says "the way to stop discrimination on the basis of race is to stop discriminating on the basis of race." Breyer responds that colorblindness in a racially stratified society perpetuates existing inequalities. Which constitutional theory is more faithful to the original purpose of the Fourteenth Amendment? Does the original purpose even resolve the contemporary question?

**The Intent Trap**: Washington v. Davis requires proof of discriminatory intent. Modern school segregation is caused primarily by residential patterns produced by government policies enacted decades ago. Current school officials can truthfully say they have no discriminatory purpose. Is the intent requirement adequate to address this structural problem? What would a constitutional rule based on discriminatory effect look like, and what would its costs be?

**Vouchers and Massive Resistance**: Do modern voucher programs resemble massive resistance? Arguments for: public subsidies for private schooling echo the tuition-grant arrangements of the 1950s-60s; some charter sectors are more racially concentrated. Arguments against: explicit segregation is unconstitutional; contemporary advocates frame vouchers as expanding opportunity for underserved students; Arlington Heights requires proof of discriminatory intent for facially neutral policies.

**The Racial Dot Map**: The residential dot map shows neighborhoods sorting into near-monochromatic blocks separated by sharp boundaries — not as a result of private choice alone, but through a century of government policy. After Milliken, those lines are constitutionally significant. After Parents Involved, racial considerations in boundary-drawing are suspect. Does the combination create structural incentives for fragmentation along racial and economic lines?

## Key Takeaways

1. The vote counts tell the doctrinal story: unanimity when establishing rights (Brown through Swann), 5-4 division when demanding structural remedies (Milliken, Parents Involved).
2. The doctrinal tools to address school segregation have been progressively dismantled: "all deliberate speed" → Green's affirmative duty → Swann's broad remedial power → Milliken's district-boundary limit → Washington v. Davis's intent requirement → Parents Involved's prohibition on voluntary racial classification.
3. Milliken's district-boundary rule is the defining structural constraint: because residential segregation was caused by federal housing policy, and school assignment follows residential patterns, courts cannot address the root cause without interdistrict proof.
4. Washington v. Davis (intent requirement) and Keyes (intent needed to trigger Green's affirmative duty) together make it very difficult to challenge modern segregation where current officials can truthfully disclaim discriminatory purpose.
5. The Roberts/Breyer debate in Parents Involved — colorblindness vs. race-consciousness — is the fundamental divide in modern equal protection doctrine, extended to higher education by SFFA v. Harvard.
6. The Kennedy window (race-aware but not race-classifying measures) is the only remaining tool for school districts seeking to maintain integration without a court order.
