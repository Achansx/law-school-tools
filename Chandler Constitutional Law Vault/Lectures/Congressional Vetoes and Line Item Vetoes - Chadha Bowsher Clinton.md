## Lecture Outline

**I. INS v. Chadha (1983) — The Legislative Veto**

The deck poses the threshold questions: who decides deportation in the first instance, is this legislative/executive/judicial, and what did INA §244(c)(2) do? *(slide 2)*. One house of Congress could veto the AG's suspension of deportation; the INA was not unusual because Congress had used legislative vetoes in approximately 200 statutes over 50 years; Chadha himself was before the Court because Kenya would not accept him and his parents were British subjects *(slide 2)*.

**The Public Rights and Plenary Power Background:** The deck addresses why an Article III court is deciding an immigration case at all, citing Murray v. Hoboken Land for the public rights doctrine (matters Congress may or may not bring within judicial cognizance) and Carlson v. Landon (1952) for the plenary-power proposition that the power to expel aliens, being essentially a power of the political branches, may be exercised entirely through executive officers with such opportunity for judicial review as Congress may see fit to authorize *(slide 3)*.

**The Holding:** Chief Justice Burger's majority struck down Section 244(c)(2). The analysis rested on two grounds *(slide 4)*:

1. *Essentially Legislative:* The one-house veto altered the legal rights, duties, and relations of persons outside the legislative branch -- that is legislation *(slide 4)*.

2. *No Presentment; Not Bicameral:* The President was denied the opportunity to sign or veto the resolution -- violating Article I, §7 -- and only one house acted, violating bicameralism; even a two-house veto without presentment would fail *(slide 4)*.

Burger's key passage: "The fact that a given law or procedure is efficient, convenient, and useful in facilitating functions of government, standing alone, will not save it if it is contrary to the Constitution. Convenience and efficiency are not the primary objectives -- or the hallmarks -- of democratic government" *(slide 4)*.

**The Formalist Themes:** The procedures prescribed by the Constitution for legislative action are not empty formalities; they were "finely wrought and exhaustively considered" *(slide 5)*. When the Constitution wanted exceptions to bicameralism and presentment, it enumerated them; the structure is not hermetically sealed, but close; shortcuts are actually bad *(slide 5)*. The deck flags the opinion as "very much of the formalist school: when it comes to the structural provisions of the Constitution, there is little room for flexibility or creativity" *(slide 5)*.

**The McCulloch Acquiescence Problem:** Congress had used legislative vetoes in approximately 200 statutes for 50 years -- under McCulloch, longstanding practice can illuminate constitutional meaning, and Burger ignores this entirely while White's dissent hammers it *(slide 5)*.

**Powell Concurrence — The Narrower Path:** Powell would have decided narrowly -- Congress was essentially adjudicating Chadha's individual case, a judicial function -- and no need to reach the broader bicameralism question, previewing the "avoid constitutional questions" canon *(slide 6)*.

**White Dissent — The Functionalist Response:** The majority puts Congress in a hard spot: either refuse to delegate (does Congress want to decide thousands of immigration cases?) or abdicate entirely to the Executive and independent agencies *(slide 7)*. The legislative veto is a bargain rather than a power grab: you give me power to adjudicate, and I relieve you of the burden -- but I retain a check against misbehavior *(slide 7)*. The deck flags the McCulloch acquiescence question -- how can it be unconstitutional after 50 years and 200 laws? *(slide 7)*. White's clearest statement: if Congress may delegate lawmaking power to independent and Executive agencies, it is most difficult to understand Article I as prohibiting Congress from also reserving a check on legislative power for itself, since agencies may issue regulations with the force of law without bicameral approval or presidential signature *(slide 8)*.

**Aftermath:** Congress had to replace one-house and concurrent-resolution vetoes across the U.S. Code *(slide 9)*. The "informal veto" survives: Congress exercises de facto vetoes by telling agencies to do this right or their budget gets cut, so practical power survives formal doctrine *(slide 9)*. IEEPA and the Impoundment Control Act now use joint resolutions of disapproval -- bicameral plus presented to the President -- which satisfies Chadha *(slide 9)*.

**II. Bowsher v. Synar (1986) — The Comptroller General**

Congress created the office of Comptroller General in 1921, housed in the GAO as "an instrumentality of the United States Government independent of the executive departments" (31 U.S.C. § 702) *(slide 10)*. The 1982 statute had the President select the CG from three individuals recommended by the Speaker and President pro tempore, appointed with advice and consent of the Senate *(slides 10-11)*. The CG may be removed by impeachment or by joint resolution of Congress for permanent disability, inefficiency, neglect of duty, malfeasance, or a felony or moral turpitude *(slide 12)*.

**Gramm-Rudman-Hollings:** The deck's three-step diagram: (1) OMB (executive) and CBO (legislative) feed the CG estimates of the budget deficit; (2) the CG reconciles estimates and makes a "recommendation" to the President about how to cut, with certain programs subject to cuts and others not; (3) if Congress doesn't override, the President sequesters the money *(slide 13)*. Liberal Republican Mike Synar thought Congress was giving away appropriations power and giving legislative officers executive functions, so he sued *(slide 13)*. The deck's core rule: Congress's job is to make law -- it cannot pass a statute and then retain control over how it is implemented (cf. Chadha) *(slide 13)*.

**The Humphrey's Executor Distinction:**
- *Humphrey's Executor* (1935): the President cannot freely remove quasi-legislative officers; limits on executive removal power are OK *(slide 12)*.
- *Bowsher* (1986): Congress retains removal power over an officer performing executive functions, which amounts to a congressional leash on the executor of laws and is not OK *(slide 12)*.
- The deck's punchline: one is a limit on the President; the other is Congress keeping a leash on an executor of the laws *(slide 12)*.

**III. The Line Item Veto — Raines and Clinton**

*Raines v. Byrd* (1997): Senator Robert Byrd (51 years in Congress) sued to prevent implementation; the Court held no standing because the injury "runs with the seat" -- it is institutional, abstract, and widely dispersed; legislators simply lost a vote *(slide 14)*.

*Clinton v. City of New York* (1998): NYC had an elaborate scheme for extra federal Medicaid money; Clinton canceled it; NYC sued with a concrete, particularized injury (multibillion-dollar contingent liability), standing was found, and NYC won *(slide 14)*.

**The Holding:** The majority rests on the text of Article I, §7 -- the Presentment Clause; the President must sign or veto a bill in its entirety; if the President is to have a different role in determining the final text of law, "such change must come not by legislation but through the amendment procedures set forth in Article V" -- and the formalist arc from Chadha is complete *(slide 14)*.

**IV. The Formalist Arc**

The deck draws the arc explicitly *(slide 16)*:
- *Chadha* (1983): Congress cannot bypass Article I when acting legislatively
- *Bowsher* (1986): Congress cannot retain control over officers executing the law
- *Clinton* (1998): The President cannot unilaterally rewrite enacted law

**V. The Fiscal Context**

About $1 trillion per year is used to service the national debt at current interest rates, consuming roughly 20% of federal revenues -- i.e. things whose benefits are often mostly in the past *(slide 15)*. This page reads the fiscal magnitude as explaining why Congress kept inventing mechanisms (Gramm-Rudman, line item veto) to force deficit reduction without requiring politically difficult votes -- a narrative gloss that goes beyond the slide's bare statistics.

## Cases Discussed

### [[INS v. Chadha]] (1983)
Legislative veto struck down. Section 244(c)(2)'s one-house veto of the AG's deportation suspension was "essentially legislative" (altering legal rights of persons outside the legislative branch), violated presentment, and violated bicameralism. Burger's formalist opinion: constitutional procedures are finely wrought, not optional. White's functionalist dissent: the legislative veto is a necessary check on delegated quasi-legislative power.

### [[Cases/Bowsher v Synar (1986)]] (1986)
Gramm-Rudman-Hollings provision assigning executive budget-reduction functions to the Comptroller General struck down. Congress cannot retain removal power over an officer performing executive functions. The Humphrey's Executor distinction: limiting the President's removal power is different from Congress keeping a leash on an executor of the laws.

### [[Raines v. Byrd]] (1997)
Congressional plaintiffs challenging the Line Item Veto Act lacked standing. Their injury—loss of a legislative vote—was institutional, abstract, and dispersed. Injury "runs with the seat," not with the individual legislators.

### [[Clinton v. City of New York]] (1998)
Line Item Veto Act struck down. President's selective cancellation of enacted statutory provisions violated Article I, Section 7's Presentment Clause. The President must sign or veto in full; partial disapproval requires a constitutional amendment. NYC's concrete multibillion-dollar injury sufficed for standing.

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

**White's Functionalist Challenge:** White's dissent presents the delegation dilemma -- either Congress refuses to delegate (and micromanages thousands of immigration cases) or it abdicates entirely to the Executive and independent agencies *(slide 7)*. The deck's clearest statement is that if Congress may delegate lawmaking power to independent and Executive agencies, it is most difficult to understand Article I as prohibiting Congress from reserving a check on legislative power for itself *(slide 8)*. This page asks whether the informal veto adequately substitutes for the formal one.

**The Informal Veto Aftermath:** The deck flags that Congress exercises de facto vetoes by telling agencies their budget will be cut if they do not comply, so practical power survives formal doctrine *(slide 9)*. This page asks whether this vindicates White's functionalism or shows the formal rule has practical bite.

**Accounting for the Comptroller General:** After Bowsher, Congress restructured Gramm-Rudman-Hollings to route the sequestration function through OMB -- editor's note: a workaround not directly addressed by the deck.

**Article V or Nothing:** Stevens in Clinton holds that any change to the President's role in determining the final text of law requires an Article V amendment *(slide 14)*. This page asks whether the Article V amendment process should be easier given how infrequently it succeeds.

## Key Takeaways

1. **The formalist arc runs through Chadha, Bowsher, and Clinton.** Together they establish that Congress must follow the constitutional procedures when acting legislatively; cannot retain operational control over officers who execute the law; and cannot grant the President a selective veto over enacted statutes. These are structural constraints, not optional procedures.

2. **Convenience and efficiency do not save constitutional violations.** Burger's Chadha opinion explicitly rejects efficiency as a constitutional justification. If a procedure is contrary to the Constitution, its utility is irrelevant. This is the formalist commitment.

3. **The Humphrey's Executor/Bowsher distinction turns on who is constrained.** A restriction on presidential removal of an officer performing quasi-legislative or quasi-judicial functions limits the President but does not give Congress control over the executor of the laws. Congressional retention of removal power over an executive officer does. The identity of the constrained party matters.

4. **Standing doctrine shaped the line item veto litigation.** Raines shows that legislators cannot use courts to contest institutional injuries from lost votes. Clinton shows that private parties with concrete injuries can bring the same challenge. The Presentment Clause concern was the same in both cases; standing doctrine determined which plaintiff could litigate it.

5. **Informal vetoes survive formal doctrine.** After Chadha, Congress exercised de facto vetoes through budget control, oversight, and appropriations conditions. The doctrine prevented the formal mechanism; it did not prevent the practical outcome. This is a recurring pattern in constitutional law—formal rules shape the form of power exercises without eliminating the underlying power.

6. **The joint resolution of disapproval is the constitutional substitute.** IEEPA, the Impoundment Control Act, and other modern statutes use joint resolutions of disapproval (bicameral, presented to the President) rather than concurrent resolutions. This satisfies Chadha while preserving congressional control.
