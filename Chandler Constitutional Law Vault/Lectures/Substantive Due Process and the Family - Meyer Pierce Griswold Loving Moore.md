## Professor Emphasis

### From source

The deck's roadmap traces the Court's evolving answer to "what does 'liberty' mean?" -- from parental control over education, to bodily autonomy, to marital privacy -- and stresses how historical crises shaped the doctrine *(slide 2)*. Meyer is presented as the first Supreme Court decision to define "liberty" under the Fourteenth Amendment expansively, beyond mere freedom from bodily restraint, to include family autonomy *(slide 5)*. Buck v. Bell is foregrounded as a doctrinal failure: the deck explicitly contrasts what Holmes did with what Lochner-era analysis would have demanded -- no identification of the right at stake, no balancing test, no scrutiny of the Jacobson analogy *(slide 18)*. The lecture also stresses Loving's dual EP/DP holding as structurally protective post-Dobbs: even if substantive due process were narrowed, the equal protection holding stands independently *(slide 32)*. The Glucksberg paradox is flagged on its own slide: anti-miscegenation laws were the history and tradition at the time of Loving, so a literal Glucksberg test would have condemned Loving as wrong -- a strong argument that history and tradition cannot be the sole measure of fundamental rights *(slide 34)*. Brennan's racial critique in Moore -- citing the 13%-vs-3% disparity in extended-family living -- is highlighted as evidence that facially neutral ordinances encode racial norms about family structure *(slides 36, 38)*.

## Lecture Outline

**I. Setting the Stage: What Does "Liberty" Mean?**
The Due Process Clause protects life, liberty, and property. But "liberty" is undefined. The cases in this lecture collectively build a definition -- and reveal how courts pick and choose which liberties receive protection *(slide 2)*.

**II. Meyer v. Nebraska (1923) — The First Expansive Definition**
Anti-German hysteria during and after World War I produced 22 state laws banning foreign-language instruction *(slide 4)*. Nebraska's Siman Act was one; Robert Meyer was convicted and fined $25 for teaching a ten-year-old to read in German *(slide 5)*. Justice McReynolds, writing for a 7-2 majority, produced the first broad judicial definition of "liberty" under the Fourteenth Amendment: not merely freedom from bodily restraint, but also the right to contract, to engage in common occupations, to acquire knowledge, to marry, to establish a home, to bring up children, and to worship *(slide 6)*. The state's goal of teaching English was legitimate; the means -- prohibiting all foreign-language instruction -- went too far. As McReynolds put it, a desirable end cannot be promoted by prohibited means *(slide 7)*.

**III. Pierce v. Society of Sisters (1925) — The Child Is Not the Mere Creature of the State**
Oregon's 1922 compulsory education act, passed by ballot initiative with Ku Klux Klan support and backed by anti-Catholic nativism, required all children ages 8-16 to attend public schools *(slide 9)*. Two plaintiffs challenged it: the Society of Sisters (a Catholic teaching order) and Hill Military Academy *(slide 9)*. McReynolds again wrote unanimously, building directly on Meyer: the Act unreasonably interferes with the liberty of parents to direct the upbringing of their children *(slide 10)*. The Court could not give the schools themselves a liberty interest (corporations have none), so it protected parental rights derivatively through the schools' property interests in their buildings and goodwill *(slide 10)*. The operative principle: "The child is not the mere creature of the State; those who nurture him and direct his destiny have the right, coupled with the high duty, to recognize and prepare him for additional obligations" *(slide 11)*.

**IV. Buck v. Bell (1927) — The Doctrine's Failure**
The American eugenics movement, funded by Carnegie and Harriman money and given scientific legitimacy by the Eugenics Record Office, produced the Virginia Sterilization Act of 1924 -- a statute drafted by Aubrey Strode at the request of Dr. Albert Priddy *(slide 13)*. Carrie Buck was not feeble-minded *(slide 14)*. She was a foster child who had been raped by her foster family's nephew; when she became pregnant, the family had her committed to the Virginia Colony to hide the assault *(slide 14)*. Her daughter Vivian would later make the honor roll; she died of an intestinal infection at age eight *(slide 14)*. The trial lasted five hours and was orchestrated by the very people who wanted sterilization approved *(slide 15)*. Her appointed guardian, Irving Whitehead, was a former Colony board member who conducted no independent investigation and conceded key facts *(slide 15)*. Holmes's 8-1 majority opinion is a monument of analytical failure: he never asked whether bodily autonomy constituted a liberty interest under the Fourteenth Amendment, despite Meyer having defined liberty to include the right to "establish a home and bring up children" just four years earlier *(slide 18)*. He analogized forced sterilization to compulsory smallpox vaccination under Jacobson -- equating a $5 fine with surgical destruction of reproductive capacity *(slides 17-18)*. "Three generations of imbeciles are enough." Not one of the three was an imbecile *(slide 14)*. Over 60,000 Americans were forcibly sterilized under state eugenics laws; Nazi Germany directly modeled its 1933 hereditary health law on American statutes, citing Buck v. Bell as precedent *(slide 19)*. The case has never been explicitly overruled *(slide 19)*.

**V. United Public Workers v. Mitchell (1947) — The Ninth Amendment Sidelined**
Federal employees challenged the Hatch Act on First, Ninth, and Tenth Amendment grounds *(slide 21)*. The Court upheld the Act and held that when Congress acts within a granted power, Ninth and Tenth Amendment objections must fail *(slides 21-22)*. This effectively killed the Ninth Amendment as an independent source of unenumerated rights *(slide 21)*. Future litigation would have to travel through the Due Process Clause -- more fruitful but more haunted by Lochner *(slide 23)*.

**VI. Griswold v. Connecticut (1965) — Marital Privacy**
Connecticut's "little Comstock" law uniquely criminalized the use of contraceptives, not merely their sale *(slide 25)*. Estelle Griswold and Dr. C. Lee Buxton deliberately opened a birth control clinic in New Haven and were arrested nine days later *(slide 25)*. Douglas's majority opinion navigated the Lochner problem by distinguishing economic regulation (which gets rational basis) from intrusions into intimate marital relations *(slide 26)*. His source for that heightened protection was not the Fourteenth Amendment's text but the "penumbras, formed by emanations" from the specific guarantees of the Bill of Rights, creating zones of privacy *(slide 27)*. Douglas's rhetorical climax invokes the marital bedroom as a "sacred precinct" that police cannot search for telltale signs of contraceptive use *(slide 28)*. Goldberg's concurrence, joined by Warren and Brennan, directly invoked the Ninth Amendment to argue that the enumeration of rights in the Constitution does not disparage unenumerated ones, and called out Mitchell directly *(slide 29)*. But the Ninth Amendment path would not prevail; substantive due process remained the primary vehicle for unenumerated rights *(slide 29)*.

**VII. Loving v. Virginia (1967) — Marriage and the Glucksberg Paradox**
Richard Loving (white) and Mildred Jeter (Black and Native American) married in D.C. on June 2, 1958 to evade Virginia's ban and were convicted under the Racial Integrity Act of 1924 *(slide 31)*. The sentencing judge, Judge Bazile, explained that Almighty God placed the races on separate continents and did not intend for them to mix *(slide 31)*. Warren's unanimous opinion rests on two independent foundations: Equal Protection (Virginia's scheme was designed to maintain White Supremacy and racial classifications restricting marriage violate the central meaning of the clause) and Due Process (marriage is one of the basic civil rights of man, fundamental to our very existence and survival) *(slide 32)*. The dual holding insulates Loving even if either doctrine shifts -- Loving has two structural legs, and removing one still leaves the other *(slide 32)*. The Glucksberg paradox: Glucksberg (1997) requires fundamental rights to be "deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition" -- but anti-miscegenation laws existed from colonial times, were still enforced in 16 states in 1967, and some had the bans written into their constitutions *(slide 34)*. Applied literally, Glucksberg would condemn Loving as wrong, yet Loving is universally accepted as correct -- which the deck flags as the strongest single argument against a purely backward-looking approach to substantive due process *(slide 34)*.

**VIII. Moore v. City of East Cleveland (1977) — Extended Family**
Inez Moore lived in East Cleveland with her son and two grandsons who were cousins, not siblings, after one grandson came to live with her following his mother's death *(slide 36)*. East Cleveland's housing ordinance defined "family" so narrowly that cousins living together violated it; Moore was convicted, sentenced to 5 days in jail, and fined $25 *(slide 36)*. Powell's plurality struck down the ordinance: traditions of extended-family living -- uncles, aunts, cousins, grandparents -- have roots equally venerable and equally deserving of constitutional recognition as the nuclear family *(slide 37)*. Brennan's concurrence, joined by Marshall, added the racial dimension: the ordinance's narrow nuclear-family definition encoded a white suburban norm, while Black families -- shaped by economic disadvantage and cultural tradition -- far more frequently included extended family members *(slide 38)*. Thirteen percent of Black families (versus three percent of white families) included relatives under 18 beyond the couple's own children *(slide 36)*. A facially neutral housing ordinance criminalized survival mechanisms shaped by racial history *(slide 38)*. Powell echoed Pierce: the Constitution prevents East Cleveland from standardizing its children -- and its adults -- by forcing all to live in narrowly defined family patterns *(slide 38)*.

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

**The Holmes-Meyer Problem.** If Holmes had applied the Meyer framework in Buck v. Bell -- asking whether bodily autonomy to reproduce is within the "liberty" Meyer defined (the right to "establish a home and bring up children") -- would the result have changed? *(slide 40)* What does it tell us that he never even asked the question? Is Buck a case of methodological failure, deliberate avoidance, or something darker?

**The Glucksberg-Loving Collision.** Loving is universally accepted as correct. But anti-miscegenation laws were deeply rooted in American history and tradition *(slide 34)*. Does this mean Glucksberg's test cannot be the sole measure of fundamental rights? *(slide 40)* Or can you reconcile them by arguing the Fourteenth Amendment itself constitutes a tradition-breaking event whose purpose was to overcome certain historical traditions? If so, who decides which historical traditions the Fourteenth Amendment was designed to overcome?

**Moore and Whose Family Counts.** The ordinance in Moore was facially neutral *(slide 38)*. It did not on its face discriminate by race. Should the racial dimension in Brennan's concurrence factor into the constitutional analysis? *(slide 40)* If the ordinance disproportionately burdens Black family structures without racial intent, does it still violate the Constitution -- and under which doctrine?

**The Ninth Amendment Road.** Goldberg in Griswold tried to give the Ninth Amendment independent force *(slide 29)*. Mitchell had killed it *(slide 21)*. What would constitutional doctrine look like if Goldberg had prevailed? Would the Lochner problem disappear -- because unenumerated rights recognized under the Ninth Amendment carry no Lochner taint -- or would the problem simply relocate?

## Key Takeaways

Meyer established that "liberty" under the Fourteenth Amendment encompasses family autonomy broadly—the right to marry, raise children, and direct education. Pierce confirmed that the state cannot standardize children by forcing them through public schools. Buck v. Bell stands as the doctrine's catastrophic failure: Holmes never applied the Meyer framework and authorized state sterilization of a woman the Court falsely labeled feebleminded; the case remains on the books. Mitchell killed the Ninth Amendment as an independent rights source. Griswold protected marital privacy through "penumbras," navigating around Lochner by limiting economic deference to economic regulation. Loving established marriage as a fundamental liberty right and, through the Glucksberg paradox, provides the strongest single argument that purely historical analysis cannot be the whole story of constitutional rights. Moore extended family-autonomy protection to extended family structures and flagged how facially neutral ordinances can encode racial hierarchy.
