## Lecture Outline

**I. What Standing Is and Why It Matters**

Before any federal court reaches the merits, it must ask: is this the right plaintiff to bring this claim? *(slide 3)*. Standing determines who gets to use the federal courts -- and by extension, which government conduct is subject to judicial review at all *(slide 3)*. The source is Article III, § 2 -- the "Case or Controversy" requirement *(slide 3)*.

**II. The Irreducible Constitutional Minimum**

The deck's three-part test *(slide 4)*: Injury-in-fact (concrete and particularized, actual or imminent; not abstract, conjectural, or hypothetical); Traceability (fairly traceable to the challenged action, not the result of "independent action of some third party not before the court"); and Redressability (likely, not speculative, that a favorable decision will remedy the injury). Causation is backward-looking (did the defendant cause the injury?) and redressability is forward-looking (will a court order fix it?), tracing the same causal chain in opposite directions -- if A caused B, then reversing A usually fixes B *(slide 5)*. The cleanest separation appears in Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment (1998), where past violations caused harm but relief cannot undo what's done -- causation yes, redressability no *(slide 5)*.

**III. Prudential Standing: The Eroding Third Layer**

The deck identifies three judge-made limits beyond the constitutional minimum: no asserting the legal rights of third parties (jus tertii); no "generalized grievances" shared by all citizens; and the complaint must fall within the "zone of interests" of the statute invoked *(slide 6)*. The key distinction is that Congress can override prudential standing requirements but cannot override constitutional standing requirements *(slide 6)*. Lexmark v. Static Control (2014) called "prudential standing" a misnomer and reclassified zone-of-interests as statutory interpretation *(slide 7)*. Generalized grievances have been constitutionalized -- Lujan and Lexmark moved this bar into Article III injury-in-fact rather than treating it as a prudential overlay *(slide 7)*. Third-party standing is the last holdout from the old prudential framework, with Lexmark reserving the question and Thompson v. N. Am. Stainless (2011) blurring the line by applying zone-of-interests to Title VII *(slide 7)*. FDA v. R.J. Reynolds Vapor Co. (2025) confirmed zone of interests is "ordinary statutory interpretation," with the label abandoned as "misleading" *(slide 7)*. Bottom line: two of three pillars reclassified; the third is on borrowed time *(slide 7)*.

**IV. Simon v. Eastern Kentucky Welfare Rights Organization (1976) — The Third-Party Action Problem**

The IRS relaxed charitable-care requirements for tax-exempt hospitals; indigent patients who were turned away sued the IRS *(slide 9)*. The injury (denial of care) was real and concrete, but the causal chain ran IRS → Hospitals (third parties) → Plaintiffs *(slide 9)*. Powell's majority held it "purely speculative" whether denials of service were traceable to IRS policy, because hospitals had independent financial reasons for limiting care *(slide 9)*. The deck identifies the third-party-action pattern as recurring in Simon, Allen v. Wright, and Clapper -- when the causal chain runs through an independent third party, the Court treats the connection as speculative *(slide 10)*.

**V. Valley Forge Christian College v. Americans United (1982) — The Victimless Violation**

The government transferred a 77-acre campus to a religious college for free, and a nonprofit sued claiming Establishment Clause violation *(slide 12)*. Rehnquist's majority denied standing on two grounds: no taxpayer standing because the transfer was under the Property Clause (Art. IV), not the Taxing and Spending Clause (Art. I, § 8); and the only injury was the "psychological consequence" of observing conduct one disagrees with -- a generalized grievance *(slide 12)*. The implication: some constitutional violations have no judicial remedy *(slide 12)*. The "offended observer" arc: lower courts in the 1970s-80s invented offended observer standing after Lemon v. Kurtzman on the theory that if endorsement is the violation, the offended observer is the victim; in 2019 American Legion's Gorsuch concurrence called it a doctrine with "no basis in law"; and in 2022 Kennedy v. Bremerton overruled the Lemon test and endorsement test entirely, holding that "offense does not equate to coercion" *(slide 13)*.

**VI. Allen v. Wright (1984) — The Central Case**

Parents of Black children in desegregating public schools across seven states brought a nationwide class action against the IRS, claiming it failed to deny tax-exempt status to "segregation academies" -- private schools established to evade desegregation orders *(slide 15)*. The IRS's own policy, confirmed in Bob Jones University v. United States (1983), was that racially discriminatory schools cannot qualify for tax exemption *(slide 15)*. Two claimed injuries: stigmatic (federal endorsement of racial discrimination) and educational (tax exemptions subsidized white flight, diminishing desegregated education) *(slide 15)*.

The plaintiffs tried two strategies to distinguish Simon *(slide 16)*: first, sidestep traceability via stigmatic injury (if the injury is the government's own act of endorsing discrimination, then the government action is the injury); second, argue a tighter causal chain (unlike hospitals in Simon, the segregation academies existed for one purpose -- racial exclusion -- so the tax exemption directly subsidized that purpose, supported by Norwood v. Harrison (1973)) *(slide 16)*.

O'Connor's 5-3 majority denied standing on both *(slide 17)*. On stigmatic injury: standing only for "those persons who are personally denied equal treatment" -- plaintiffs were not applying to the schools, making it a generalized grievance. On desegregation injury: cognizable as "one of the most serious injuries in our legal system" but not fairly traceable because the causal chain was "attenuated at best" and depended on "independent action of some third party not before the court" *(slide 17)*. The circularity problem: O'Connor demanded specificity obtainable only through discovery, but standing is a prerequisite to discovery *(slide 17)*. The deck quotes O'Connor directly: "The law of Art. III standing is built on a single basic idea -- the idea of separation of powers" *(slide 18)*.

Brennan's solo dissent argued separation of powers is "mere incantation" -- not an analytical framework -- and that "common sense alone would recognize" that eliminating tax exemptions would lessen the schools' impact on desegregation, because unlike Simon the schools' entire purpose was racial exclusion *(slide 20)*. Stevens (joined by Blackmun) framed the government as providing "cash grants" to discriminatory schools and invoked elementary economics -- subsidize something, you get more of it; remove the subsidy, you get less -- concluding that standing has "nothing to do with" separation of powers *(slide 20)*.

**VII. Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife (1992) — Canonicalization**

Scalia formalized Allen v. Wright's framework into the canonical three-part test -- injury-in-fact, causation, redressability -- elevated to "irreducible constitutional minimum" status *(slide 22)*. Environmental groups challenged a regulation limiting the Endangered Species Act to domestic actions; plaintiffs' intent to return "some day" to foreign habitats was too indefinite for imminent injury *(slide 22)*. The separation-of-powers move: if Congress could convert the public interest in executive compliance into an individual right, it would "transfer from the President to the courts the Chief Executive's most important constitutional duty" *(slide 22)*.

**VIII. Clapper v. Amnesty International USA (2013) — Chain of Speculation**

Journalists and lawyers challenged NSA surveillance under § 702 of FISA, but targeting decisions were classified -- plaintiffs could not prove interception *(slide 23)*. Alito's majority found a "highly attenuated chain of possibilities" and held the threatened injury was not "certainly impending" *(slide 23)*. The parallel to Allen v. Wright: both cases demand deterministic certainty while refusing probabilistic reasoning accepted elsewhere -- the more systemic and secretive the government conduct, the harder it is for anyone to challenge it *(slide 23)*.

**IX. Trump v. CASA (2025) — Universal Injunctions**

The arc slide places Trump v. CASA at the 2025 endpoint as the universal-injunctions chapter alongside the six earlier standing cases *(slide 24)*. Standing limits who can sue; universal injunction limits constrain what courts can do when they do find standing; together they narrow the federal judiciary's structural role *(slide 24)*.

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

**The Allen Circularity.** The deck flags directly that O'Connor demanded specificity obtainable only through discovery, but standing is a prerequisite to discovery *(slide 17)*. This page asks whether this is a genuine doctrinal problem or appropriate gatekeeping, and how one would draft a complaint that cleared the Allen v. Wright bar.

**Stevens's Economics Argument.** The deck preserves Stevens's "subsidize something, get more of it; remove the subsidy, get less" framing as a direct challenge to the O'Connor traceability analysis *(slide 20)*. This page asks why O'Connor rejected this and whether the difficulty is the magnitude or the dependence on white parents' choices.

**The Systemic Coverage Gap.** The deck flags directly that the more systemic and secretive the government conduct, the harder it is for anyone to challenge it -- naming Clapper as the paradigm and the third-party action problem as the structural pattern *(slides 10, 23)*. This page asks whether there is a principle that justifies this pattern or whether it is an inadvertent structural consequence.

**Prudential Standing's Fate.** Lexmark reclassified zone of interests as statutory interpretation *(slide 7)*. This page asks whether third-party standing should be reclassified similarly and what would change if jus tertii were statutory rather than constitutional.

## Key Takeaways

Standing is not a procedural technicality—it determines which government conduct federal courts can review at all. The irreducible constitutional minimum (injury-in-fact, traceability, redressability) was formalized by Lujan as a three-part test explicitly grounded in separation of powers. The third-party action problem (Simon, Allen) treats causal chains running through independent actors as speculative. The victimless violation (Valley Forge) means some constitutional violations may have no judicial remedy. Allen v. Wright is the central case: stigmatic injury requires personal denial of equal treatment, while even a cognizable desegregation injury failed traceability because of independent school choices—despite Brennan's and Stevens's compelling common-sense and economic arguments. Clapper shows that secretive, systemic programs face the hardest standing challenges. Prudential standing is largely being absorbed into constitutional doctrine or statutory interpretation, leaving the three-part Lujan test as the primary battleground. The arc from 1976 to 2025 (Simon through Trump v. CASA) is a trajectory of contraction of the federal judiciary's structural role.

## Connections

- **Allen v. Wright and IRS** → direct follow-on to Bob Jones University v. United States (1983), which established that racially discriminatory schools cannot be tax-exempt; Allen assumes that premise but denies standing to enforce it
- **Lujan's separation of powers** → connected to Political Question Doctrine (executive determinations insulated from review); both limit judicial oversight of executive conduct
- **Clapper and surveillance** → raises FISA context; connected to Fourth Amendment third-party doctrine in Carpenter v. United States (2018)
- **Valley Forge offended-observer doctrine** → collapsed after American Legion (2019) and Kennedy v. Bremerton (2022); connected to Establishment Clause doctrine lecture
- **Trump v. CASA universal injunctions** → connects to equitable remedies doctrine and the Court's recent skepticism of nationwide injunctions in immigration, regulatory, and executive-action cases
