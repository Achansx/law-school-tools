## Professor Emphasis

### From source

The deck opens with a three-tile warning: "DO NOT CONFUSE THIS WITH THE PRIVILEGES OR IMMUNITIES CLAUSE OF THE 14TH AMENDMENT" *(p. 2)*. Article IV P&I is presented as "essentially an equality right, not an absolute right": absolute rights set floors or ceilings, while equality rights "do not directly set floors or ceilings, but by prohibiting classification on certain bases they may affect the calculus for legislative determination of rights" *(p. 3)*. The doctrine is part of the Constitution's "glue" alongside full faith and credit and recovery of fugitive slaves, with Cardozo's "sink or swim together" framing *(p. 4)*. Corfield v. Coryell is flagged as "strangely, the leading case is not a Supreme Court case" -- Bushrod Washington riding circuit, with the substantive dispute about New Jersey oyster-harvesting *(p. 7)*. The deck calls the resulting catalogue "The Oyster Civil Rights" *(p. 9)* and emphasizes the trailing "and many others which might be mentioned" -- the "unspecified friendship rights" *(p. 10)*. Modern cases proceed through Hicklin (broad Alaska Hire law fails the "peculiar source of the evil" prong) *(pp. 13-14)*, Baldwin (elk hunting not "basic to the maintenance or wellbeing of the Union") *(p. 26)*, McBurney (FOIA as political accountability, not protectionist) *(p. 23)*, and Piper (substantial reason + substantial relationship) *(p. 25)*. Saenz v. Roe is taught alongside Article IV P&I to contrast it with the right to travel and 14th Amendment P or I; the deck flags Saenz as "a very difficult case, particularly for beginning students" to revisit later *(p. 15)*. Distinctions from DCC: Congress cannot waive Article IV P&I, and there is no market-participant exception *(p. 24)*.

### Editor's framing

This page treats the warning against confusing Art. IV with the 14th Amendment P or I as the doctrine's organizing claim and packages Corfield's oyster facts as the doctrine's foundational "fundamental rights" puzzle. The framing of Saenz as a contrast case is the deck's structure, but the deck does not itself characterize the right-to-travel doctrine as a "different hook." The DCC comparison table is editorial synthesis of the deck's distinct slides (24-25) on distinctions and similarities. The Dred Scott framing as a "leading case on PI-IV" is faithful to the deck's cloud bubble (p. 4), but the page's reading that Article IV P&I "played a structural role in Taney's argument" is editorial sharpening of the bullet that Taney denied citizenship because it would exempt free Black persons from oppressive Southern laws (p. 4).

## Lecture Outline

### The Clause and Its Structure

Article IV, Section 2, Clause 1: "The Citizens of each State shall be entitled to all Privileges and Immunities of Citizens in the several States" *(p. 4)*.

The textual command: if a state grants a "privilege" or "immunity," it cannot restrict it to its own citizens -- whomever another state recognizes as a citizen, the granting state must grant the right too *(p. 4)*. The Clause is part of the Constitution's structural "glue" alongside Full Faith and Credit and recovery of fugitive slaves *(p. 4)*. Justice Cardozo framed the principle: the Constitution "was framed upon the theory that the peoples of the several states must sink or swim together, and that in the long run prosperity and salvation are in union and not division" *(p. 4)*.

**It is an equality right, not an absolute right** *(p. 3)*. Absolute rights set floors or ceilings; equality rights do not, but by prohibiting classification on certain bases they affect the legislative calculus *(p. 3)*. The deck's illustration: "If I make a law that is adverse to the interests of class A, whom I dislike, I have to apply the same law to class B, whom I like. Since I now have to take harm to B into account, I won't enact the law" *(p. 3)*.

### Who Is Protected

The deck lists *(p. 5)*: natural United States citizens; "not corporations or other artificial persons" (Paul v. Virginia); and not aliens either. By contrast, the deck notes corporations and aliens "have rights under DCC" and the First Amendment (Citizens United on free speech; church cases on free exercise) *(p. 5)*. The clause acts against "discrimination based on state citizenship," and durational residency discrimination is "not covered" by Article IV P&I *(p. 6)*.

### The Role of Dred Scott

The deck flags that "a leading case on PI-IV is Dred Scott v. Sandford" *(p. 4)*. Per Justice Taney in Dred Scott, even free Black persons could never have been intended as eligible for US citizenship because they would then be exempt from the oppressive laws Southern states had in place for persons of African descent *(p. 4)*. Article IV P&I thus played a structural role in Taney's argument for excluding Black Americans from constitutional citizenship altogether.

### Corfield v. Coryell: What Counts as "Fundamental"?

Not all classifications based on state citizenship will founder on PI-IV *(p. 7)*. Strangely, the leading case is not a Supreme Court case (Corfield v. Coryell), but it does come from Justice Bushrod Washington -- "the nephew, which is almost as good as coming from George" -- and "the case is about oysters!" *(p. 7)*. Washington's test confines Article IV P&I to those privileges and immunities that "are, in their nature, fundamental; which belong, of right, to the citizens of all free governments; and which have, at all times, been enjoyed by the citizens of the several states which compose this Union, from the time of their becoming free, independent, and sovereign" *(p. 8)*.

**The "Oyster Civil Rights" catalog** *(p. 9)*: protection by the government -- "the enjoyment of life and liberty, with the right to acquire and possess property of every kind, and to pursue and obtain happiness and safety" -- "subject nevertheless to such restraints as the government may justly prescribe for the general good of the whole"; the right of a citizen of one state to pass through or reside in any other state "for purposes of trade, agriculture, professional pursuits, or otherwise"; the right to claim the benefit of habeas corpus; to institute and maintain actions in the courts of the state; to take, hold, and dispose of real or personal property; and exemption from higher taxes than those paid by other citizens of the state.

**The unspecified friendship rights:** Washington also said: "These, and many others which might be mentioned, are, strictly speaking, privileges and immunities" -- a deliberate catalog non-closure tied to the Articles of Confederation preamble's purpose of "secur[ing] and perpetuat[ing] mutual friendship and intercourse among the people of the different states of the Union" *(p. 10)*.

### Hicklin v. Orbeck (1978)

Alaska had an unemployment problem in the 1970s and enacted the "Alaska Hire" law requiring many employers to prefer Alaska residents *(p. 13)*. The law extended to employers with no connection to the state's oil and gas, who performed no work on state land, had no contractual relationship with the State, received no payment from the State -- even suppliers who provide goods or services to subcontractors *(p. 13)*. Brennan, J., 9-0 held the law violated Article IV P&I *(p. 13)*.

**Alaska's argument** *(p. 14)*: (1) the State's unemployment problem -- but the State must show that nonresidents are a "peculiar source of the evil at which the statute is aimed"; (2) the pipeline ("state participant" metaphor) -- but Alaska has little or no proprietary interest in much of the activity, and the connection of the State's oil and gas with much of the covered activity is "too attenuated." The deck poses the question whether the state-participant concept should be imported into PI-IV at all *(p. 14)*.

### Baldwin v. Fish and Game Commission of Montana (1978)

Montana believed it needed to protect elk from excessive hunting and charged out-of-staters much more for an elk hunting license *(p. 26)*. Blackmun held that "only with respect to those 'privileges' and 'immunities' bearing upon the vitality of the Nation as a single entity must the State treat all citizens, resident and nonresident, equally" *(p. 26)*. Elk hunting by nonresidents is "a recreation and a sport," "not a means to the nonresident's livelihood"; "Equality in access to Montana elk is not basic to the maintenance or wellbeing of the Union"; "elk hunting by nonresidents in Montana is not one of" the fundamental rights *(p. 26)*.

The holding crystallizes the doctrine's baseline: Article IV P&I protects rights bearing on national vitality (livelihood, court access) more robustly than recreational activities. The deck's flip-side note: "Harry Blackman was clearly not a hunter" *(p. 26)*.

### The Two-Prong Discrimination Test (Supreme Court of New Hampshire v. Piper, 1985)

Piper synthesizes the doctrine: "The Clause does not preclude discrimination against nonresidents where (i) there is a substantial reason for the difference in treatment; and (ii) the discrimination practiced against nonresidents bears a substantial relationship to the State's objective" *(p. 25)*. The deck flags this as a similarity to DCC -- "the anti-discrimination principle [is] not absolute" *(p. 25)*.

### McBurney v. Young (2013)

Virginia enacted a law restricting its FOIA law to Virginia residents *(p. 23)*. Challenged by an individual who wanted to find out why Virginia messed up his request for child support, and by a person who wanted to make a business of examining county real estate records *(p. 23)*. The Supreme Court unanimously upheld the residency restriction *(p. 23)*: no protectionist purpose was shown (FOIA conceptualized as an effort at political accountability); no showing of intent to economically advantage Virginians; and no DCC violation either because it does not burden or regulate interstate commerce *(p. 23)*. Thomas concurred -- "there is no DCC!" *(p. 23)*.

### Saenz v. Roe (1999) and the Right to Travel

The deck flags Saenz v. Roe as a "very difficult case" to revisit late in the course *(p. 15)*. California enacted a statute setting welfare benefits, for the first year of residency, at the level the newly arrived would have received in their former state *(p. 15)*. California's arguments: it saves money; it is not punitive; distinguishable from Shapiro v. Thompson (equal protection, strict scrutiny); rational basis should be used *(p. 16)*. The deck flags a "tiers obsession" worth discussing *(p. 16)*.

Stevens, J., grounded the holding in the "right to travel" *(p. 17)*, which the deck notes is unenumerated: "How do you figure out the meaning of unenumerated (a/k/a imaginary) rights?" with "not much textual or historical analysis" *(p. 17)*. The right protects three components *(p. 17)*: the right of a citizen of one State to enter and to leave another State; the right to be treated as a welcome visitor rather than an unfriendly alien when temporarily present in the second State; and for those travelers who elect to become permanent residents, the right to be treated like other citizens of that State.

The Court reconciles its holding with cases permitting durational distinctions (Baldwin and Vlandis v. Kline -- hunting license and state university tuition) by observing that the Clause's protections are not "absolute," but "does bar discrimination against citizens of other States where there is no substantial reason for the discrimination beyond the mere fact that they are citizens of other States" *(p. 18)*. The deck quotes the Court's distinction from college education: welfare benefits "will be consumed while they remain in California," so there is no danger of out-of-staters establishing residency just long enough to acquire a readily portable benefit, "such as a divorce or a college education," to be enjoyed after return to their original domicile *(p. 19)*.

Rehnquist and Thomas dissented *(p. 20)*: they agreed a literal (non-textual) right to travel exists and that Article IV P&I would have protected these persons if they had remained non-citizens of California. They disagreed with the revival of 14th Amendment privileges and immunities; argued States should retain the ability to use bona fide residence requirements "to ferret out those who intend to take the privileges and run"; and treated welfare as an investment in human capital, "as much an investment in human capital as is a tuition subsidy, and their attendant benefits are just as 'portable'" *(p. 20)*. Thomas's separate dissent wanted to "reopen our whole PI-XIV clause jurisprudence" -- the deck notes he has "gained perhaps one additional vote" since, and that his position is hard to understand until the 14th Amendment is covered *(p. 21)*.

### Distinctions from the Dormant Commerce Clause

The deck lists distinctions *(p. 24)*: Congress cannot waive Article IV P&I (no Prudential v. Benjamin equivalent); and there is no market participant exception. Similarities with DCC *(p. 25)*: in theory, the anti-discrimination principle is not absolute -- Piper's substantial reason + substantial relationship test.

| Attribute | Article IV P&I | Dormant Commerce Clause |
|-----------|---------------|------------------------|
| Who is protected | Natural US citizens | Corporations and aliens too |
| Congress's power | Cannot waive by statute (Prudential exception does not apply) | Congress can authorize discriminatory state laws |
| Market participant exception | No equivalent | States acting as market participants are exempt |
| Standard | Substantial reason + substantial relationship | Strict scrutiny for discrimination; Pike balancing for incidental burdens |

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

**Maui Fires Hypo (from slides):** Maui politicians are unhappy to see mostly out-of-state developers buying up large tracts of property destroyed in the 2023 wildfires, calling them "like vultures" *(p. 22)*. Two proposals *(p. 22)*: (1) Prohibit all sales over $1 million without a Maui Property Evaluation Commission finding that the price is fair to local residents (non-discriminatory); (2) Prohibit all sales to non-residents of Hawaii for three years unless purchasers agree to pay 20% of the purchase price into a Maui Disaster Recovery Fund (discriminatory by residency). Under Article IV P&I, proposal (2) requires substantial reason (protecting local residents from displacement? a legitimate interest) and substantial relationship (is a 20% surcharge the right remedy?). Note that the DCC would also apply to proposal (2) if it burdens interstate commerce -- here commerce in real estate.

**Tiers of Scrutiny Parallel:** Professor Chandler notes that students will be tempted to apply equal protection tiers to P&I analysis. Resist this: P&I has its own framework. The Court has not adopted strict scrutiny for fundamental P&I rights in the equal-protection sense; it asks instead whether there is a substantial and substantially related state interest.

## Key Takeaways

- Article IV P&I (not 14th Amendment P or I) prohibits states from discriminating against non-resident citizens regarding fundamental interstate rights.
- "Fundamental" is narrow: economic livelihood and court access are protected; recreation is not (Baldwin).
- The two-part test: substantial reason + substantial relationship between the discrimination and the state's objective.
- Corporations and aliens are not protected by Article IV P&I (though they have DCC rights).
- Congress cannot waive Article IV P&I restrictions; there is no market-participant exception.
- Saenz v. Roe involves a different doctrine (right to travel + 14th Amendment P or I) -- track the constitutional hook carefully on exams.
