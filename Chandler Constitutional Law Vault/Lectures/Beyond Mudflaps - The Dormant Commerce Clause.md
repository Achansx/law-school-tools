# Beyond Mudflaps - The Dormant Commerce Clause

## Professor Emphasis

### From source

The deck opens with a "why should you care" frame: many state regulations have significant effects on interstate and international commerce, and the DCC can infiltrate e-commerce, internet regulation, abortion, and laws addressing morality — a "secret litigation weapon" and a lens into thinking about non-textual parts of constitutional law (slide 2). The threshold question of legitimacy is anchored in the Thomas dissent in Camps Newfound/Owatonna (1997) — "The negative Commerce Clause has no basis in the text of the Constitution, makes little sense, and has proved virtually unworkable in application" — and the Scalia dissent in Wynne (2015), which calls the doctrine a "judicial fraud" (slide 4). The deck explicitly frames the DCC as a "meta-statute" — a default rule about what laws courts will deem Congress to have auto-preempted (slide 9). It walks the rule (slide 6), exceptions including congressional authorization and the state-as-market-participant doctrine (slides 7-8), the two-century historical struggle (slides 11-15), the modern approach with discriminatory intent/effect, Pike balancing, and tax implementation under Complete Auto Transit (slides 16-25), Wynne and the internal-consistency test (slides 26-29), extraterritoriality (slides 30-31), and National Pork Producers Council v. Ross (slides 32-41). The deck closes with the once-you-see-it-everywhere observation and the broader question of how to think about non-textual parts of constitutional law (slides 42-43).

## Lecture Outline

**I. The Threshold Question: Is the DCC Legitimate?**
Thomas (dissenting in *Camps Newfound/Owatonna*, 1997): "The negative Commerce Clause has no basis in the text of the Constitution, makes little sense, and has proved virtually unworkable in application" (slide 4).

Scalia (dissenting in *Comptroller of Treas. of Maryland v. Wynne*, 2015): the Constitution does not contain a negative Commerce Clause; nothing authorizes judges to set aside state laws they believe burden commerce; the "clearest sign that the negative Commerce Clause is a judicial fraud" is the holding that congressional consent can lift a constitutional prohibition (slide 4).

The deck flags that at least 81 Supreme Court cases discuss the DCC or "negative commerce clause," with several more using its ideas (slide 5). The professor presents the critique as a lens into thinking about non-textual constitutional law generally — commandeering, sovereign immunity beyond the Eleventh Amendment, and much of separation of powers doctrine also lack explicit textual grounding (slide 43).

**II. The DCC as Meta-Statute**
Think of the DCC as a default rule about what laws the courts will deem Congress to have auto-preempted without writing an explicit preemption statute (slide 9). Congress may override this default by affirmatively authorizing state laws that would otherwise violate the DCC — the McCarran-Ferguson Act regulating insurance and undoing Southeastern Underwriters, per Prudential v. Benjamin (1946), and the Webb-Kenyon Act of 1913 allowing states to bar out-of-state alcohol (slide 7).

This framing distinguishes DCC from preemption: preemption arises when a state law conflicts with an actual federal statute or regulation, so analysis usually starts with the statute and uses DCC as a fallback (slide 9). Congress can also simply preempt state laws without putting much in their place — ERISA preemption being the paradigm, alongside the Federal Arbitration Act (1925) and the ICC Termination Act (1995) (slide 10).

Exception: the state-as-market-participant doctrine. Where a state is truly acting as a market participant it can prefer in-state customers like any other business (slide 8). But it cannot impose downstream restrictions on products it has sold to out-of-state purchasers — a major limitation, per *S.-Cent. Timber Dev., Inc. v. Wunnicke* (slide 8).

**III. The Black-Letter Rule**
A state law violates the DCC if it (slide 6):
1. Discriminates against interstate commerce — through intentional discrimination, a discriminatory "non-incidental" effect, or an unbalanced burden under Pike balancing even if the effect is incidental (slide 6)
2. Regulates extraterritorially too much (slide 6)
3. In tax cases, fails Complete Auto Transit's four-part framework, with the "internal consistency" idea — what if every state did this — operating as a key tool (slide 6)

The DCC also applies to state treatment of foreign commerce (*Japan Line, Ltd. v. City of Los Angeles*, 1979) — though that test is a little more complicated — and the deck flags an open question for Indian tribes (slide 6).

**IV. Historical Development**
- *Willson v. Black Bird Creek Marsh Co.* (1829): dormant commerce as a defense to trespass for wrecking the state's dam; the dam does hinder murky navigable waters and raises a possibility of preemption, but for murky reasons does not impede Congress's dormant commerce power — the power is imagined but not deployed (slide 12).
- *Cooley v. Board of Wardens* (1851): "Whatever subjects of this power are in their nature national, or admit only of one uniform system, or plan of regulation, may justly be said to be of such a nature as to require exclusive legislation by Congress" (slide 13). McLean's dissent: the power is "recognised in the State, because the subject is more appropriate for State than Federal action" (slide 13).
- *Barnwell Brothers* (1938): South Carolina's size and weight restrictions on trucks upheld under a deferential rational-basis-like test — where the legislature acts within its power, fairly debatable questions as to reasonableness and wisdom are not for courts, even when the regulation incidentally burdens interstate commerce (slide 14).
- *Hunt v. Washington State Apple Advertising Commission* (1977): North Carolina barred out-of-state growers from marking their apples at higher grades; NC claimed consumer protection from fraud, but the record showed protectionism (NC apple producers, not consumers, wanted the law) (slide 15). The practical effect both burdened interstate sales and discriminated against Washington State apples, stripping away Washington's investment-backed advantage and leveling its competitive advantage (slide 15).
- *Bibb v. Navajo Freight Lines* (1959): Arizona required trucks to use straight mudflaps; Navajo Freight Lines had mudflaps contoured to the shape of the truck's fenders (slide 18). Struck down as discriminatory effect.

**V. The Modern Framework in Detail**

*Discriminatory Intent:* very seldom exists without discriminatory effect (slide 17). The deck's hypothetical: Texas places a 25% excise tax on importation of Bluebonnets, asserting that letting out-of-state growers ship them in would make Texas less special; as it happens Bluebonnets only grow in Texas, but a violation exists on discriminatory intent even where the burden on interstate commerce is less than incidental (slide 17).

*Discriminatory Effect:* the deck pairs *Hughes v. Oklahoma* (1979) — Oklahoma's law prohibiting export of minnow and other baitfish — with *Bibb* on the mudflaps as the canonical examples (slide 18). The deck also asks whether a particular Alaska salmon-tax scheme — 4% on salmon frozen on ship but canned in Washington, 6% canned in Alaska, 1% caught in Alaska and sold frozen in Alaska, none if sold fresh — counts as discrimination, illustrating that the answer is not always obvious (slide 19).

*Pike Balancing:* "Where the statute regulates evenhandedly to effectuate a legitimate local public interest, and its effects on interstate commerce are only incidental … it will be upheld unless the burden imposed on such commerce is clearly excessive in relation to the putative local benefits" (slide 20). If a legitimate local purpose is found, the question becomes one of degree, with the extent of the burden tolerated depending on the nature of the local interest and whether it could be promoted with a lesser impact on interstate activities (slide 20). The deck explicitly worries about "lurking Lochnerism": balancing is "kind of subjective" when the court weighs interstate burdens against putative local benefits, and judicial competence depends on measuring a benefit (slide 22).

*Tax Implementation — Complete Auto Transit v. Brady* (1977): Mississippi imposed a 5% gross-receipts tax on transportation businesses operating in the state; Mississippi applied the tax to a business moving cars from Michigan to Mississippi (slide 23). A taxpayer has a good chance if the tax (slide 24): lacks a sufficient nexus with the state; discriminates against interstate commerce; is unfairly apportioned; or is unrelated to services provided by the state. But "unrelated" does not mean comparing benefits to taxes paid — instead look to whether the tax's identical application by every state would place interstate commerce at a disadvantage compared with intrastate (slide 24). *Commonwealth Edison v. Montana* (1981) upheld Montana's large coal severance tax (90% of Montana coal shipped out of state); the measure of the tax must be reasonably related to the extent of the contact, but courts do not compare the value of benefits conferred against taxes paid, and the appropriate level or rate is "essentially a matter for legislative, and not judicial, resolution" (slide 25).

*Extraterritoriality:* the rule (slide 31): (1) a state statute may not apply to commerce wholly outside the state even if the commerce has effects within the state; (2) a state statute regulating commerce wholly outside the state's borders is unconstitutional even if the legislature did not intend extraterritorial application; (3) statutes are evaluated by considering how they interact with legitimate regulation in other states and what would happen if every state enacted similar laws. *Edgar v. MITE* (1982) — Illinois corporate acquirer must register with the Illinois Secretary of State, who could block the offer — raises the question whether the doctrine sounds in DCC or due process / minimum contacts; the deck flags the broader question whether Texas could criminalize conduct legal in California (slide 30).

*Comptroller of Maryland v. Wynne* (2015, 5-4): Maryland has both state and county income taxes and does not let residents credit other states' income taxes against the county tax, while also taxing non-residents on Maryland-sourced income (slide 28). Applying the internal-consistency test (slide 29): were every state to adopt Maryland's scheme including the special non-resident tax, taxpayers living in one state but earning income in another would be taxed twice while purely intrastate taxpayers would be taxed only once — interstate commerce would be disadvantaged relative to purely intrastate commerce. The county component of Maryland's tax fails (slide 29).

**VI. National Council of Pork Producers v. Ross (2023)**
In 2018, California voters passed Proposition 12, amending the state Health and Safety Code to prohibit the sale of pork from animals confined in a manner inconsistent with California standards (slide 32). The law is not discriminatory in that it applies to pigs raised in California too — but California produces very little pork, and the law may impel hog producers in producing states to conform to avoid losing the California market (slide 32). The Ninth Circuit held that Baldwin is not limited to price-control cases but that upstream effects don't count (slide 32).

The conflict (slide 33): pork producers argue it is hard for a reseller to know the conditions of the sow, that indemnity from criminal violations is unavailable, and that producers nationwide would have to conform to the California standard or forego the California market. Californians argue their sense of morality is subverted if citizens can buy cheap pork produced under cruel conditions — what if it were slave labor or child labor? — and that the law fosters humane pork production.

Why might this law violate the DCC even though it does not discriminate against interstate commerce (slide 35)? The deck pairs the question with two extraterritoriality precedents: *Baldwin v. G. A. F. Seelig* (1935) — New York's minimum-price-for-milk applied to out-of-state producers, struck down as "like a milk tariff" — and *Healy v. Beer Institute* (1989) — Connecticut's requirement that out-of-state beer sellers pledge their Connecticut prices were no higher than non-Connecticut prices, struck down as discriminatory (what if every state did this?) (slide 35). The deck reads SCOTUS as treating these cases as having an anti-discrimination principle rather than a true extraterritorial-effect principle (slide 35).

The deck pushes back against any per se extraterritoriality rule (slide 38): in our interconnected national marketplace, many state laws have "the practical effect of controlling" extraterritorial behavior — state income tax, environmental regulation, defamation — but the legislative power of a state is generally limited to acts upon persons and property within its territory, and not only the DCC but Full Faith and Credit and Due Process are involved (slide 38).

On Pike balancing in NPPC, the majority reinterprets Pike as a way to smoke out a discriminatory purpose (slide 39): why would you write a law in which local benefits were small compared to the burden on interstate commerce unless protectionism was the aim? Gorsuch asks: "How is a court supposed to compare or weigh economic costs (to some) against noneconomic benefits (to others)?" (slide 39). If pig husbandry "imperatively demands" a single uniform nationwide rule, those affected are free to petition Congress; preventing state officials from enforcing a democratically adopted state law in the DCC's name is "a matter of extreme delicacy," to be done only where "the infraction is clear" (slide 39). Sotomayor and Kagan dislike the Gorsuch reconstruction and would just say NPPC hasn't shown a substantial burden (slide 39); Barrett likes the incommensurability emphasis but thinks the costs are large (slide 39). Roberts dissented (slide 40).

Justice Kavanaugh added that the law should also be analyzed under the Import-Export Clause, the Privileges and Immunities Clause, and the Full Faith and Credit Clause, worrying that California's regulation could be "a blueprint for other States" to shutter markets against goods produced in ways that offend their moral or policy preferences and force other states to regulate accordingly (slide 41).

Once you are sensitive to the DCC, you can see it everywhere (slide 42): lots of state laws have effects on interstate commerce, and once into Pike balancing the state begins to bear a burden — try to denigrate the putative local benefit, quantify or expand the burden on interstate commerce, and adapt the internal-consistency idea from tax to conventional regulations by asking "what if every state did this," generalizing "this," especially as states grow efforts to export their notions of morality (slide 42).

## Cases Discussed

### [[Willson v. Black Bird Creek Marsh Co.]] (1829)
State dam on navigable waterway; dormant commerce power "imagined but not deployed." Early articulation of the doctrine's existence without application.

### [[Cooley v. Board of Wardens]] (1851)
National uniformity test for subjects requiring a single national rule; local subjects may be regulated by states pending federal action. Early doctrinal framework.

### [[South Carolina State Highway Department v. Barnwell Brothers]] (1938)
Size and weight restrictions on trucks upheld under rational-basis-like review. Pre-modern deferential approach to state regulations incidentally burdening interstate commerce.

### [[Hunt v. Washington State Apple Advertising Commission]] (1977)
North Carolina's apple grading ban struck down as discriminatory in effect. Despite facial neutrality, the law stripped Washington State's competitive investment-backed advantage. Evidence of protectionist motivation (apple producers, not consumers, favored the law).

### [[Bibb v. Navajo Freight Lines]] (1959)
Illinois contoured-mudflap requirement struck down under Pike balancing. Incompatible with the straight-mudflap standard required (or permitted) in Arkansas and elsewhere; burden on interstate trucking clearly excessive relative to safety benefits.<!-- corrected 2026-05-26: page previously listed "Arizona straight-mudflap requirement"; canonical Bibb is Illinois-contoured -->

### [[Commonwealth Edison Co. v. Montana]] (1981)
Montana's large coal severance tax upheld even though 90% of coal is exported. Courts do not compare the value of services to taxpayers against the amount of taxes paid.

### [[Comptroller of Maryland v. Wynne]] (2015)
Maryland county income tax lacking a credit for out-of-state income taxes struck down. Internal consistency test: every-state application would disadvantage interstate commerce relative to purely intrastate commerce.

### [[National Council of Pork Producers v. Ross]] (2023)
California Prop 12 (pork hog housing standards) upheld by plurality. Not discriminatory. Not extraterritorial. Pike balancing reconstructed as a tool for detecting hidden protectionism, not for weighing incommensurable values. Gorsuch plurality joined by Thomas, Sotomayor, and Kagan—cross-ideological alignment.

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

**The Texas Tree Anti-Torture Act of 2024** (slide 36): after Vermont passes a statute providing additional protection for abortion providers, Texas enacts findings that harvesting maple trees to extract their syrup is barbaric and painful to the trees, then prohibits in-state sale of maple syrup derived from sap harvested by methods that restrict a tree's vascular flow for more than 8 hours per season — applying to all maple syrup sold in Texas regardless of where the sap was harvested (slide 36). Maple syrup providers in Canada, Vermont, and elsewhere say this will greatly increase prices; Texas accounts for 8% of national maple syrup consumption (slide 36). Constitutional?

**The Massachusetts Barbecue Filtration Act of 2023** (slide 37): Massachusetts requires any barbecue device to contain CO2 recapture devices and an interlock system that extinguishes the heat source if recapture is bypassed (slide 37). Manufacturers complain this will double the cost of barbecue equipment; Massachusetts itself contains only a few artisan barbecue manufacturers, with most made overseas or in Tennessee and Texas (slide 37). Does NPPC's reconstruction of Pike balancing — asking whether the law's local benefits are genuine or pretextual — change the analysis?

**The Morality Problem in Pike Balancing:** How should courts balance the alleged moral benefits of banning pork from confined pigs against the economic burden on pork producers nationwide? Gorsuch says courts can't. Sotomayor says just apply traditional Pike. Is Gorsuch's incommensurability point correct as a matter of institutional competence?

**Internet and E-Commerce:** A state requires all online retailers to collect state sales tax at the point of purchase. A state requires all internet service providers to block certain content for in-state users. How does DCC apply? Does the internal consistency test help?

## Key Takeaways

1. **The DCC has no textual basis—and that's the most interesting thing about it.** Thomas and Scalia are right that the Constitution contains no negative commerce clause. The DCC is a judge-made default rule about congressional preemption. That it has persisted for nearly two centuries suggests that non-textual constitutional doctrine can achieve stability, but its legitimacy remains contested.

2. **The meta-statute framing is useful.** Think of the DCC as specifying what laws Congress is presumed to have preempted without writing a preemption statute. Congress can override this default by authorizing state laws (McCarran-Ferguson, Webb-Kenyon). This framing distinguishes DCC from preemption and explains why congressional consent "lifts" a DCC violation.

3. **Discrimination against interstate commerce triggers near-per-se invalidity.** Whether the discrimination is intentional or has a non-incidental discriminatory effect, the state bears a near-impossible burden to justify the law. Hunt v. Washington is the paradigm case.

4. **Pike balancing is the contested frontier after NPPC.** The plurality's reconstruction—Pike as protectionism detector, not cost-benefit weighing—is a significant doctrinal shift. But the plurality commanded only 3 votes for this reconstruction. The scope of judicial balancing remains unsettled.

5. **The extraterritoriality doctrine is more limited than its critics fear.** NPPC rejected the argument that upstream out-of-state production effects constitute regulated-out-of-state conduct. A California sales standard is not a California production mandate even if market pressure makes compliance effectively necessary.

6. **The state-as-market-participant exception is a major limitation.** When a state is genuinely buying or selling in the market, it can prefer in-state parties as any private actor would. The exception draws a line at downstream restrictions—it does not extend to regulatory mandates on how goods are produced or used after purchase.
