## Lecture Outline

**The Three-Category Framework: Lopez (1995)**

United States v. Lopez (5-4, Rehnquist) struck down the Gun-Free School Zones Act of 1990, which made it a federal offense "for any individual knowingly to possess a firearm at a place that the individual knows, or has reasonable cause to believe, is a school zone" *(slide 2)*. Rehnquist's OR test identified three categories *(slide 4)*: (1) channels of interstate commerce -- the pipes themselves (roads, railroads, the internet) -- which Congress may regulate to preserve their pristineness; (2) instrumentalities of interstate commerce -- things that go through the channels (planes, trains, automobiles, routers, computers, people); and (3) activities having a substantial effect on interstate commerce (McClung, Heart of Atlanta, Wickard). Rehnquist cited Perez v. United States (1971) for the three-category structure *(slide 5)*. The channel/instrumentality distinction: a channel is the physical or intangible means by which commerce moves across state lines; an instrumentality is a physical vehicle or operating entity, "may be tricky to fit into Congress' commerce clause power" *(slide 6)*.

**What Was Wrong with GFSZA 90**

The statute failed on multiple dimensions *(slide 7)*: (1) not an essential part of a larger regulation of economic activity; (2) no jurisdictional element to check if this particular firearm affects interstate commerce; (3) no Congressional findings about how this affects commerce; (4) the theory of indirect effect produces a slippery slope. Breyer "has no pitons" -- "no going to pile inference upon inference" *(slide 7)*. The deck flags tension with Wickard v. Filburn's aggregation principle *(slide 7)*.

The deck notes the UH Law faculty were "literally running around the halls shocked" when Lopez came down, and poses the counterfactual: could the case have come out differently if decided after Columbine in 1999? *(slide 2)*.

**O'Connor's Accountability Theme**

O'Connor is "the political theorist" reprising Madison's Federalist 51 *(slide 8)*: "Were the Federal Government to take over the regulation of entire areas of traditional state concern, areas having nothing to do with the regulation of commercial activities, the boundaries between the spheres of federal and state authority would blur and political responsibility would become illusory. The resultant inability to hold either branch of the government answerable to the citizens is more dangerous even than devolving too much authority to the remote central power" *(slide 8)*. The deck flags the Brandeisian "experimentation theme" alongside this *(slide 8)*.

**Thomas's Structural Concurrence**

Thomas argued the New Deal interpretation of "commerce" was itself the wrong turn: "the 'wrong turn' was the Court's dramatic departure in the 1930s from a century and a half of precedent" *(slide 9)*. The deck flags Thomas's "heavy use of dictionary and Founder originalism" *(slide 9)*. Substantial effects renders other constitutional provisions superfluous and surely cannot apply to every congressional power; the aggregation principle just makes matters worse *(slide 9)*. Thomas's stopping-point hypothetical: if Congress passed an omnibus "substantially affects interstate commerce" statute "purporting to regulate every aspect of human existence, the Act apparently would be constitutional" *(slide 10)*.

**Morrison (2000): The Radical Case**

The deck calls Morrison "absolutely" radical *(slide 12)*. VAWA's civil remedy provision was at issue; the deck recites the Brzonkala allegations and the defendant's denial in detail *(slide 13)*. Rehnquist's interpretation of Lopez *(slide 15)*: noneconomic, criminal nature was central; no jurisdictional element; no congressional findings; tenuous link to interstate commerce; and "any activity related to economic productivity of individual citizens" would sweep in family law including marriage, divorce, and child custody. Rehnquist's application *(slide 16)*: still non-economic; no jurisdictional element; mere congressional findings aren't enough; here the findings were predicated on a "bogus" theory that nationwide aggregated impact of crime has substantial effects on employment, production, transit, or consumption. The deck asks: "Is this Lochnerism? Is this City of Boerneism? If it were, would that be so bad?" *(slide 16)*. The deck flags that Chief Justice Rehnquist, as head of the Judicial Conference, had expressed opposition to the VAWA provisions and "did not recuse" *(slide 16)*.

The deck also raises competing characterizations of the regulated conduct: sexual violence has "major economic consequences" (dropping out of school, costly precautions, STD transmission, pregnancy); or rape is itself economic, "providing a substitute for paid prostitution or a weapon that compels the victim to provide services to the rapist" *(slide 15)*.

**The Dissents**

Souter argued the majority was "innovating" and "it's not logical," and the deck asks whether he predicted Gonzalez v. Raich *(slide 17)*. The deck asks: "Is Morrison to the commerce clause as Cruikshank was to the 14th amendment?" *(slide 17)*. Breyer dissented on institutional-competence grounds: economic/non-economic distinction is not easy to apply; if there is no difference, "have we reached the Darby/Wickard bottom of the slope?"; and Congress can evade Morrison through "a regulatory scheme that limits it to public transport" *(slide 19)*. The deck notes both Breyer and Thomas "would make your exams simpler. But each lost. For now" *(slide 11)*.

**Aftermath and Foreign Commerce**

The VAWA civil remedy statute "is still on the books" -- 34 U.S.C. 12361 *(slide 20)*. The deck adds a note on foreign commerce *(slide 22)*: the Supreme Court "to my knowledge, NEVER" struck down a congressional enactment on grounds that it exceeds Congress's foreign-commerce power. Citing 18 USC 2423 (illicit sexual conduct abroad), the deck quotes circuit-court reasoning that the foreign commerce clause is "unencumbered by federalism limitations" and adopts an "effects test" at least as expansive as the interstate commerce test *(slide 22)*. There is "definitely a dormant foreign commerce clause" that limits states' ability to regulate international commerce *(slide 22)*. Recent controversy *(slide 23)*: United States v. Davila-Mendoza (11th Cir. 2020) vs. United States v. Barbosa-Rodriguez (D.P.R. 2023), both arising under the Maritime Drug Law Enforcement Act, with related issues under Congress's "Piracies and Felonies" and "Offences against the Law of Nations" powers -- flagged as "a good student note topic."

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

**Fixing Morrison:** The deck's "practice exam question": "Assume you dislike the outcome of Morrison. What statutes could Congress write that would most closely achieve its result without having undesirable side effects?" *(slide 18)*. Suggestions: conditional spending; better findings; addition of a jurisdictional element; violence using an instrumentality of interstate commerce (luring by cell, weapon traveling in interstate commerce); whether the answer is non-judicial -- voters should get states to take sexual violence more seriously, or pass the ERA *(slide 18)*.

**The Economic/Non-Economic Line:** Breyer's Morrison dissent argues the distinction is "not easy to apply" *(slide 19)*. Sexual violence can plausibly be characterized as providing a substitute for paid prostitution or as a weapon compelling labor, making it "economic" *(slide 15)*. If so, is Morrison just Lochner in disguise?

**Thomas and the Aggregation Principle:** Thomas's Lopez concurrence poses a stopping-point trap: "one always can draw the circle broadly enough to cover an activity that, when taken in isolation, would not have substantial effects on commerce" *(slide 10)*. If Congress passes an omnibus "substantially affects commerce" statute "purporting to regulate every aspect of human existence, the Act apparently would be constitutional" *(slide 10)* -- proving the doctrine has no stopping point.

## Key Takeaways

- Lopez restores a meaningful Commerce Clause limit for the first time since 1937, but the limit is narrow: non-economic, criminal conduct without a jurisdictional nexus element falls outside the substantial effects category.
- Morrison extends the limit: even extensive congressional findings are insufficient when the theory connecting non-economic activity to commerce requires piling inferences and would effectively federalize traditional state domains like family law.
- The three-category framework (channels, instrumentalities, substantial effects) is the operative structure, but the real line-drawing is between economic and non-economic activity under the substantial effects prong.
- Gonzalez v. Raich (2005) later cabined Lopez and Morrison: economic activity that is part of a broader regulatory scheme may be regulated even without particularized commerce nexus in each application.
- The Lopez/Morrison retrenchment is incomplete -- Garcia's political-process baseline remains; the commandeering and sovereign immunity doctrines do the heavier structural federalism work.
