## Lecture Outline

**The Constitutional Text and Core Definition**

A bill of attainder is a legislative act that singles out a named person or a sufficiently definite, readily ascertainable group for punishment without a judicial trial (slide 2). The prohibition appears in two places: Article I, Section 9, Clause 3 — "No Bill of Attainder or ex post facto Law shall be passed" — and Article I, Section 10, Clause 1 — "No State shall…pass any Bill of Attainder" (slide 2). The doctrine targets one central evil: the legislature cannot do the job of a court (slide 2).

**Historical Background**

The Framers banned bills of attainder because Parliament had wielded them as instruments of political destruction for centuries; the practice peaked during periods of rebellion, subserviency to the Crown, and violent political excitement (slide 3). Parliament assumed "judicial magistracy": it pronounced guilt, determined sufficiency of proof without evidentiary rules, and fixed punishment — with death, banishment, forfeiture of property, and corruption of blood as standard penalties (slide 3). The Earl of Kildare (Henry VIII) was attainted along with "all such persons which be or heretofore have been comforters, abettors, partakers, confederates, or adherents" — an entire class convicted of high treason by legislative act, without individual trial (slide 3). The Earl of Clarendon (Charles II) was sentenced to perpetual exile with threats of treason if he returned, escape conditioned on surrendering by a fixed date — a legislative sentence, not a judicial one (slide 3).

**The Modern Three-Part Test**

(1) Specificity (slide 4): has the legislature singled out a person or a sufficiently definite, readily ascertainable group? A closed class — a named person, members of a named organization, persons with a fixed past status, persons who performed a completed past act, or any readily identifiable closed set — is suspicious; an open, forward-looking category (all future applicants, all future union officers, all contractors generally) is much less suspicious (slide 5).

(2) Punishment (slide 4): does the law impose a burden that counts as punishment? Three sub-inquiries from Nixon (slide 6): historical punishment (does the burden resemble imprisonment, banishment, confiscation, or exclusion from employment or office?); functional punishment (is the burden excessive relative to a plausible nonpunitive purpose?); and motivational punishment (does the legislative record reflect retribution or condemnation?).

(3) No judicial trial (slide 4): did the legislature impose the burden without ordinary adjudication?

**United States v. Brown (1965) — Struck Down**

Congress made it a crime for any member of the Communist Party to serve as a labor-union officer (slide 7). The law targeted a specific named organization and imposed a traditional occupational disability (slide 7). The Court held that the Bill of Attainder Clause is "a general safeguard against legislative exercise of the judicial function" (slide 7). Congress designated Communist Party members as dangerous rather than leaving that determination to courts; the occupational disqualification was itself the "punishment" — even though Congress's purpose was preventive, not retributive (slide 7). The Court found it "unnecessary to consider the First and Fifth Amendment arguments" (slide 7).

Slide 8 contrasts Brown with United States v. Robel (1967): in Robel, the Court struck a similar law (CP members barred from defense jobs) on First Amendment grounds, with the attainder claim not reached (slide 8). The Brown dissent had warned the attainder holding "now forbids Congress to do precisely what was validated in Douds" (1950), effectively overruling precedent through a different clause (slide 8).

**Nixon v. Administrator of General Services (1977) — Upheld**

Congress passed a law dealing specifically with former President Nixon's presidential papers, directing government custody and review (slide 9). The law named one person — Nixon himself — but provided for just compensation (slide 9). The Court held that specificity alone does not make a law a bill of attainder (slide 9). The Act served legitimate nonpunitive purposes — preserving historical records and evidence for judicial proceedings — and did not inflict any traditional attainder punishment: no imprisonment, no banishment, no confiscation without compensation (slide 9). The exam-mnemonic gloss the deck offers: specificity is not enough; the key question is whether the burden counts as punishment (slide 9).

**Selective Service System v. Minnesota PIRG (1984) — Upheld**

Congress denied federal student aid to men who failed to register for the Selective Service (slide 10). The denial of a noncontractual governmental benefit is not a punishment historically associated with bills of attainder (slide 10). The law furthered nonpunitive goals — encouraging draft compliance and fairly allocating scarce resources (slide 10). Because students could register late, they "carr[ied] the keys of their prison in their own pockets" (slide 10).

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

The deck's strong-versus-weak attainder framework creates an exam template (slide 11). A strong attainder claim features a closed class defined by name, past membership, or completed past conduct, a traditional disability such as exclusion from employment, office, or benefits, and legislative hostility or evidence of retribution in the record — modeled on Brown (slide 11). A weak attainder claim features an open, forward-looking category, a regulatory condition tied to a plausible nonpunitive purpose, and no evidence of legislative retribution — modeled on Nixon and Selective Service (slide 11). The deck's final-rule slide summarizes: Brown means the legislature cannot target a disfavored group with occupational disability; Nixon means specificity alone does not equal punishment; Selective Service means a regulatory burden tied to a nonpunitive purpose is valid (slide 12).

Exam applications: a law barring all persons convicted of a crime from voting (closed class by past act; punishment?); a law barring only members of a named organization from receiving government contracts (Brown analog); a law requiring all future contractors to meet ongoing disclosure requirements (forward-looking, probably not specific enough).

## Key Takeaways

- The Bill of Attainder Clause prohibits legislative acts that single out an identifiable person or group for punishment without a judicial trial.
- The three-part test asks: specificity (closed class?), punishment (historical, functional, or motivational?), and no judicial trial.
- Brown is the paradigm case: occupational disability imposed on a named-organization membership is punishment.
- Nixon is the counterweight: specificity alone is not enough; a nonpunitive purpose insulates the law.
- Selective Service confirms that regulatory burdens tied to plausible nonpunitive goals survive, especially when the target holds the cure in their own hands.
- The doctrine is distinct from First Amendment political-association analysis; the two often run in parallel but yield different analytical paths (see Brown/Robel).
