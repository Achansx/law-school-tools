## Professor Emphasis

### From source

The deck opens with a "Scorecard" framing the pre-Lochner baseline -- a sense that state legislatures can be the source of oppression, sometimes violating the Bill of Rights and other times violating diffuse notions of liberty, with Slaughterhouse, Cruikshank, and Bradwell crippling P&I-14 and only Burlington & Quincy implicitly accepting Fifth Amendment incorporation as a nascent idea of SDP *(slide 2)*. "Other factoids" foreground laissez faire and libertarianism as natural law, a narrow conception of state action, endemic racism and sexism, and the entanglement of early SDP with gender discrimination and "protection" *(slide 3)*. Lochner itself is then framed by analogy to *Slaughter-House*: pressure for the elite judiciary to push back against "stupid" laws persists and finds a new channel *(slide 4)*. The Lochner statute is presented as a multi-prong bakers'-sanitation law with a tacked-on 60-hour maximum, and the deck asks both the good reason (better-rested workers are safer) and the bad reason (thwart harder-working immigrants) *(slide 5)*. The deck frames Lochnerism as the Court asking "is this a fair, reasonable and appropriate exercise of the police power of the state" and presses how far that is from asking whether the court thinks the statute is "a good idea" *(slide 6)*. A bullet-list slide enumerates "What exactly is wrong with Lochnerism" -- assessing the wisdom of a statute, importing one's own economic beliefs, fact-finding errors, underinclusivity strikes, hostility to line-drawing, impugning legislative motive, finding rights not explicitly in the constitution, lack of originalism *(slide 7)*. Holmes's dissent reproducing "The Fourteenth Amendment does not enact Mr. Herbert Spencer's Social Statics" and his "fundamental principles … traditions of our people and our law" gloss appear in full *(slide 10)*. Muller is presented as a labor-restriction-plus-gender-discrimination combination, with the deck flagging the Brandeis Brief and reproducing the Court's "she" passages in extended block quotes *(slides 11-15)*. Meyer and Pierce are presented as introducing a new "right of parents to direct children's education" alongside the older Lochner/Adkins "right to teach," with the deck explicitly asking what happens if one abandons textualism here *(slides 17, 19-20)*. Adkins is framed as "Beautiful Lochnerism: assessing whether this is a good rule," with Holmes's dissent again raising deference and text-mooring *(slides 24-25)*. The deck presents a "Strands" two-column scorecard separating the regulatory-power line (Slaughterhouse, Petit, Jacobson, Muller, O'Gorman, West Coast Hotel, Carolene Products) from the libertarian line (Burlington & Quincy, Lochner, Adkins, Meyer, Pierce) *(slide 26)*. Nebbia is the thaw, and the deck quotes the "no closed class or category of businesses affected with a public interest" passage and the "no more Lochnerism" deferential-policy paragraph *(slides 27-29)*. West Coast Hotel is presented as similar-to-Adkins, with the deck reproducing "The Constitution does not speak of freedom of contract" and asking whether the legal standard is really any different than Lochnerism *(slide 31)*. Carolene Products is built up to Footnote 4's three sentences -- incorporation, political process, discrete and insular minorities -- with the deck reproducing each sentence in full and then asking whether Pierce was really about religious minorities and Meyer about national minorities *(slides 36-42)*. The closing exercise asks students to define "Lochnerism" precisely and to opine on when SCOTUS should overrule prior constitutional cases *(slide 43)*. Williamson is the endpoint -- "Lochnerism, at least for economic regulation, is dead. Or is it…" *(slide 44)*.

## Lecture Outline

The deck opens with the pre-Lochner baseline -- a "Scorecard" of post-bellum cases (Slaughterhouse, Cruikshank, Bradwell) that crippled the Fourteenth Amendment's Privileges or Immunities Clause and left only Burlington & Quincy implicitly accepting incorporation under the Fifth Amendment, with a nascent idea of SDP and no move to revive P&I-14 *(slide 2)*. The deck flags laissez-faire and libertarianism-as-natural-law, a narrow state-action concept, and endemic racism and sexism -- noting how early SDP gets tangled up with gender discrimination and "protection" from the outset *(slide 3)*. The deck asks what case Lochner is closest to and answers Slaughterhouse: an arguably bad law infringing the ability to make a living, where the Court had neutered P&I-14 as a challenge vehicle, so pressure for the elite judiciary to push back against "stupid" laws found a new channel *(slide 4)*.

Lochner v New York (1905) itself: New York's Bakeshop Act was a multi-prong statute mostly devoted to baker sanitation, with a 60-hour maximum tacked on; the deck poses both a good reason (better-rested workers are safer) and a bad reason (thwart harder-working immigrants) *(slide 5)*. Peckham's majority ran into precedents upholding maximum hours in mining, smelting, and Sunday barber closures, and the deck identifies the essence of Lochnerism as the Court asking whether a statute is a "fair, reasonable and appropriate exercise of the police power" -- and how far that is from asking whether the court thinks the statute is "a good idea" *(slide 6)*. Peckham wrote that "there must be more than the mere fact of the possible existence of some small amount of unhealthiness to warrant legislative interference with liberty" and asked whether we are all "at the mercy of legislative majorities" *(slide 6)*. The deck offers a checklist of what is wrong with Lochnerism -- assessing the wisdom of a statute, importing one's own economic beliefs, looking too carefully into the facts, making bad findings, striking down for underinclusivity, hostility to line-drawing, questioning legislative judgment, impugning legislative motive, finding rights not explicitly there, lack of originalism *(slide 7)*. Justice Harlan dissented; the deck asks whether he is weighing the evidence differently or using a different test, and answers that these are not the same thing *(slide 9)*. Justice Holmes dissented more famously, writing that "the Fourteenth Amendment does not enact Mr. Herbert Spencer's Social Statics" and that "liberty" is perverted when it prevents "the natural outcome of a dominant opinion" unless it infringes "fundamental principles as they have been understood by the traditions of our people and our law" *(slide 10)*.

The deck then works through Lochnerism's full doctrinal footprint. Muller v Oregon (1908) is presented as a labor restriction combined with gender discrimination, decided by a court that had just decided Lochner, and using the Brandeis Brief and sociological "evidence" *(slide 11)*. The deck dwells on what is wrong with Muller -- the objectification and homogenization of women, the "she" language, bad science, paternalism that can lead to weakness, failure to insist on finer-tuned decision rules -- and asks whether this is much different from City of Boerne proportionality-and-congruence or from later levels-of-scrutiny review *(slide 12)*. The Court's Muller passages are reproduced verbatim: "woman's physical structure and the performance of maternal functions place her at a disadvantage which justifies a difference in legislation," and the extended "she" block running through "looking at it from the viewpoint of the effort to maintain an independent position in life, she is not upon an equality" *(slides 13-15)*.

Meyer v Nebraska (1923) is presented as a forced-assimilation post-WWI Nebraska law that mandated English-only instruction through 8th grade as a criminal offense, with an exception only for Latin, Greek, and Hebrew, and a Bible-teaching-in-German plaintiff; Bartels v. Iowa is the German-specific companion *(slide 16)*. The deck flags Meyer as introducing a new "right of parents to direct children's education" alongside the older "right to teach" from Lochner/Adkins, and asks the student to think hard about textualism's consequences either way *(slide 17)*. Pierce v Society of Sisters (1925) is presented as Oregon's compulsory-public-education law that would have closed Catholic Sisters' orphanage schools and military schools *(slide 18)*, with the rights asserted being parents', children's, and schools' (jus tertii) *(slide 19)*. The deck reproduces Pierce's "child is not the mere creature of the State" passage in full and asks how similar this is to rational-basis review in equal protection, observing that the Court was "untroubled by lack of direct textual support or disregard for 'democratic' decisions" *(slide 20)*. The deck flags contemporary complications -- "mature minor" exceptions that complicate the Pierce/Meyer model with triangular tensions between state, parents, and children's autonomy *(slide 21)*. A corporations-and-the-Constitution detour follows, noting that the Lochner-era Court indicated corporations have no "liberty" rights under the Fourteenth Amendment but that subsequent cases relax this *(slides 22-23)*.

Adkins v Children's Hospital (1923) is framed by the deck's question how the Court differentiates the minimum-wage strike-down from the maximum-hour uphold, asking whether it rests on equal protection and whether it would come out the same applied to men -- "Beautiful Lochnerism: assessing whether this is a good rule" *(slide 24)*. Holmes dissents again; the deck cautions students against embracing Holmes's views too quickly *(slide 25)*.

The deck presents a two-column "Strands" scorecard separating the regulatory-power line (Slaughterhouse 1873, Petit 1900, Jacobson 1905, Muller 1908, O'Gorman 1931, West Coast Hotel 1937, Carolene Products 1938) from the libertarian line (Burlington & Quincy 1897, Lochner 1905, Adkins 1923, Meyer 1923, Pierce 1925) *(slide 26)*.

Nebbia v New York (1934) is the 5-4 thaw -- New York fixes the price of milk; Nebbia evades it by giving free bread with milk and is charged; the Court upholds *(slide 27)*. The deck quotes the Court's extension of the "public interest" concept ("there is no closed class or category of businesses affected with a public interest") *(slide 28)* and reproduces the "no more Lochnerism" deferential paragraph ("a state is free to adopt whatever economic policy may reasonably be deemed to promote public welfare … the courts are without authority either to declare such policy, or, when it is declared by the legislature, to override it") *(slide 29)*. The dissent is presented as the "last gasp of Lochnerism in the economic sphere" *(slide 30)*.

The 1937 break: West Coast Hotel v Parrish on minimum wages for women, similar to Adkins *(slide 31)*. The deck reproduces "The Constitution does not speak of freedom of contract" and asks whether the legal standard is really any different than Lochnerism, given the still-active "reasonable relation to a proper legislative purpose / neither arbitrary nor discriminatory" formula *(slide 31)*. The deck asks how West Coast Hotel goes beyond Nebbia and answers that Nebbia opened the door by rejecting the rigid public/private distinction while West Coast Hotel went further by explicitly dismantling the Lochner framework and establishing a deferential approach *(slide 32)*. The dissent raises stare decisis and a "subversive" equal-protection argument that men need a minimum wage at least as much as women, possibly inspiring Craig v. Boren *(slides 33-34)*. A historical note flags that 14th-Amendment review of state economic regulation and Commerce Clause regulation (Darby) shifted in tandem, removing the constitutional impediments to an interventionist state *(slide 35)*.

In 1938 the Carolene Products case -- a federal "filled milk" statute -- offers Stone's "Ode to butterfat" attempt at a field theory of Lochnerism *(slide 36)*. The presumption of constitutionality is the new baseline for "regulatory legislation affecting ordinary commercial transactions" *(slide 37)*. Footnote 4 is then presented in three sentences: (1) narrower presumption when legislation appears on its face to be within a specific Bill of Rights / Fourteenth Amendment prohibition (incorporation) *(slides 38-39)*; (2) more exacting scrutiny for legislation restricting political processes that would ordinarily repeal undesirable legislation (voting, dissemination, political organization, peaceable assembly) *(slide 40)*; (3) similarly searching review for statutes directed at particular religious, national, or racial minorities, where prejudice against "discrete and insular minorities" curtails the political processes ordinarily relied upon to protect minorities *(slide 41)*. The deck flags the recasting move: was Pierce really about religious minorities? Was Meyer really about national minorities? Was McCulloch really about searching judicial inquiries? *(slide 42)*.

Williamson v Lee Optical (1955) closes the deck: Oklahoma's optical-licensing law, the Court's "legislature might have concluded …" reasoning, the rule that the law "need not be in every respect logically consistent with its aims to be constitutional," equal-protection one-step-at-a-time -- and the deck's punchline that "Lochnerism, at least for economic regulation, is dead. Or is it…" *(slide 44)*.

## Key Takeaways

- Lochner-era substantive due process embedded laissez-faire economic theory into the Fourteenth Amendment, and the Court abandoned that project in 1937 under intense political pressure.
- Carolene Products footnote 4 is the doctrinal pivot: economic regulation now gets rational-basis deference, while the tiers of scrutiny handle political-process and fundamental-rights concerns.
- The methodological machinery (unenumerated "liberty" under the Due Process Clause) survived and was redeployed in Meyer and Pierce, and then in Griswold, Roe, Lawrence, and Obergefell.
- Holmes's Lochner dissent is a touchstone for judicial-restraint arguments across political coalitions.
- Exam framing: be able to explain why Lochner is "bad" (concrete doctrinal reasons), what the modern autonomy cases owe to Lochnerism, and how Carolene Products tiered scrutiny resolves the methodological anxiety.

## Comparison: What 1937 Discarded vs What Survived (The Non-Economic Liberty Lineage)

The lecture's central framing question is whether the autonomy cases descending from Meyer and Pierce can be principled without being "Lochner for liberals." The table below separates the Lochner-era doctrinal moves that West Coast Hotel and Carolene Products retired from the doctrinal moves that quietly survived and became the methodological spine of the modern privacy and autonomy line.

| Axis | Discarded in 1937 (economic branch) | Survived in 1937 (non-economic branch) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Canonical Lochner-era case | [[Cases/Lochner v New York (1905)\|Lochner v New York (1905)]]; [[Cases/West Coast Hotel Co v Parrish (1937)\|Adkins v Children's Hospital (1923)]] overruled | [[Cases/Meyer v Nebraska (1923)\|Meyer v Nebraska (1923)]]; [[Cases/Pierce v Society of Sisters (1925)\|Pierce v Society of Sisters (1925)]] |
| Substantive liberty asserted | Freedom of contract between employer and employee; economic bargaining as a constitutional interest | Parental control over child-rearing and education; direction of the intellectual and religious upbringing of minors |
| Textual vehicle | Due Process Clause "liberty" | Same Due Process Clause "liberty" |
| Methodological move | Treat "liberty" in the Due Process Clause as a substantive constraint on state police power | Identical move, applied to non-economic family and personal choices |
| What 1937 did to the branch | [[Cases/West Coast Hotel Co v Parrish (1937)\|West Coast Hotel]] retires "freedom of contract" as a judicially enforceable right; [[Cases/United States v Carolene Products (1938)\|Carolene Products Footnote Four]] assigns rational-basis review to economic regulation | Meyer and Pierce are left untouched by West Coast Hotel and are expressly preserved; they become building blocks rather than targets |
| Post-1937 doctrinal path | Rational basis with conceivable purpose; [[Cases/Williamson v Lee Optical of Oklahoma (1955)\|Williamson v Lee Optical (1955)]] is the endpoint | Bridge cases to [[Cases/Griswold v Connecticut (1965)\|Griswold v Connecticut (1965)]]; marital-privacy penumbras; [[Cases/Roe v Wade (1973)\|Roe v Wade (1973)]]; [[Cases/Lawrence v Texas (2003)\|Lawrence v Texas (2003)]]; [[Cases/Obergefell v Hodges (2015)\|Obergefell v Hodges (2015)]] |
| Modern stare decisis status | Lochner treated as anti-canonical; no realistic prospect of revival; economic SDP effectively dead letter | Glucksberg methodology narrows but does not retire the line; [[Cases/Dobbs v Jackson Women's Health Organization (2022)\|Dobbs (2022)]] overrules Roe and Casey but expressly preserves Meyer, Pierce, Griswold, Lawrence, and Obergefell on potential-life grounds; Thomas concurrence in Dobbs urges reconsideration |
| The "Lochner for liberals" charge | Not in play; the economic branch is repudiated, so the charge has no work to do | The core methodological anxiety: if Peckham's reading of "liberty" was illegitimate, what principles distinguish the post-1937 autonomy line from Lochner's reasoning? |
| Principled distinguishing move available | Not needed | Carolene Products Footnote Four supplies the two candidate distinctions: (i) specific textual guarantees get heightened scrutiny, and (ii) discrete and insular minorities shut out of political processes get heightened scrutiny; economic interests fit neither category |

The "Lochner for liberals" charge has force only if one rejects Footnote Four's two-track framework. The Court's operational answer for eighty-odd years has been that Footnote Four supplies the principled distinction: economic regulation gets rational-basis deference because economic actors are not a discrete and insular minority and economic liberty is not textually enumerated, while the privacy and autonomy cases either involve textual penumbras (Griswold, read through the Bill of Rights) or involve groups subject to political-process exclusion (Lawrence, Obergefell). Whether that answer is load-bearing or merely rhetorical is precisely the question the lecture puts on the table.
