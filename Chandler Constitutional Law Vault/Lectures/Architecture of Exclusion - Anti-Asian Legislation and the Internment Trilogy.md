## Professor Emphasis

### From source

The deck opens with a methodological note: the primary sources are reproduced as written, because the ideology of exclusion is visible only in the original language, and the regime was a cohesive one — not isolated acts — using citizenship, property, and "whiteness" to define Asian immigrants as perpetually foreign and inassimilable (slide 2). The structural question Chandler poses for the war-powers half is explicit on the slide: "How far down the chain of delegation does constitutional authority travel?" (slide 15). The deck stresses "Prize deference" as "the most extreme form of judicial deference in American constitutional law," compared to The Prize Cases (1863) (slide 17). The Hirabayashi concurrences are flagged as "planting seeds" — Douglas's machinery-of-loyalty caveat and Murphy's "brink of constitutional power" warning (slide 19). For Korematsu, the deck enumerates the three dissents on three distinct grounds: Jackson (institutional design / "loaded weapon"), Roberts (exclusion and detention inseparable in fact), and Murphy (evidentiary insufficiency and race-not-evidence) (slides 21-22). ## Lecture Outline

### I. The Foundation: Racialized Citizenship (1790–1870)
- Naturalization Act of 1790: limited naturalization to "free white persons" of good character — created the permanent legal category of "aliens ineligible for citizenship" (slide 3)
- Naturalization Act of 1870: extended naturalization to "aliens of African nativity and descent"; Western representatives specifically lobbied to keep Asian immigrants excluded (slide 3)
- Binary established: "white" or "African"; every Asian immigrant fell into the residual category — legally present, but permanently ineligible to belong (slide 3)

### II. The Gendered Architecture: Page Act (1875) and Its Progeny
- First significant federal restrictive immigration law; ostensibly targeting cheap Chinese labor and importation of women for "immoral purposes," used primarily to bar Chinese women from entry (slide 4)
- Administrative mechanism — certified documents and intensive interrogation at ports of entry — established the precedent for using moral gatekeeping as a tool of racial exclusion (slide 4); practical effect was a reproductive control measure ensuring the Chinese population remained a transient, male-dominated "bachelor society" (slide 4)
- Gentlemen's Agreement (1907): Japan stopped issuing passports to laborers; U.S. desegregated San Francisco's "Oriental Schools"; family reunification permitted, creating the "Picture Bride" system (slide 6)
- Ladies' Agreement (1920): under U.S. pressure, Japan ceased issuing passports to picture brides, closing the last avenue for Asian family formation and leaving approximately 24,000 bachelor laborers without family prospects (slide 6)
- Cable Act of 1922: any American-born woman who married a man "ineligible for naturalization" lost her own citizenship (slide 6)

### III. The Naturalization Cases
- **Ozawa v. United States (1922)**: Sutherland held that "white person" was "synonymous with the words 'a person of the Caucasian race,'" importing a racial and not an individual test (slide 7); Ozawa, "being of the Japanese race," was "clearly of a race which is not Caucasian and therefore belongs entirely outside the zone" (slide 7); cultural assimilation was irrelevant, even though Ozawa was a Berkeley High graduate who had attended UC, educated his children in American schools, and maintained English as the language of his home (slide 7)
- **United States v. Thind (1923)**: three months later, Sutherland pivoted — "free white persons" are now "words of common speech, to be interpreted in accordance with the understanding of the common man," not scientific ethnology (slide 8); though Thind might be scientifically Caucasian, "the average man knows perfectly well that there are unmistakable and profound differences" between a "blond Scandinavian and the brown Hindu" (slide 8); science was invoked to exclude the Japanese, then discarded to exclude Indians (slide 8)

### IV. The Property War: Alien Land Laws and State Constitutional Discrimination
- Alien Land Laws used the "ineligible alien" designation as a facially neutral proxy for race; Japanese families fought back by purchasing land in the names of their American-born children, prompting further legislation to close that "loophole" (slide 9); Oregon (1859), California (1913 and 1920), Washington (1921) all enforced versions for decades (slide 9)
- State constitutional provisions: Oregon (1857) barred suffrage and real estate ownership for "Negro, Chinaman, or Mulatto"; California (1879) limited full property rights to "Foreigners of the white race or of African descent, eligible to become citizens"; Washington (1889) prohibited land ownership by aliens who had not declared intent to naturalize — a condition Asian immigrants could never satisfy (slide 10)
- Municipal harassment: over a dozen San Francisco laundry ordinances 1873-1884 including the "Pigtail Ordinance" and the Wooden Building Ordinance — permits systematically denied to Chinese applicants (slide 11); public health used as pretext for quarantine-based segregation (slide 11); racially restrictive covenants forbade occupancy by "person of Asiatic, African or Negro blood" unless employed as domestic servants (slide 11)

### V. The National Cap: Immigration Acts of 1917, 1921, 1924
- 1917 Asiatic Barred Zone (slide 12); 1921 Emergency Quota at 3% of each nationality's foreign-born population per the 1910 census (slide 12); 1924 Johnson-Reed Act dropped quotas to 2% based on the 1890 census — chosen to predate peak Asian/SE European immigration — and banned all persons "ineligible for citizenship" (slide 12)
- After Ozawa and Thind confirmed Asian ineligibility, the 1924 Act's "ineligible" provision banned virtually all Asian immigration; it also directly insulted Japan by negating the Gentlemen's Agreement, a diplomatic wound that festered for two decades (slide 12)

### VI. The Internment Trilogy: Legal Architecture
- Executive Order 9066 (Feb. 19, 1942): authorized the Secretary of War and military commanders to prescribe military areas "from which any or all persons may be excluded" — said nothing about detention, internment, or race (slide 15)
- Public Law 503 (Mar. 21, 1942): Congress made it a federal misdemeanor to knowingly violate orders issued under EO 9066, ratifying delegation without specifying what orders might be issued (slide 15)
- General DeWitt's Orders: Public Proclamation No. 3 (curfew on persons of Japanese ancestry) and Civilian Exclusion Orders directing Japanese Americans to report to assembly centers (slide 15)
- War Relocation Authority (EO 9102): the WRA's "leave procedure" conditioning release on loyalty review was an administrative creation with no specific statutory or executive authorization (slide 15)
- The deck's structural question: how far down the chain of delegation does constitutional authority travel? (slide 15)

### VII. Hirabayashi v. United States (1943) — 9-0
- Facts: Gordon Hirabayashi, an American citizen born in Seattle, deliberately violated curfew and exclusion orders and turned himself in to create a test case; convicted on both counts with sentences running concurrently (slide 18)
- Holding: the combined war powers of Congress and the President authorize a military curfew applied to a racial group; Court addressed only the curfew, avoiding the exclusion question because concurrent sentences made it unnecessary (slide 18)
- Deference standard ("Prize deference"): the Constitution gives Congress and the Executive "wide scope for the exercise of judgment and discretion" in the war power; courts will not "sit in review of the wisdom" or "substitute its judgment for theirs" so long as the political branches had "ground for believing" in the necessity (slide 17)
- Youngstown category: President acting with congressional authorization (Category One) — executive authority at its "maximum" (slide 18)
- Concurrences plant seeds: Douglas concurred narrowly because "there was no adequate time" to assess individual loyalty, but warned that "if I thought that there were no machinery whereby a citizen could prove loyalty, it would be a different question" (slide 19); Murphy recharacterized a dissent as a concurrence and warned the case went "to the very brink of constitutional power" with "few indeed have been the invasions upon essential liberties which have not been accompanied by pleas of urgent necessity advanced in good faith by responsible men" (slide 19)

### VIII. Korematsu v. United States (1944) — 6-3
- Facts: Fred Korematsu, a loyal American citizen, refused to leave his home in San Leandro; loyalty was never questioned; convicted of violating the exclusion order (slide 20)
- Holding (Black, J.): if the curfew was valid in Hirabayashi, exclusion is "a further step" along the same continuum; the military's inability to "precisely and quickly" ascertain loyalty justified the group-based measure (slide 20)
- Critical limitation: the majority explicitly declined to address detention, stating exclusion and the relocation program "pose different problems and may be governed by different principles" — the escape hatch for the same-day Endo ruling (slide 20)
- Frankfurter's concurrence: "The validity of action under the war power must be judged wholly in the context of war"; a military order expressing "an allowable judgment of war needs" is not "lawless because like action in times of peace would be lawless" (slide 20)
- Jackson's dissent (institutional design): "A military order, however unconstitutional, is not apt to last longer than the military emergency.… But once a judicial opinion rationalizes such an order to show that it conforms to the Constitution, or rather rationalizes the Constitution to show that the Constitution sanctions such an order, the Court for all time has validated the principle" (slide 21); "The principle then lies about like a loaded weapon, ready for the hand of any authority that can bring forward a plausible claim of an urgent need" (slide 21); the Court should refuse to validate the principle even if it does not enjoin the order (slide 21)
- Roberts (factual inseparability): by the time Korematsu was arrested, the only way to comply with exclusion was to report to an assembly center; this was "a case of convicting a citizen as a punishment for not submitting to imprisonment in a concentration camp" (slide 22)
- Murphy (evidentiary sufficiency): the government produced no evidence of actual espionage or sabotage by Japanese Americans, and Americans of German and Italian ancestry were not comparably treated (slide 22)

### IX. Ex Parte Endo (1944) — 9-0
- Facts: Mitsuye Endo — American citizen, English-speaking, brother in the U.S. Army, concededly loyal — filed habeas corpus challenging her continued detention; the government offered conditional release; she refused, demanding unconditional freedom (slide 23)
- Holding (Douglas, J.): "Whatever power the War Relocation Authority may have to detain other classes of citizens, it has no authority to subject citizens who are concededly loyal to its leave procedure" (slide 23); the authority to detain derives from the power to prevent espionage and sabotage and is "exhausted" once loyalty is conceded (slide 23)
- Critical feature: resolved on statutory and executive-order grounds — neither EO 9066 nor Public Law 503 authorized detention, only exclusion — through constitutional avoidance (slide 23)
- Doctrine of constitutional avoidance (slide 24): if a statute can be read in a way that avoids constitutional difficulty, courts should adopt that reading; advantages include getting more information before constitutional pronouncement and avoiding etching wartime doctrine in stone (slide 24); disadvantages include failing to deter future abuse and resting on a fiction — Roberts' point that both Congress and the President knew about and supported detention (slide 24)

### X. The Separability Principle and Repudiation
- Same-day results explained (slide 25): curfew (Hirabayashi, 9-0 upheld) → exclusion (Korematsu, 6-3 upheld) → detention (Endo, 9-0 struck down) via the separability principle, the loyalty distinction ("exhausted" once loyalty conceded), and a delegation limit on the WRA's leave procedure
- 1983: Korematsu's conviction vacated via coram nobis after discovery that the Solicitor General suppressed FBI and ONI intelligence reports contradicting the military's factual claims of necessity (slide 26); 1987: Hirabayashi's convictions overturned on the same basis (slide 26); the "Prize deference" standard had assumed an honest factual judgment — the Court deferred to a fabrication (slide 26)
- Trump v. Hawaii (2018): Roberts wrote that "Korematsu was gravely wrong the day it was decided, has been overruled in the court of history, and… 'has no place in law under the Constitution'" (slide 26); but the dissenters argued the travel ban case raised the same structural problem of deference to a potentially pretextual national-security determination (slide 26)
- The deck closes with five open war-powers questions: delegation chain, factual deference, temporal limits, judicial validation, and how subsequent courts should treat wartime precedents (slide 27)

### XI. The Architecture in Summary
Three pillars sustaining one regime (slide 29):
1. Denial of naturalization (1790–1952): "free white persons" prerequisite confirmed in Ozawa and Thind (slide 29)
2. Restriction of movement (1875–1965): Page Act through Johnson-Reed Act, engineered to prevent entry, family formation, and settlement (slide 29)
3. Prohibition of property (1859–1966): Alien Land Laws and restrictive covenants denied the tools of economic integration — then cited the resulting poverty as evidence of unfitness (slide 29)

The road to repeal was geopolitical: the Magnuson Act (1943) repealed Chinese exclusion with a quota of only 105/year; Luce-Celler (1946) extended naturalization to Filipinos and Indians at 100 visas/year each; McCarran-Walter (1952) ended the Asiatic Barred Zone but kept the "Asia-Pacific Triangle" cap; and Hart-Celler (1965) dismantled the national-origins quota system (slide 28).

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

**The 1942 Decision-Maker Hypothetical** (slide 14): You have no racist animus but believe some Japanese Americans might pose a genuine security risk. How do you prevent immediate sabotage? Impose severe restrictions on all persons in the military zone, or on some smaller class — on what basis? What review mechanism gathers further information? How quickly must individualized assessment replace group classification? The FBI and ONI both concluded that mass internment was unnecessary; Director Hoover opposed it; Naval Intelligence officer Kenneth Ringle's report (largely suppressed) recommended individual investigation; the Solicitor General failed to present these findings to the Court (slide 14).

**Jackson's Loaded Weapon**: Jackson argues courts should refuse to constitutionally validate emergency measures even if they decline to enjoin them; a military order is transient, a judicial opinion permanent (slide 21). But what is the Court saving its institutional capital for? Is there a middle ground between blessing and blocking? (slide 21)

**Endo's Constitutional Avoidance Tradeoff**: Douglas resolves Endo without reaching the constitutional question. Advantages: avoids etching wartime doctrine in stone. Disadvantages: provides no clear signal about constitutional limits, and rests on a fiction — Roberts noted Congress and the President both knew about detention (slide 24). Murphy and Roberts concur but argue the Court should have reached the constitutional question (slide 24). Which approach better serves constitutional law over time?

## Key Takeaways

1. The architecture of exclusion was a cohesive, self-reinforcing regime — federal ineligibility → state property bars → municipal harassment → immigration caps — not a collection of isolated acts.
2. Ozawa and Thind illustrate how the Court moved the goalposts: science deployed to exclude the Japanese, then discarded to exclude Indians, with the same result each time.
3. "Prize deference" in Hirabayashi is the most extreme form of judicial deference in American constitutional law — courts will not second-guess the political branches' military judgments so long as there was "ground for believing" in necessity.
4. The three Korematsu dissents attack the war power claim on three distinct grounds that remain analytically distinct: Jackson (institutional — don't validate the principle), Roberts (factual — exclusion and detention are inseparable), Murphy (evidentiary — no factual basis for military necessity).
5. Endo's constitutional avoidance shows the costs of narrow rulings: the government's factual premises were fabricated, but the statutory holding provided no constitutional deterrent against future abuse.
6. Trump v. Hawaii's formal repudiation does not answer how courts should respond to the next national-security classification that might rest on pretextual grounds.
