# Standing and Article III Justiciability

## Professor Emphasis

### From source

The deck opens by warning that standing doctrine, although it could be dismissed as a technicality, pervades contemporary constitutional law and is gamed both by drafters trying to write statutes where no one can sue and by litigants developing counter-strategies to create standing *(slide 1)*. It cautions that "it is almost impossible to get all the standing cases to line up" — Article III's "case and controversy" text is heavily burdened, justices have very different views about justiciability, and there is a "credible claim that ideological views often get entangled in standing" — "the worse the justices find the conduct, the more likely they might be to find standing" *(slide 5)*. The three-part Lujan mantra (injury-in-fact, causation, redressability) is presented with two causal requirements — one between conduct and injury, the other between relief and injury reduction *(slides 3, 14)*. The deck offers "Chandler's Translation" — distinct and palpable non-abstract injury, causation with respect to named defendant, injury not overdetermined, relief reducing injury *(slide 15)* — and Chandler's Heuristic: "If it takes you more than one sentence to explain why you have standing then you probably don't have standing" *(slide 27)*. The deck flags the structural-secrecy paradox of Clapper, the Lyons distinction between damages and injunctive relief, and the special problem of taxpayer, third-party, and legislator standing.

### Editor's framing

This page treats the lecture as a doctrinal tour anchored by the Lujan three-part test, with mootness, ripeness, and adjacent doctrines clustered around it. ## Lecture Outline

**Constitutional Foundations and Advisory Opinions**

Article III limits federal court jurisdiction to "Cases" and "Controversies" *(slide 2)*. The founding-era baseline is Washington's request to the Supreme Court for legal advice on neutrality during the Anglo-French war: Jay declined, establishing that federal courts do not give advisory opinions. This non-advisory-opinion principle underlies the entire justiciability doctrine: courts resolve live disputes, not abstract legal questions.

Standing is one of several justiciability doctrines alongside mootness, ripeness, and the political question doctrine. The deck flags that Congress can restrict standing — the default rule is the "zone of interests" Congress meant to protect — but Congress cannot grant standing in excess of what the Constitution permits *(slide 4)*. It just takes one plaintiff with standing; the rest piggyback along *(slide 4)*.

**The Three-Part Standing Test**

Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife (1992) (Scalia, J.) is the definitive statement of the constitutional minimum: (1) injury-in-fact — a legally protected interest, concrete and particularized, actual or imminent (not conjectural or hypothetical); (2) a causal connection between the injury and the challenged conduct; and (3) likely (not speculative) that a favorable decision will redress the injury *(slide 14)*.

Lujan involved federal agencies' obligation to consult on actions threatening endangered species or their habitats, including actions abroad *(slide 12)*. The Endangered Species Act's citizen-suit provision permits "any person" to sue to enjoin violations *(slide 13)*. The plaintiffs' "some day" plans to return to Sri Lanka and Egypt to see endangered animals failed the imminence requirement *(slide 16)*. The "animal nexus" theory — that people who work with animals have a particularized interest — also failed *(slide 17)*. On redressability, the Secretary of the Interior could only direct consultation; there was no guarantee that cutting off aid would help the Nile crocodile; and other funding existed *(slide 18)*. Scalia further held that Congress cannot manufacture the constitutional minimum: "Vindicating the public interest (including the public interest in government observance of the Constitution and laws) is the function of Congress and the Chief Executive" *(slide 19)*.

**Allen v. Wright (1984): Traceability and Redressability**

Allen v. Wright involved IRS policy denying tax exemption to schools that did not admit students of all races, enforced through self-certification with little follow-up checking *(slide 6)*. Parents of Black public-school children sued; the deck stresses the causal chain was too speculative: better IRS enforcement → segregated private schools lose exemptions → tuition rises → marginal white students return to public school → white parents care about public schools → funding and diversity improve → black kids are helped *(slide 7)*. "Ideological injury is not enough" (Sierra Club v. Morton; Schlesinger v. Reservists Committee) *(slide 7)*. The deck flags "hostility to statistical injury": even if the chain were true on average, proving it is hard, and the result implicates whether government must "take care" to enforce laws it dislikes *(slide 11)*. Stigmatic injury can count if the plaintiff is personally victimized, but ordinarily must be tied to concrete harm; stigmatic injury does not count if the plaintiff is merely a member of a victimized group (Heckler v. Mathews) *(slide 9)*.

**Clapper v. Amnesty International (2013): Imminence Under Surveillance**

Post-9/11 FISA permits the AG and DNI to authorize targeting non-U.S. persons abroad without probable cause that the target is a foreign power or agent and without specifying particular facilities — a significant change from prior FISA practice *(slide 21)*. Foreign citizens outside the U.S. lack standing (no "case"); criminal defendants charged based on the surveillance have both *(slide 22)*. The plaintiffs in Clapper — attorneys and human-rights investigators communicating with overseas contacts — fell in the gap *(slide 22)*. The majority held the injury was not "certainly impending" because it required a five-step chain: Government decides to target, invokes §1881a (not another method), FISC judges approve, interception succeeds, plaintiffs are parties to the intercepted communications *(slide 23)*. Avoidance costs — measures plaintiffs took to protect their communications — could not manufacture standing: "an enterprising plaintiff would be able to secure a lower standard for Article III standing simply by making an expenditure based on a nonparanoid fear" *(slide 24)*. The deck flags the Marbury right-remedy implication: this may mean no one has standing to challenge an unconstitutional action, but the Court has accepted that result before in Valley Forge and similar cases *(slide 25)*. The dissent thought the probability high enough — probabilistic injury should be enough *(slide 26)*.

**City of Los Angeles v. Lyons (1983): Prospective Injunctive Relief**

Adolph Lyons, a 24-year-old Black man, was subjected to a violent chokehold during a 1976 traffic stop for a burned-out taillight, blacked out, woke up bloody and humiliated, and was handed a citation *(slide 30)*. Lyons sued for damages and a prospective injunction against the chokehold practice; the chokehold was pursuant to departmental policy *(slide 31)*. White's 5-4 majority held the damages claim could go forward but Lyons lacked standing for injunctive relief because he could not show a likelihood of substantial and immediate irreparable injury to himself — he would have to show he would again be stopped and choked *(slide 31)*. "Lyons is no more entitled to an injunction than any other citizen of Los Angeles; and a federal court may not entertain a claim by any or all citizens who no more than assert that certain practices of law enforcement officers are unconstitutional" *(slide 31)*. The deck flags associational-standing aggregation as an unresolved workaround: if each member has a 1% probability of injury and there are 200 members, the probability no one is hurt is ~14%; UAW v. Brock suggests the association may have standing, other cases ("coterminous" cases) suggest not *(slide 32)*.

**Mootness**

The deck offers the prisoner-transfer hypothetical: prisoner moved without a hearing, sues for a hearing, the prison apologizes and moves him back — the case is moot because no injunction would accomplish anything *(slide 37)*. DeFunis v. Odegaard (1974) is presented as an early affirmative-action case where the petitioner became a 3L by the time SCOTUS could rule, leaving no relief available *(slide 38)*. Three major wrinkles: capable of repetition yet evading review (think one-year college programs, abortion cases); voluntary cessation (the defendant who changes policy each time it is sued); and state-court justiciability *(slide 39)*.

Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (2000) sets the voluntary-cessation standard: a case might become moot if subsequent events make it absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur, and the heavy burden lies with the party asserting mootness *(slide 40)*. The Laidlaw facility had been closed and the company had substantially complied with environmental law eight years earlier, but recurrence was possible enough to keep the case live *(slide 40)*.

New York State Rifle and Pistol Association v. City of New York (2020) raised voluntary cessation in a different posture: NYC banned firearms owners from transporting guns to a second home or shooting range outside the city, the suit went up, and after cert was granted New York amended its laws to remove the offending provision *(slide 41)*. The per curiam held the constitutional claim moot but remanded for consideration of whether damages remained. Alito dissented on mootness (joined by Gorsuch, and by Thomas in part), arguing the City could reinstate the regulations; Kavanaugh joined the per curiam majority on mootness but wrote separately to flag concerns about lower-court application of Heller and McDonald.<!-- corrected 2026-05-26: was "Kavanaugh and Alito dissented on mootness"; in NYSRPA (2020) Kavanaugh concurred with the per curiam — Alito (joined by Gorsuch, and by Thomas in part) was the principal mootness dissenter -->

**Ripeness**

Abbott Laboratories v. Gardner (1967) established the two-part ripeness test: (1) fitness of the issues for judicial decision (are the issues legal, concrete, and not dependent on future contingencies?); (2) hardship to the parties of withholding court consideration *(slide 46)*. The deck flags that ripeness may just be a corollary of standing, with open questions about "prudential ripeness" *(slide 46)*.

**Other Standing Doctrines**

Taxpayer standing is almost never recognized: not for gifts of property to religious organizations (Valley Forge, 1981); not for tax credits as opposed to direct expenditures (Christian School Tuition Org. v. Winn, 2011); not for non-earmarked general appropriations the executive funnels to religious organizations (Hein, 2007) *(slide 33)*. Flast v. Cohen (1968) remains good law on its facts but the deck doubts today's Court would sustain it *(slide 33)*. Quasi-generalized-grievance cases like FEC v. Akins (Breyer: widely shared informational injury) and Vermont Agency of Natural Resources (qui tam as assignment of the government's claim) survive *(slide 34)*. Uzuegbunam v. Preczewski (2021) held nominal damages of $1 can sustain a case and controversy *(slide 35)*.

Third-party standing is generally disfavored but recognized in close-relationship cases (beer vendor for gender-based restrictions; doctor for abortion patient — though "maybe not" for abortion pills), organizational standing for member injuries related to organizational purpose, and qui tam as assignment *(slide 43)*.

Legislator standing: legislators generally cannot sue to challenge federal laws they think unconstitutional; legislators could not bring the line-item-veto challenge on separation-of-powers grounds; but a victim of cut funds can sue and win, legislators can intervene to defend laws the executive will not enforce, and legislatures cannot themselves appeal decisions that they gerrymandered districts — subject to "the complete nullification exception" *(slide 45)*. Coleman v. Miller (1939) and Powell v. McCormack (1969) are the older anchoring cases *(slide 50)*.

State-court justiciability: the case-or-controversy requirement applies only to federal courts; states can issue advisory opinions or relax federal standing; ASARCO v. Kadish (1989) governs when SCOTUS can review a state-court decision premised on a non-justiciable federal claim *(slide 42)*.

The deck closes its "elevator pitch" tour with Allen v. Wright on causation and statistical proof, Lujan on imminence, Massachusetts v. EPA on heightened state-standing claims, and Clapper on avoidance costs — and notes that some constitutional provisions are extremely difficult to enforce due to standing limits *(slide 51)*.

**Relationship to Political Question Doctrine**

Baker v. Carr distinguished standing from political question doctrine: standing asks whether the plaintiff is the right party to invoke the court's jurisdiction, while political question asks whether the subject matter is committed to another branch. Both serve as gatekeeping mechanisms. NFIB v. Sebelius implicated standing because the Anti-Injunction Act argument threatened to deprive the Court of jurisdiction over the penalty challenge, which Roberts resolved before addressing the merits.

The deck notes the structural shift: standing may once have been "only a prudential limitation" but now has morphed into a constitutional limitation as well, functionally giving the Court leeway to avoid decisions where political heat might be too great or where one wing fears losing on the merits *(slide 57)*. Lexmark Int'l v. Static Control Components recasts prudential limitations as questions of statutory interpretation, while moving the generalized-grievance bar into the constitutional/mandatory category *(slide 58)*.

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

The deck offers Karen the lawyer hypothetical: a lawyer in a neighborhood where deportable persons "hang out" was once assaulted, believes deportable persons commit crimes of violence at higher rates, carries a weapon, pays for training, and is criticized at work — can she sue ICE for failure to enforce deportation laws? What about the Association of Women Attorneys suing on similar facts? *(slide 29)*. The intended pedagogical pull is to test Allen v. Wright's causation logic against an emotionally distinct enforcement-of-criminal-law scenario.

The lecture closes with implicit hypotheticals testing application of the standing requirements:

(1) A citizen alleges that the President is violating the Emoluments Clause. Does the citizen have standing? (No: no particularized injury distinguishable from general law enforcement interest shared by all citizens; the deck notes "perhaps a competing hotel chain could sue for competitive injury" but the Court mooted the case after Biden was elected) *(slide 8)*.

(2) A hospital that receives federal funding challenges a federal regulation requiring hospitals to perform abortions in emergencies. Does the hospital have standing to raise the rights of doctors who conscientiously object? (Third-party standing: possibly yes under the close-relationship exception, but the hospital has its own independent regulatory burden creating direct injury.)

(3) A regulated entity challenges a rule before any enforcement action. Ripe? (Abbott Labs analysis: is there immediate compliance hardship, and are the issues fit for judicial resolution without more facts? *(slide 46)*)

(4) A surveillance target learns from a leak that their communications were intercepted. Does that change the standing analysis from Clapper? (Possibly yes: now the injury is concrete and proven rather than merely feared, but sovereign immunity and state secrets may be the next barrier.)

(5) A state official challenges a federal spending condition after accepting the funds. Standing to raise the coercion argument? (NFIB-style: the state must show the condition is genuinely coercive and that it had no real choice.)

(6) A member of Congress challenges the President's use of emergency powers without a congressional declaration. Standing? (Raines v. Byrd suggests individual legislators lack standing for institutional injuries *(slide 45)*; the House or Senate as an institution may have standing in some circumstances.)

## Key Takeaways

- The constitutional minimum standing requirements (injury-in-fact, causation, redressability) cannot be lowered by Congress; they enforce Article III's limits on federal court jurisdiction.
- Lujan's "imminence" requirement and its skepticism of congressional standing-creation reflect a methodological choice to restrict the use of standing as a vehicle for policy reform.
- Lyons illustrates the structural-reform problem: past injury from a systemic practice does not establish standing for prospective injunctive relief absent evidence of imminent personal future harm.
- Clapper illustrates the security-secrecy paradox: surveillance programs that most implicate civil liberties may be structurally immune from standing challenges because the injury cannot be proven without access to classified information.
- Voluntary cessation does not moot a case unless there is no reasonable prospect the challenged conduct will resume; courts scrutinize strategic mooting.
- Ripeness doctrine uses a two-part inquiry (fitness and hardship) to determine whether to reach constitutional questions before the full factual record develops.
