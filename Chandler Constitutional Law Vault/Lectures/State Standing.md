# State Standing

## Professor Emphasis

### From source

The deck opens by labeling Lyons "the Lyons wrinkle" and using the Karen-the-lawyer hypothetical to test individual-plaintiff standing before extending to states *(slides 3-4)*. Massachusetts v. EPA is presented as the special-solicitude case for states — states have "quasi-sovereign" interests, may find themselves without the full range of sovereign powers when federal action or inaction is involved, and accordingly receive a more lenient standing threshold *(slide 10)*. ## Lecture Outline

**The Lyons Wrinkle -- Injunctive Standing**

The deck opens with the Karen-the-lawyer hypothetical: a lawyer in a neighborhood near deportable persons who fears assault, carries a weapon at her own expense, and believes more aggressive immigration enforcement would reduce her burden — can she sue ICE for failure to enforce? What if the Association of Women Attorneys were suing? *(slide 4)*.

Los Angeles v. Lyons (1983) is the foil. Adolph Lyons, a 24-year-old Black man, was forcefully detained by LAPD officers during a 1976 traffic stop for a burned-out taillight; despite complying, he was subjected to a violent chokehold, blacked out, and woke up bloody and humiliated before being handed a citation *(slide 5)*. Lyons sued both for damages and injunctive relief against the chokehold practice, which was pursuant to departmental policy *(slide 6)*. White's 5-4 majority allowed the damages claim to go forward but denied standing for the injunction: Lyons would need to show a likelihood of "substantial and immediate irreparable injury TO HIM" specifically *(slide 6)*. "Lyons is no more entitled to an injunction than any other citizen of Los Angeles; and a federal court may not entertain a claim by any or all citizens who no more than assert that certain practices of law enforcement officers are unconstitutional" *(slide 6)*.

The associational-standing limit: an association needs members, at least one member facing potential injury, interests relating to the organization's purpose, and relief that does not require individuals to participate *(slide 7)*. But what about probabilistic aggregation — if each of 200 members has a 1% probability of harm, the probability no one is hurt is about 14%? *(slide 7)*. UAW v. Brock (1986) suggests the association may have standing, but other cases impose a "coterminous" requirement *(slide 7)*.

**Taxpayer Standing and Generalized Grievances**

Federal taxpayer standing is almost never recognized: not for gifts of property to religious organizations (Valley Forge, 1981); not for tax credits as opposed to direct expenditures (Christian School Tuition Org. v. Winn, 2011); not for non-earmarked general appropriations the executive funnels to religious groups (Hein, 2007); not for failure to keep proper accounts (Richardson, 1974); not for reservist-Congressman cases (Schlesinger, 1974) *(slide 8)*. Flast v. Cohen (1968) found taxpayer standing for a federal program buying secular textbooks for religious schools, but the deck flags it as good law limited to its facts that today's Court is unlikely to sustain *(slide 8)*.

**Informational Injuries and Qui Tam**

FEC v. Akins is the principal exception: when the FEC declined to force AIPAC to list itself as a political committee (which would have subjected it to more reporting), offended folks sued, and Breyer found standing based on a widely shared "informational injury" *(slide 9)*. Qui tam suits, recognized for over 100 years and approved in Vermont Agency of Natural Resources v. United States, permit private plaintiffs to sue on the government's behalf for a share of the recovery; Scalia approved this as essentially an assignment of the government's claim to the relator *(slide 9)*.

**Massachusetts v. EPA -- Special Solicitude for States**

Massachusetts sued the EPA for refusing to list CO2 as a pollutant, arguing rising sea levels were taking over state-owned seashore *(slide 10)*. The Court emphasized that states have "quasi-sovereign" interests — protecting their natural resources and the well-being of their citizens — and when these interests are threatened, states may find themselves without the full range of sovereign powers to address the harm, especially when federal action or inaction is involved *(slide 10)*. The Court afforded states a more lenient standing threshold, recognizing their diminished capacity to safeguard their interests compared to fully sovereign entities *(slide 10)*. This "special solicitude" ensures that states can seek judicial redress when federal policies or omissions adversely affect their quasi-sovereign interests *(slide 10)*.

**Recent State Standing Cases -- The Cutting Edge**

The deck groups three recent decisions as the cutting edge *(slide 11)*: Biden v. Nebraska, California v. Texas, and United States v. Texas.

Biden v. Nebraska (2023): can a state assert injury based on injury to an instrumentality that does not itself want to sue? *(slide 11)*. The deck also flags this as an opportunity to address and apply the Major Questions Doctrine *(slide 11)*. Roberts's majority treated MOHELA as a Missouri instrumentality — created by, supervised by, and serving the public purposes of the state — such that harm to MOHELA was a direct injury to Missouri. Kagan dissented: MOHELA is a legally and financially separate entity, Missouri's revenues do not depend on MOHELA's fees, and MOHELA actively distanced itself from the litigation; the majority "blows through a constitutional guardrail."

California v. Texas (2021): the deck divides this into the "easy part" (no standing just because plaintiffs are offended) and the harder standing-through-inseverability theory *(slide 11)*. The deck asks why two states are suing each other (or whether they really are), what the alleged injuries to the individuals are, what Texas's injury is from the §5000A modification versus the ACA itself, what Alito's Seila Law example is doing, whether §5000A is really inseverable, "what happened to special solicitude," and the forum-shopping and nationwide-injunction backdrop *(slide 12)*. After Congress zeroed out the ACA individual mandate, Texas argued the zero-penalty mandate was still unconstitutional and the entire ACA was inseverable, but the Court dismissed for lack of standing; special solicitude was not invoked to relax the requirements.

United States v. Texas (2023): Texas challenged the Mayorkas enforcement-priority memo as violating mandatory detention statutes *(slide 13)*. The deck asks what exactly Texas says Secretary Mayorkas was doing wrong, what limits could be applied while retaining concrete/particular injury, "how really is this case any different from Massachusetts v. EPA," whether blue states could sue for "overenforcement" because they lose sales-tax revenue from deportees, the importance of tradition in cognizing injury, whether this is constitutional or statutory and why that matters, and what the remedy for underenforcement could even be *(slide 13)*. Kavanaugh's 8-1 majority held the states lacked standing; financial costs must be "legally and judicially cognizable" and rooted in a tradition "capable of resolution through the judicial process"; there is no historical tradition of courts ordering the Executive Branch to make more arrests; ordering such relief would intrude on a core Article II function.

The Kavanaugh majority left four caveats: selective prosecution, congressional authorization, total abdication (wholesale abandonment of statutory responsibilities), and benefits vs. enforcement (challenges to affirmative grants of status are distinguishable from challenges to non-enforcement). The deck explicitly frames "wholesale abandonment" as the exception the Court recognized *(slide 13)*.

The deck asks what Roberts and Gorsuch are really disagreeing about, what has happened to special solicitude, and runs Alito's dissent: "The majority distorts standing doctrine to shield the Executive from judicial review"; "The decision empowers the President to ignore statutory mandates without accountability"; "The ruling weakens federalism by preventing states from protecting their interests"; "The Constitution does not allow the President to refuse to enforce laws passed by Congress" *(slide 14)*. The deck closes by asking how this transfers to President Trump's apparent refusal to spend money Congress has appropriated, and who will have standing to challenge that *(slide 14)*.

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

The lecture poses an associational-standing puzzle: if the probability of harm to any given member is 1 in 100 but the organization has 200 members so there is roughly an 86% probability that at least someone will be harmed, does the organization have standing? UAW v. Brock (1986) suggests yes under some conditions, but later cases impose a "coterminous" requirement that the injured member must be identified *(slide 7)*.

On state standing, the deck asks: could blue states sue the Trump administration for "overenforcement" of immigration law on the theory that lost sales-tax revenue from deported residents is a concrete injury? *(slide 13)*. How really is that different from Massachusetts v. EPA? *(slide 13)*. The answer likely turns on the tradition-of-judicial-cognizance factor from United States v. Texas and whether reduced enforcement by one administration creates a symmetric standing claim for increased enforcement by another. As this page reads the framework, it is party-neutral: any President can deprioritize enforcement of any statute, and states injured by non-enforcement will face the same courthouse door.

The deck also asks how the United States v. Texas standing framework will transfer when courts encounter President Trump's apparent refusal to spend money Congress has appropriated, and who will have standing to challenge that *(slide 14)*.

## Key Takeaways

- State standing is not a different doctrine but an application of the standard injury-in-fact, traceability, and redressability requirements with a "special solicitude" gloss for quasi-sovereign interests.
- Massachusetts v. EPA's special solicitude principle is contested: United States v. Texas (2023) effectively set it aside in the enforcement-discretion context.
- The Lyons principle that damages and injunctive standing are separate inquiries applies to states as much as individuals: concrete past injury does not automatically confer standing for prospective relief.
- The MOHELA instrumentality theory in Biden v. Nebraska opens a new avenue for state standing through state-created government corporations, but Kagan's dissent flags a serious objection about corporate separateness.
- Kavanaugh's four caveats in United States v. Texas are the practical guide for future state standing arguments against executive non-enforcement.
