# Gibbons v Ogden - Commerce Clause Foundations

## Professor Emphasis

### From source

The deck opens with the big-picture questions: who controls the economy, on what principles, to what extent does the word "commerce" (as opposed to broader terms) circumscribe Congress's power -- by dictionary approach or functionalist approach -- and how does constitutional language regarding state power affect the outcome (slide 2). What makes this case tricky: it is both a commerce clause case and a dormant commerce clause case and a preemption case, and one of the most cited cases ever (slide 3). The deck's framework for state power: states can generally do what they want; state power can be defeated by express congressional action, by implication (because Congress can't react to every protectionist law), by individual-liberty provisions like the Privileges & Immunities clause and Article I section 9, by express preemption, or by the dormant commerce clause (slide 4). Marshall's federalism line: federal action reaches "all the external concerns of the nation" and internal concerns that affect the States generally, but the "completely internal commerce of a State, then, may be considered as reserved for the State itself" -- a very functionalist approach with a very broad dictionary definition; "see if this line-drawing survives until the present" (slide 11). The plenary-within-the-grant reading: the power to regulate "is complete in itself, may be exercised to its utmost extent, and acknowledges no limitations other than are prescribed in the Constitution" (slide 12). The political-process safeguard: "the wisdom and the discretion of Congress, their identity with the people, and the influence which their constituents possess at elections" are "the sole restraints" -- states will protect against federal aggrandizement; the deck flags this as Justice Stevens's favorite theme (slide 13).

## Lecture Outline

The lecture begins with the political and economic stakes. The deck frames the facts as states offering innovation rewards -- "if you create a steamboat that can do X, we will reward you by letting you have monopoly profits for 30 years," with huge money in nationwide monopolies but huge costs to passengers (slide 5). Most states did not grant; New York and the Orleans territory did (slide 5). Gibbons had at first joined forces with Ogden's monopoly but they had a falling out -- Ogden had represented Gibbons' wife in the Gibbons divorce proceeding! (slide 5). Federal control over interstate travel was paramount because the US wanted to build canals and roads (slide 5). The tension: New York's monopoly is "crony capitalism," Congress had enacted an "act for enrolling or licensing ships or vessels to be employed in the coasting trade and fisheries, and for regulating the same," and the Bellona (Gibbons' ship) had such a license used to ferry passengers between New York and New Jersey (no tunnels or bridges at the time), so the "coasting trade" probably includes Hudson ferries (slide 6).

The constitutional text is the grant: "Congress shall have Power… To regulate Commerce with foreign Nations, and among the several States, and with the Indian Tribes" (slide 8). The case turns on three questions: what counts as commerce, what counts as among the several states, and what it means for Congress to regulate that commerce.

On the definition of commerce, Marshall rejects the narrow buying-and-selling view; commerce is intercourse, including navigation, and the definition is functional and broad. This functional definition ties the lecture to the later expansion in Wickard v Filburn (1942) and the limit sought in United States v Lopez (1995).

On the among-the-states limit, Marshall draws a boundary preserving federalism: federal action applies to "all the external concerns of the nation" and to those internal concerns affecting the States generally, but "the completely internal commerce of a State, then, may be considered as reserved for the State itself" (slide 11). This is the internal-external boundary that later cases will contest.

On the meaning of regulation, Marshall adopts a plenary reading: "It is the power to regulate, that is, to prescribe the rule by which commerce is to be governed. This power, like all others vested in Congress, is complete in itself, may be exercised to its utmost extent, and acknowledges no limitations other than are prescribed in the Constitution" (slide 12). The principal political restraint is "the wisdom and the discretion of Congress, their identity with the people, and the influence which their constituents possess at elections" (slide 13).

Marshall addresses the structural counterargument: if states can tax, why can't they regulate? Taxation is different from regulation because it is "the simple operation of taking small portions from a perpetually accumulating mass, susceptible of almost infinite division," and one power's exercise is not, "in its nature," incompatible with another's (slide 14). On Article I section 10, the limitations on state duties on imports and exports are limitations on the taxing power, not on an otherwise existing power to regulate commerce (slide 15). On inspection laws: they act upon the subject "before it becomes an article of foreign commerce or of commerce among the States," and along with quarantine laws, health laws, and laws regulating internal commerce, turnpikes, and ferries, they are part of the immense mass of legislation reserved to the States (slide 16). The deck closes with the open question: does this mean Congress cannot regulate pre-"commerce" manufacturing (slide 16)?

On preemption, Marshall finds that the federal coasting license authorizes Gibbons's steamboats to operate on navigable waters of the United States and that New York's monopoly grant, to the extent it interferes with this federal authorization, must yield under the Supremacy Clause. Gibbons wins; Ogden's injunction is dissolved.

This page traces the forward projection: Gibbons is the source of the functional definition of commerce that Wickard v Filburn (1942) will later push to its aggregation-of-effects logic, and it is the source of the among-the-states limit that Lopez (1995) and NFIB v Sebelius (2012) will revive as a constitutional check on modern Commerce Clause legislation.

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

**Hypothetical 1 (Same side as Gibbons): Intrastate feeder to interstate canal.** A state grants an exclusive license to a steamboat operator running a purely intrastate route that connects at one end to an interstate canal carrying cargo to and from New York. A competitor with a federal coasting license wishes to run the same route. Reasoning: the continuous-current theory Gibbons endorses treats the intrastate segment as part of the larger interstate flow. The steamboat is an instrument of interstate commerce because the cargo it carries passes through it on the way between states. The federal coasting license preempts the state grant under the Supremacy Clause. This hypo applies Marshall's functional definition and his among-the-states limit read together: internal becomes external when the activity is integrated into an interstate chain.

**Hypothetical 2 (Opposite side of Gibbons): Purely intrastate trucking with no interstate link.** A state licenses a trucking company that operates exclusively between two cities within one state, hauling goods that are produced, sold, and consumed entirely within the state and never enter the interstate market. Congress attempts to regulate the company under the Commerce Clause. Reasoning: Marshall's among-the-states limit preserves commerce completely internal to a State, which does not affect other States, for the State to regulate. If the activity genuinely lacks any interstate dimension, the Commerce Clause alone does not reach it, though modern doctrine since Wickard v Filburn (1942) has dramatically expanded what counts as affecting interstate commerce. Under Gibbons's stated limit, this hypo falls outside congressional reach; under later aggregation doctrine, the answer depends on whether the activity is part of a class that in the aggregate affects interstate commerce.

**Hypothetical 3 (Fence-sitter): Borderline internal-external boundary.** A small business within one state sells goods only to in-state customers but purchases all its raw materials from out-of-state suppliers. Congress enacts a labor-law regulation reaching the business. Reasoning: this is the fact pattern that made Gibbons's limit so difficult to administer. Marshall's functional definition of commerce as intercourse including navigation points in favor of inclusion because the business is integrated into interstate supply chains. But his reservation of purely internal commerce points the other way because the business's final sales are intrastate. The hypo surfaces the tension that later doctrine had to resolve and previews Wickard's move to aggregation as a way to collapse the ambiguity in favor of federal reach. Students should see that Gibbons provides both the broad-inclusion premise and the internal-external limit, and that the doctrinal arc from Gibbons through Wickard to Lopez to NFIB is a continuing argument over where to draw this line.

**Hypothetical 4 (Same side, doctrinal projection to NFIB's activity-inactivity line): Federal mandate to enter commerce.** Congress orders every adult to purchase at least one American-manufactured vehicle every ten years, relying on Gibbons's broad reading of the Commerce Clause. Reasoning: even under Marshall's generous functional definition, the power to regulate presumes the existence of commerce to be regulated. Gibbons speaks to existing commercial intercourse; it does not contemplate compelling commerce into being. This is the premise NFIB v Sebelius (2012) makes explicit: the Commerce Clause regulates activity, not inactivity. The hypo shows students that Marshall's functionalism is broad but not unlimited, and that the modern activity-inactivity line in NFIB is consistent with (not a departure from) Gibbons's underlying architecture.

## Key Takeaways

- Commerce is intercourse in all its branches, including navigation. The functional definition rejects the narrow buying-and-selling reading and anchors every later Commerce Clause case.
- Among the several states means interstate commerce. Commerce completely internal to a State is reserved; commerce that crosses state lines or forms a continuous current with interstate trade is within Congress's reach.
- The power to regulate is plenary within the constitutional grant. It may be exercised to its utmost extent subject only to express constitutional limits and political accountability.
- A validly enacted federal license preempts a conflicting state grant under the Supremacy Clause. Gibbons is the original federalism-meets-preemption case.
- Gibbons is the Commerce Clause companion to McCulloch: both opinions read federal power broadly and state interference narrowly, and together they define the Marshall Court's nationalist constitutional project.

**Doctrinal placement.** Gibbons is the Commerce Clause's foundational opinion and the Marshall Court companion to McCulloch. Marshall does two interpretive moves simultaneously: he reads the substantive power broadly (commerce as intercourse, reaching navigation and the instruments of commercial exchange), and he keeps the among-the-states limit meaningful by reserving commerce completely internal to a State for state regulation. The doctrinal arc from Gibbons to the modern Commerce Clause runs through Wickard v Filburn's aggregation of effects (pushing Marshall's functional definition to cover intrastate activity substantially affecting interstate commerce), through United States v Lopez (1995) and United States v Morrison (2000) (reviving the among-the-states limit against non-commercial intrastate activity), through Gonzales v Raich (2005) (narrowing Lopez when regulated activity is part of a comprehensive federal scheme), and through NFIB v Sebelius (2012) (clarifying that regulation presumes existing activity). Every one of these cases is arguing over the same two ideas Marshall articulated in 1824: what counts as commerce, and what counts as among the states. Treat Gibbons as the source code of the entire Commerce Clause line.
