## Professor Emphasis

### From source

The deck frames the case as the Supreme Court's first treatment of presidential immunity in criminal prosecutions, with the central tension between accountability and executive authority (slide 2). The majority's tripartite framework -- absolute immunity for core constitutional functions, presumptive immunity for other official acts, and no immunity for unofficial acts -- is the lecture's doctrinal spine (slide 4). ## Lecture Outline

### Background and Procedural Context

The case arose from Special Counsel Jack Smith's indictment of former President Trump for alleged efforts to overturn the results of the 2020 presidential election (slide 3). The charges included pressuring the Department of Justice to declare the election corrupt, pressuring state officials to change certified results, and pressuring Vice President Pence to reject or delay certification of electoral votes (slide 3). Lower courts -- the district court and the D.C. Circuit -- both rejected Trump's immunity claims, and the Supreme Court granted certiorari (slide 3). The decision issued in June 2024, reversing and remanding.

### The Three-Tier Framework (Roberts, J.)

The majority (Roberts, J.) established a tripartite framework for presidential immunity from criminal prosecution (slide 4):

**Tier 1: Absolute Immunity -- Core Constitutional Functions.** A President has absolute immunity from criminal prosecution for acts taken pursuant to his core constitutional functions (slide 4). These include the pardon power, the commander-in-chief function, and other powers vested directly in the President by Article II. This immunity is categorical and cannot be rebutted regardless of the prosecution's asserted interest.

**Tier 2: Presumptive Immunity -- Other Official Acts.** For acts that are official but not core constitutional functions, the President enjoys presumptive immunity, rebuttable only if prosecution would pose no threat to executive functions (slide 4). Courts cannot consider motive when classifying acts as official, and official acts cannot be used as evidence in trials for unofficial acts (slide 4).

**Tier 3: No Immunity -- Unofficial Acts.** A President has no immunity for purely private or unofficial acts (slide 4). Unofficial conduct is subject to criminal prosecution the same as any private citizen's conduct.

### Concurring and Dissenting Opinions

**Justice Thomas (concurring):** Questioned the constitutional basis for the Special Counsel's appointment, suggesting Jack Smith's appointment may have been unlawful (slide 5). Thomas wrote separately to flag this structural issue even while joining the majority on the immunity question.

**Justice Barrett (concurring in part):** Expressed concern about the breadth of the application of presumptive immunity (slide 5). On this page's reading, that concern targets the majority's evidentiary rule -- the holding that official acts cannot be introduced as evidence in prosecutions for unofficial conduct.

**Justice Sotomayor (dissenting, joined by Kagan and Jackson):** The decision places the President above the law (slide 5). Sotomayor distinguished Nixon v. Fitzgerald (civil damages) from criminal prosecution, which is the government enforcing the law on behalf of the public; her dissent also drew on the Speech or Debate Clause asymmetry, the Impeachment Judgment Clause, and Federalist 69 and 77 as textual and historical sources -- elaborations this page provides beyond the deck.

**Justice Jackson (dissenting):** Warned the ruling could shield hypothetical crimes including ordering assassinations or instigating coups if those acts could be characterized as "official" (slide 5). Jackson viewed the majority's framework as unworkable and the bright-line rule as dangerous.

### Key Precedents Applied

**United States v. Nixon (1974):** The Court rejected absolute executive privilege (slide 6). The majority used this holding as a springboard: if a mere subpoena triggers only a presumptive privilege, then criminal prosecution -- a far greater intrusion on the executive -- logically warrants at least as strong protection.

**Nixon v. Fitzgerald (1982):** Established absolute civil immunity for official acts; the Trump majority extended that logic to criminal cases (slide 6). The editorial gloss -- that the greater deterrent of criminal prosecution should warrant at least equivalent protection to the lesser deterrent of civil damages -- is this page's reconstruction of the majority's reasoning, not language drawn from the deck.

**Clinton v. Jones (1997):** The Court held that a sitting President has no civil immunity for unofficial acts; the dissent in that case saw this as reinforcing accountability (slide 6). The Trump majority read Clinton v. Jones as confirming the unofficial-act category has no immunity, while extending Fitzgerald to criminal prosecutions for official acts.

### Open Questions

The majority deliberately left several issues unresolved (slide 7):

- Defining official versus unofficial acts remains unclear (slide 7).
- How difficult will it be to rebut presumptive immunity for other official acts (slide 7)?
- Does the ruling create a loophole for misconduct (slide 7)?
- Future litigation will shape the standard further (slide 7).

On this page's reading, the evidentiary rule's effect on practical accountability -- whether official-act evidence is needed to prove motive or context in unofficial-conduct trials -- is the most consequential of these open questions, a sharpening that goes beyond the deck's open-ended bullets.

### Post-Decision Aftermath

Trump's election killed off the Florida and DC prosecutions, and Smith resigned (slide 8). The New York hush-money case found Trump's actions were unofficial (slide 8). The Georgia RICO case may require a future immunity determination (slide 8).

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

The defining exam tension: given a set of presidential actions, classify each on the three-tier scale. The hardest cases are in Tier 2 -- where the act involves the exercise of power associated with the presidency but is not squarely a core constitutional function. Candidates for future litigation: ordering the DOJ to open or close investigations; directing military action; communicating with foreign governments. Whether courts can look at motive in the Tier 2 analysis is expressly foreclosed by the majority.

## Key Takeaways

- Trump v. United States (2024) established for the first time that former Presidents have immunity from criminal prosecution for official acts.
- The three tiers -- absolute (core), presumptive (other official), none (unofficial) -- track the civil immunity doctrines from Fitzgerald and Clinton v. Jones, extended to the criminal context.
- The evidentiary rule (no official-act evidence in unofficial-conduct prosecutions) may be the most consequential long-run holding.
- Line-drawing between "core" and "other official" acts is left entirely to lower courts.
- Sharp dissents argue the decision places the President above the law and creates structural accountability gaps the Founders did not envision.
