### Editor's framing

This page treats the deck as essentially a table of contents that Chandler uses to orient students to the doctrinal arc of the course. The original stub's outline of nine numbered eras (Pre-constitutional baseline → Antebellum constitutional crises → Reconstruction → Lochner/New Deal → Warren Court → Rehnquist/Roberts) was a reasonable scholarly reconstruction but went beyond what the deck itself specifies. The actual deck collapses these into broader divisions: Formation Period, Civil War and Reconstruction, Participation Enlargement, the "switch in time" through Warren, Warren, Burger/Rehnquist, and Roberts. Because the deck supplies almost no in-slide prose, this page stays short by design — fabricating timeline content not present in the source would violate the convention.

### The "Lurking" Equal Rights Amendment

The only substantive content slide in the deck is a treatment of the Equal Rights Amendment, framed as the "lurking" 28th amendment (slide 6). The deck reproduces the 1972 proposal language, in which Congress (with two-thirds of each House concurring) proposed the article for ratification by three-fourths of the state legislatures within seven years (slide 6). The proposed text:

- **Section 1.** Equality of rights under the law shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or by any State on account of sex.
- **Sec. 2.** Congress shall have the power to enforce the article by appropriate legislation.
- **Sec. 3.** The amendment shall take effect two years after the date of ratification (slide 6).

The deck's procedural history: in 1978 Congress extended the deadline to 1982 but did not get two-thirds of the votes to do so; in 1982, insufficient states had ratified (slide 6). The deck notes that since then thirty-eight states have at one time or another ratified, though some have rescinded, and links a Congressional Research Service report discussing lawsuits demanding that the National Archivist recognize the amendment's passage (slide 6). The deck closes the slide with the open prompt: how would this amendment change constitutional law (slide 6)?
