## Professor Emphasis

### From source

The packet is titled "Counseling IQS" and bundles three doctrines together — Commerce Clause, Political Question, and Mootness — around a single client scenario rather than treating Commerce Clause in isolation (slide 1). The fact pattern is a federal "Critical Commodities Stabilization Act" (CCSA) that authorizes the President to declare a national economic emergency and order domestic manufacturers to produce specified quantities at government-set prices, backed by civil penalties up to $50,000 per day and possible criminal sanctions (slide 3). The client is a small, single-facility semiconductor manufacturer that sells exclusively to in-state customers and has never sold products outside the state; the mandate would force it to retool at roughly $2.3 million and suspend its existing specialty production for four-plus months (slide 2). After the district court denied a motion to dismiss and signaled skepticism, the President rescinded the emergency declaration while keeping the CCSA itself in force and warning that mandates might return (slide 4). The packet then drives the student through three counseling questions: whether the Commerce Clause challenge has merit, whether the political-question argument has force, and whether the case survives the mootness motion (slide 5).

The Commerce Clause answer key flags NFIB v. Sebelius's regulate-versus-compel line as the strongest argument: the Commerce Clause presupposes existing commercial activity, and the CCSA's production mandate forces the client to manufacture a product it has never produced, effectively creating commerce rather than regulating it (slide 7). The packet treats the "operates entirely in-state" answer as weaker because under Gonzales v. Raich even purely intrastate activity can be reached if it substantially affects interstate commerce, and treats the Tenth Amendment "traditional state functions" answer as foreclosed by Garcia v. San Antonio MTA (1985) (slide 7).

The mootness answer key turns on Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw's voluntary-cessation doctrine: a case becomes moot only if subsequent events make it "absolutely clear" that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur, and the government carries a "heavy burden" (slide 9). The packet calls the doctrine "squarely triggered" because the President unilaterally rescinded after the court signaled skepticism, the CCSA remains in force, and the President expressly threatened to reinstate mandates (slide 9). Two facts are flagged as carrying the voluntary-cessation argument: the President's press-conference statement that he would "not hesitate to reinstate emergency production requirements" if conditions deteriorate, and § 7(a) of the CCSA, which provides that nothing precludes the President from issuing a new declaration on the same commodity (slide 11).

The political-question answer key sets up a Baker v. Carr two-sided argument. The government's side leans on the "textually demonstrable constitutional commitment" prong because § 2(a) of the CCSA commits the emergency determination to the President's assessment (slide 13). The client's side responds that the suit is not asking the court to decide whether an emergency exists but whether Congress has the constitutional authority under the Commerce Clause to compel production — a quintessential judicial question about the scope of an enumerated power, with judicially manageable standards drawn from existing Commerce Clause doctrine (slide 13).

The Raich-versus-NFIB tension is presented as the central doctrinal collision in the packet (slide 14). The government's Raich argument is that Congress may regulate purely intrastate, non-commercial activity if failure to regulate that class of activity would undercut regulation of the interstate market, and that small manufacturers refusing to produce standardized chips could undermine a comprehensive scheme to stabilize the semiconductor supply chain (slide 15). The client's NFIB response insists on the regulate-versus-compel line: Raich involved regulation of existing activity (growing marijuana), while the CCSA compels the client to begin an entirely new line of production (slide 15).

The packet closes with a counseling question — should the client pursue the lawsuit or accept the rescission and walk away — and the answer key directs counsel to continue litigating because rescission does not protect against future mandates and § 7(a) of the CCSA expressly preserves the President's re-declaration authority (slides 16-17). The closing summary repackages the four operative moves: NFIB's regulate-not-compel principle is the strongest Commerce Clause argument; the political-question argument has surface appeal but fails because the challenge is to congressional power rather than the President's factual determination; voluntary cessation keeps the case alive; and the client should continue litigating because a favorable ruling provides lasting protection against $50,000-per-day penalty exposure (slide 18).

## Issue-Spotting Architecture

The packet is structured as a multiple-choice plus short-answer counseling sequence built around a single client scenario. The architecture is worth tracing because it shows which analytical moves the packet treats as exam-critical.

### The Client and the Statute

The hypothetical client is "Marcus Chen & Precision Silicon Corp.," a single-facility specialty semiconductor manufacturer in fictional Franklin that has never sold outside the state (slide 2). The statute under attack is the Critical Commodities Stabilization Act: § 2(a) lets the President declare a "national economic emergency" for any commodity essential to economic security; § 3(a) lets the Secretary of Commerce order domestic manufacturers to produce specified quantities at government-set prices; § 7(a) keeps the declaration alive until terminated and expressly allows re-declaration for the same commodity; § 5(a) carries the $50,000-per-day civil penalty and criminal exposure (slide 3). The procedural posture is engineered to put mootness in play: the President rescinded the emergency only after the district court denied the motion to dismiss and signaled skepticism, and the government then moved to dismiss as moot while the CCSA itself stayed in force (slide 4).

### Question 1 — Commerce Clause (Multiple Choice)

The question asks which option "best describes the strongest constitutional argument" the client can make under the Commerce Clause (slide 6). The four options are:

- (A) Commerce Clause does not authorize compelled production — the power to "regulate" presupposes existing activity, so compelling production creates commerce rather than regulates it.
- (B) Commerce Clause does not reach the client because it operates entirely in-state with no interstate connection.
- (C) The CCSA violates the Tenth Amendment because manufacturing is a traditional state function.
- (D) The CCSA lacks an "intelligible principle" and so violates the nondelegation doctrine.

The packet identifies (A) as the correct answer because NFIB v. Sebelius held that Congress cannot compel individuals to become active in commerce, and the CCSA's mandate to produce a never-before-manufactured product crosses the regulate-versus-compel line (slide 7). It rejects (B) under Raich (purely intrastate activity can be reached when it substantially affects interstate commerce, and semiconductors are part of a national market), rejects (C) because Garcia v. San Antonio MTA (1985) abandoned the "traditional state functions" test, and treats (D) as a separate doctrine that is not the strongest Commerce Clause argument the question asks for (slide 7).

### Question 2 — Mootness (Multiple Choice)

The question asks which mootness doctrine is most applicable to the client's argument that the case remains live (slide 8). The four options are:

- (A) "Capable of repetition, yet evading review."
- (B) "Voluntary cessation."
- (C) Not moot because the client already suffered actual injury (retooling costs) that cannot be undone.
- (D) Not moot because the CCSA itself, not the emergency declaration, is the object of the challenge.

The packet identifies (B) as the correct answer under Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw: the government has not shown it is "absolutely clear" the conduct could not reasonably be expected to recur (slide 9). It treats (A) as a poorer fit because capable-of-repetition typically requires that the challenged action be too short in duration for full litigation, treats (C) as factually unavailable because the client had not yet retooled, and treats (D) as a mismatch because the suit challenges the mandate under the Commerce Clause, not the existence of the statute (slide 9).

### Question 3 — Voluntary Cessation Facts (Short Answer)

Identify two specific facts supporting the argument that the case is not moot under voluntary cessation; the prompt nudges toward what the President said and what the statute allows (slide 10). The packet's two facts are: (1) the President's press-conference statement that "if supply-chain conditions deteriorate, I will not hesitate to reinstate emergency production requirements," which under Friends of the Earth demonstrates affirmative intent to repeat the challenged action; and (2) § 7(a) of the CCSA, which preserves the President's authority to re-declare the emergency on the same commodity (slide 11).

### Question 4 — Political Question (Short Answer Both Sides)

Identify one argument for and one against the political-question dismissal, with a nudge toward Baker v. Carr's two-part test and what the client is actually challenging (slide 12). The packet's both-sides answer:

- **For the government:** Baker v. Carr's "textually demonstrable constitutional commitment" prong applies because § 2(a) of the CCSA commits the emergency determination to the President's assessment of supply-chain vulnerabilities, foreign dependency, and economic disruption (slide 13).
- **Against the government:** The client is not asking the court to decide whether an emergency exists; the client is challenging whether Congress has the constitutional authority under the Commerce Clause to compel production. That is a quintessential judicial question about the scope of an enumerated power, and Baker's "judicially discoverable and manageable standards" prong is satisfied by existing Commerce Clause doctrine (slide 13).

### Question 5 — Raich vs. NFIB (Short Answer Both Sides)

The government argues the CCSA is valid under Raich's "comprehensive regulatory scheme" doctrine; the prompt flags the key tension as regulating existing intrastate activity versus compelling new production (slide 14).

- **Government's Raich argument:** Congress may regulate purely intrastate, non-commercial activity if failure to regulate that class of activity would undercut regulation of the interstate market in the commodity. Semiconductors are a critical national commodity, and small-manufacturer refusal to produce standardized chips, multiplied across the class, could undermine a comprehensive supply-chain stabilization scheme (slide 15).
- **Client's NFIB response:** NFIB drew a critical line between regulating existing commercial activity and compelling it. Raich involved regulation of existing activity (homegrown marijuana); the CCSA compels the client to begin an entirely new line of production. Even within a comprehensive scheme, the constitutional line between regulating what someone already does and ordering them to do something they have never done holds (slide 15).

### Question 6 — Should the Client Sue (Counseling)

Should counsel advise the client to pursue the lawsuit or accept the rescission and move on; identify one reason to continue (slide 16). The packet recommends continuing the litigation: the rescission does not protect the client from future mandates because the CCSA remains in effect, the President has expressly threatened to reinstate, and § 7(a) expressly preserves re-declaration authority. A favorable ruling now would provide lasting protection — a judicial determination that the Commerce Clause does not authorize compelled production — and the costs of continued litigation are justified by the risk of a renewed $50,000-per-day penalty exposure (slide 17).

## Key Takeaways

- **NFIB's regulate-not-compel line is the strongest Commerce Clause argument when the federal action is a production mandate.** The packet treats the distinction between regulating existing commercial activity and compelling new commercial activity as the operative move (slide 7, slide 18).
- **Raich aggregation can be neutralized by foregrounding "compulsion vs. regulation."** Even if a federal statute claims comprehensive-scheme cover, the client should anchor on whether the regulated party already engages in the relevant activity (slide 15).
- **Voluntary cessation is the dominant mootness doctrine when a government rescinds challenged conduct mid-litigation.** The government carries a heavy burden to show absolute clarity that the conduct will not recur; press-conference threats and surviving re-declaration authority defeat that burden (slides 9, 11).
- **Political-question arguments should be re-channeled.** When the government invokes Baker's "textually committed" prong against an enumerated-powers challenge, the client should reframe the question from "is there an emergency?" (committed to the executive) to "does Congress have power X?" (a classic judicial question) (slide 13).
- **Counsel should advise continued litigation when the underlying statute survives the rescission.** Walking away after rescission leaves the client exposed to renewed mandates with no precedent to deter them (slide 17).
