## Professor Emphasis

### From source

The deck poses the central question -- "Why Classify Classifications?" -- and names four competing theories: anti-subordination, anti-classification, process theory, and displaced Lochnerism *(slides 1-2)*. The deck's framing of equal protection's literal problem is that every law classifies -- speed limits, tax brackets, criminal law treating bank robbers differently from non-bank robbers -- so equal protection cannot mean "no classifications" and must mean "some classifications require justification that others do not" *(slide 2)*. The "Displaced Lochnerism" slide is the deck's most pedagogically distinctive: each tier is "a judicial prior probability" -- strict scrutiny = high prior (probably unconstitutional), intermediate = moderate, rational basis = low; the deck calls the relocation of value judgment from the retail statute to the wholesale category "Lochnerism—at the tier-assignment stage" *(slide 7)*. ## Lecture Outline

**The Literal Problem**

The text of the Fourteenth Amendment: "nor shall any State ... deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws" *(slide 2)*. Read literally, this would prohibit all legislative classifications, because every law classifies -- a speed limit treats fast drivers differently from slow ones; tax brackets treat high earners differently from low earners; criminal law treats bank robbers differently from non-bank robbers *(slide 2)*. The necessary inference: if equal protection cannot mean "no classifications," it must mean some classifications require justification that others do not *(slide 2)*. The deck identifies four candidate theories: anti-subordination (caste-like hierarchy), anti-classification (traits rarely relevant), process theory (politically unprotected groups), and displaced Lochnerism (tier assignments are themselves judicial value judgments) *(slide 2)*. The question is not whether the Court makes value judgments but which framework best disciplines them *(slide 2)*.

**Theory 1: Anti-Subordination**

The Slaughter-House Cases (1873) supply the original understanding: Justice Miller wrote that the Fourteenth Amendment's "one pervading purpose" was the protection of the newly freed slaves; equal protection was designed to prevent state-imposed subordination of a specific group -- the formerly enslaved -- through institutions like the Black Codes; Miller doubted the clause would "ever be applied" beyond racial discrimination *(slide 3)*. Modern anti-subordination theorists (Fiss, Balkin, Siegel) generalize: the clause targets classifications that create or maintain social hierarchy -- systems of group-based subordination that deny dignity and full citizenship; on this view, race-based affirmative action might be permissible (it dismantles hierarchy) while race-based exclusion is not *(slide 3)*. Strengths: faithful to the historical purpose of the Fourteenth Amendment; explains asymmetry between subordinated-group discrimination and "reverse discrimination"; provides a substantive standard *(slide 3)*. Difficulties: who counts as a subordinated group risks infinite contestation; courts must make sociological judgments about group status; hard to explain why the Court applies strict scrutiny to laws benefiting subordinated groups (e.g., *SFFA v. Harvard*) *(slide 3)*.

**Theory 2: Anti-Classification**

The anti-classification view holds that certain traits -- race, sex, national origin -- are almost never a legitimate basis for legislation, so any law using them is presumptively suspect and the burden shifts to the government *(slide 4)*. This is the formal logic of strict scrutiny: the classification itself triggers heightened review regardless of whether the law harms or helps any particular group *(slide 4)*. Anti-classification is colorblind: if race is suspect, it is suspect when used to benefit racial minorities just as when used to harm them *(slide 4)*. This view prevailed in *Adarand* (1995), *Parents Involved* (2007), and *SFFA v. Harvard* (2023); Roberts: "The way to stop discrimination on the basis of race is to stop discriminating on the basis of race" *(slide 4)*. The political-process paradox: anti-classification provides its strongest judicial protection to the politically powerful -- men benefit from heightened review of sex classifications, whites benefit from heightened review of racial classifications, even where these groups can protect their interests through ordinary politics; this is the tension process theory attempts to resolve *(slide 4)*.

**Theory 3: Process Theory -- Footnote Four**

*United States v. Carolene Products* (1938) established the default rule: legislation affecting "ordinary commercial transactions" receives only rational basis review -- the post-Lochner bargain in which courts would stop second-guessing economic regulation *(slide 5)*. Stone's Footnote Four created three exceptions to deference: (1) laws restricting rights in the Bill of Rights; (2) laws restricting political processes (voting, speech, assembly); and (3) laws targeting "discrete and insular minorities" who face "prejudice" that "curtails the operation of those political processes ordinarily to be relied upon to protect minorities" *(slide 5)*. The deck reproduces the footnote: prejudice against discrete and insular minorities may be a special condition calling for "a correspondingly more searching judicial inquiry" *(slide 5)*. The deck's logic: heightened scrutiny is warranted where the political process cannot be trusted -- Ely's "representation-reinforcing" theory -- but deciding which groups the process fails is itself a value judgment *(slide 5)*. This page reads *Craig v. Boren*'s intermediate scrutiny for sex -- applied to young men buying beer -- as a case process theory cannot explain; the anti-stereotyping principle is doing the work.

**Theory 4: Displaced Lochnerism**

In *Lochner* (1905), the Court asked whether a particular law was a good enough idea to survive review; that case-by-case judicial evaluation was discredited *(slide 7)*. The tiers framework does not eliminate the value judgment but relocates it -- instead of asking "is this law a bad idea?" the Court now asks "is this type of law probably a bad idea?" *(slide 7)*. Each tier is effectively a judicial prior probability that the classification is unconstitutional -- strict scrutiny = high prior (probably unconstitutional); intermediate = moderate prior (maybe unconstitutional); rational basis = low prior (probably constitutional); evidence at trial can update the prior, but when the starting point is extreme it is very difficult to overcome *(slide 7)*.

The race-vs-age comparison: both are immutable, both are often irrelevant; yet race gets strict scrutiny and age gets rational basis -- the conventional answer (political process, history) "masks a value judgment" that the Court has decided racial classifications are almost certainly bad ideas while age classifications are usually fine; that is Lochnerism at the tier-assignment stage *(slide 7)*.

**The Three-Tier Framework in Practice**

Strict scrutiny applies to race, national origin, and alienage (with exceptions): compelling interest plus narrow tailoring; burden on the government; "strict in theory, fatal in fact" -- almost all laws fail, with rare survivors Korematsu and Grutter *(slide 6)*. Intermediate scrutiny applies to sex and legitimacy: important interest plus substantial relation; burden on the government; mixed outcomes -- struck down in *Craig v. Boren* and *VMI*, upheld in *Rostker* and *Nguyen* *(slide 6)*. Rational basis applies to everything else (age, disability, wealth, etc.): rationally related to a legitimate government interest; burden on the challenger; nearly all laws upheld, with rare failures *Moreno*, *Cleburne*, and *Romer* -- "rational basis with bite" *(slide 6)*. The deck's diagnostic claim: the outcome of most cases is determined at the threshold -- which tier applies -- and the real judicial work is classifying the classification, not evaluating the evidence; this is what the "displaced Lochnerism" thesis targets *(slide 6)*.

**Tier Obsession and Its Metastasis**

The tier-assignment structure has spread beyond equal protection *(slide 7)*. First Amendment: content-based versus content-neutral *(slide 7)*. Free Exercise: neutral/general versus targeted *(slide 7)*. Second Amendment: *Bruen* rejected tiers entirely *(slide 7)*. The displaced Lochnerism critique thus cuts across multiple doctrinal areas.

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

The lecture's centerpiece hypo is the race-versus-age comparison *(slide 7)*. Both race and age are immutable; both are often irrelevant. Yet race gets strict scrutiny and age gets rational basis. The conventional explanation (political power, history) is examined critically: is it just that the Court decided racial classifications are probably unconstitutional while age classifications are probably fine? And if that is Lochnerism at the tier level, does it undermine the anti-Lochner bargain of 1937?

The Bayesian framing generates its own hypothetical *(slide 7)*: if strict scrutiny is a prior probability of unconstitutionality of approximately 95%, should a sufficiently compelling factual record be able to change the outcome? The Court's answer in practice is "almost never" -- which raises the question of whether the tier system is doing legal work or simply expressing the Court's policy preferences about what kinds of laws are probably bad ideas.

## Key Takeaways

- Four theories compete to explain equal protection doctrine: anti-subordination, anti-classification, process theory, and displaced Lochnerism.
- No single theory explains all the doctrine: process theory cannot explain Craig; anti-subordination cannot explain SFFA; anti-classification cannot explain the symmetry paradox.
- The tier system is a Bayesian prior -- each level represents the Court's starting probability that the classification is unconstitutional.
- Because tier assignment drives outcomes, all the real judicial work happens at the threshold question of which tier applies.
- Displaced Lochnerism is the honest critique: the tier system does not eliminate judicial value judgments about which laws are probably bad ideas; it relocates them from the statute to the category.
- The same structural critique has infected other doctrinal areas: First Amendment, Free Exercise, and (per Bruen) Second Amendment.
