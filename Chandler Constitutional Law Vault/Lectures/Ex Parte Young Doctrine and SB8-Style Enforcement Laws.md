# Ex Parte Young Doctrine and SB8-Style Enforcement Laws

<!-- 2026-05-26: convention rollout (slide anchors + Professor Emphasis split) -->

## Professor Emphasis

### From source

The "one slide" framing: the Eleventh Amendment does not prohibit suits based on alleged constitutional wrongs against state officials for injunctive relief, but states may be able to restrict that pathway by creating private rights of action lodged in non-state officials -- and federal courts cannot enjoin a state court judge or court clerks from hearing such cases (slide 2). The lecture is structured around Ex Parte Young's "fiction" (slide 9) and how the SB 8 design defeats it (slides 13-15). The deck flags the slogan "I don't care about abortion or Texas" -- the statute is a template for states to make it difficult to vindicate disfavored constitutional rights, and California's SB 1327 against assault weapons and ghost guns is offered as the parallel case (slide 28). Mitchell's 2018 Virginia Law Review article is presented as the blueprint that Texas implemented (slides 13-14).

### Editor's framing

This page treats the lecture as a paired study: the Ex Parte Young workaround on one side, the Mitchell/SB 8 counter-workaround on the other. The "double fiction" gloss (officer is not the state for immunity but is the state for constitutional purposes) is editorial packaging of the deck's single-fiction framing. The "in terrorem" characterization of asymmetric fees and the framing of Jackson as showing "the power and limits of the maneuver" are this page's organization, not slide language.

## Lecture Outline

**Background: State Punishments and Prospective Relief**

States can punish conduct through criminal law and civil enforcement (slide 3). The traditional mechanisms for challenging an unconstitutional state law include asserting a constitutional defense at criminal trial -- the evidence was based on an unlawful search, denial of right to counsel, or the "crime" is really protected first-amendment speech -- federal review by certiorari, concurrent attack (difficult under 28 U.S.C. 2283), or prospective attack in state or federal court via declaratory or injunctive action (slide 4). Susan B. Anthony List v. Driehaus (2014) (9-0 by Justice Thomas) established a favorable standing doctrine for pre-enforcement challenges and is flagged as where Ex Parte Young fits in (slide 4).

Why seek prospective relief rather than defending at trial? In criminal cases the need is obvious; in civil cases the deck enumerates appeal bonds, expensive litigation, multiple lawsuits, distant forums, attorneys' fee liability, stigma, and insurance implications (slide 6). The whole-system cost of waiting for enforcement can be prohibitive, especially for medical providers facing $10,000-per-procedure liability.

**Ex Parte Young (1908): The Fiction**

Minnesota enacted a criminal statute punishing allegedly excessive railroad rates; railroads could challenge only by charging the high rates and risking prison; the Eleventh Amendment presumably barred suit against the state itself, with Hans v. Louisiana extending that bar to citizen-vs.-state suits (slide 8). But could the railroads (or their shareholders, derivatively) sue the officer who would enforce the criminal prohibition (slide 8)? The Court said yes via the "fiction" of Ex Parte Young: the general discretion of an officer is not interfered with by an injunction restraining steps toward enforcing an unconstitutional enactment, since the officer is simply prohibited from doing an act he had no legal right to do, and use of the State's name to enforce an unconstitutional act does not affect the State in its sovereign capacity (slide 9). The Court reasoned that an unconstitutional law is void, so the officer who enforces it acts without any legal authority.

Limits of Ex Parte Young: a federal court that first obtained jurisdiction may hold it to the exclusion of all others, but the right to enjoin an individual does not include power to restrain a state court from acting, or to prevent grand-jury investigation -- enjoining a state court would violate "the whole scheme of our Government" (slide 10). Retroactive money damages are barred as effectively suits against the state under Edelman v. Jordan. The deck notes that gazillions of lawsuits have proceeded against state officials using this fiction, including section 1983 civil rights suits (slide 11).

**Abortion Doctrine and the SB 8 Innovation**

The lecture pauses to set the doctrinal predicate: Roe v. Wade (1973) established a constitutional right to abortion grounded in privacy, and Planned Parenthood v. Casey (1992) reaffirmed Roe while introducing the undue-burden standard for pre-viability regulation (slide 12). Roe and Casey remained good law until Dobbs, though unpopular with a minority and viewed as dubious by many scholars (slide 12). Jonathan Mitchell, former Texas Solicitor General, foretold the SB 8 strategy in The Writ-of-Erasure Fallacy, 104 Va. L. Rev. 933 (2018): create an invisible enemy by creating a private right of action and prohibiting governmental enforcement, both criminal and otherwise (slide 13). Rely on the uncertainty effect to deter exercise of constitutional rights -- no statute of limitations and bar tolling, no mistake-of-law defense, broad standing (state courts not bound by Lujan), no defendant-side attorneys' fees, and favorable venue (slide 14).

Texas adopted the Mitchell strategy in SB 8, enacted May 2021 and effective September 1, 2021, creating a civil cause of action against persons participating in abortions after a heartbeat (typically 6 weeks LMP -- pre-viability and clearly unconstitutional under Roe/Casey) with minimum $10,000 damages, injunctive relief, plaintiff-only attorneys' fees, and no fees for prevailing defendants (slide 15). The deck poses the puzzle: why don't doctors just go ahead and perform abortions permitted by Casey when the 6-23 week prohibition is clearly unconstitutional (slide 16)? The statutory text bars public enforcement: requirements "shall be enforced exclusively through the private civil actions described," and no enforcement may be taken or threatened by the state, a political subdivision, a district attorney, or any executive officer (slide 17). The combination creates prohibitive uncertainty even though the substantive provision is clearly unconstitutional under Roe/Casey.

**Whole Woman's Health v. Jackson (2021)**

The case generated multiple lawsuits, including the federal district court action by Whole Woman's Health against a state court judge (Jackson), a state court clerk, the Texas AG, a medical licensure official, and an anti-abortion advocate; a state-court suit by providers against Texas Right to Life; and a separate federal suit by the United States that yielded a preliminary injunction the Fifth Circuit stayed (slide 20). The procedural history saw the district court enjoin, the Fifth Circuit stay, the Supreme Court refuse emergency appeal, and ultimately take the case on a "mediumish fast track" (slide 21). The lineup: Gorsuch authored, joined by Alito, Kavanaugh, and Barrett, with Thomas concurring in most of it but rejecting any claim against licensing defendants; CJ Roberts dissented joined by Breyer, Sotomayor, and Kagan, and Sotomayor wrote separately joined by Kagan and Breyer (slide 22).

The decision held WWH v. Jackson closely resembles Ex Parte Young -- an attempt to keep an arguably unconstitutional enactment in state courts and prevent federal review -- and that suit against the state AG can circumvent the Eleventh Amendment (slide 24). But Young's dicta controlled: an injunction by a federal court against a state court would violate the whole scheme of this Government, and the fact-pattern of "no individual to be enjoined" was not before the Court (slide 24). CJ Roberts in dissent invoked United States v. Peters: if state legislatures may at will annul federal judgments, the constitution itself becomes a solemn mockery; the nature of the federal right does not matter -- it is the role of the Supreme Court in our constitutional system that is at stake (slide 26). The majority responded to Sotomayor's systemic-oppression critique by analogizing private enforcement to private attorneys general acts, tort law, federal antitrust law, and even the Civil Rights Act of 1964, and asking where her plan to overthrow precedent and expand federal equitable powers would stop (slide 27).

**Aftermath and Counter-Strategies**

California enacted SB 1327, using the same template to allow suits against anyone who manufactures, distributes, transports, or sells assault weapons, .50 BMG rifles, or ghost guns, and to penalize unapproved home storage; the asymmetric attorneys' fees provision was struck down in Miller v. Bonta (S.D. Cal. 2022) (slide 28). Potential responses canvassed by the deck (slide 29): removal under 28 U.S.C. 1443 (unavailable because Vlaming v. W. Point Sch. Bd. (4th Cir. 2021) limits it to racial-equality claims); state-court declaratory judgment paths (the Texas Supreme Court reversed in Texas Right to Life v. Van Stean (Nov. 22, 2024) because standing always comes first); congressional creation of a federal removal right for Roe/Casey defenses; spending-power indemnity for SB 8 victims; abrogation of state sovereign immunity under Section 5 (as limited by City of Boerne v. Flores); or something like HR 3755 (slide 29).

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

**The No-Defendant Problem:** What if SB 8 had been even more carefully drafted to eliminate all collateral state consequences -- no licensing effects, no criminal penalties, purely private civil liability? The Jackson majority acknowledged this fact was not before it. Chandler implies the answer is that Ex Parte Young provides no remedy at all if no officer has any enforcement role, which would represent a potentially complete evasion of constitutional rights vindication in federal court.

**Template Use:** Could states use the Mitchell strategy to effectively nullify the Second Amendment (California SB 1327), First Amendment (bounties for reporting on-campus speech), or Fourth Amendment? Chandler uses this to press students on whether the majority's "private attorneys general" analogy holds up when the bounty system is specifically designed to deter exercise of constitutional rights that are facially valid.

**Congressional Fix:** 28 U.S.C. 1443 removal jurisdiction is currently limited by precedent to racial equality claims. Could Congress expand it? Could Congress use Section 5 to create a broader right of removal for any constitutional defense? These questions connect to City of Boerne and the congruence-and-proportionality framework for Section 5 legislation.

## Key Takeaways

- Ex Parte Young provides prospective injunctive relief against state officers enforcing unconstitutional laws, working around sovereign immunity through the "fiction" that the officer acts without legal authority.
- Ex Parte Young has two categorical limits: it cannot reach state courts or grand juries, and it cannot require retroactive money damages (Edelman).
- SB 8's private-enforcement design defeats Ex Parte Young by eliminating injunctible state officers; the Supreme Court in Jackson recognized this but provided no complete remedy.
- The SB 8 template can be applied to any constitutional right; California used it against gun manufacturers, illustrating the non-abortion stakes.
- Congressional responses are available (removal expansion, Section 5 abrogation, spending-power indemnification) but limited by precedent on 28 U.S.C. 1443 and by the congruence-and-proportionality requirement.
