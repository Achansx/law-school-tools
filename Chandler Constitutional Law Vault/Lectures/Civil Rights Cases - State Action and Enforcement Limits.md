### From source

The deck poses the central textual question: can Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment "prophylactically reach private conduct, particularly where state protection of 14(1) is weak?" *(slide 6)*. ## Lecture Outline

**Reconstruction Context**

After the Civil War, secessionist states had no representation in Congress (and sought none) during the war; the South surrendered between April and November 1865, beginning with Lee's surrender to Grant on April 9, 1865 *(slide 2)*. By December 1865, all secessionist states except Texas had asked to rejoin the Union *(slide 2)*. The Thirteenth Amendment was ratified even before the Civil War ended, and states did not have to have representation in Congress in order to ratify a constitutional amendment *(slide 3)*. Congress then conditioned readmission of Virginia, North Carolina, South Carolina, Georgia, Mississippi, Alabama, Louisiana, Texas, Florida, and Arkansas on ratification of the Fourteenth Amendment, which got through Congress only after a tough fight and many versions *(slides 4-5)*. Despite military victory, the North had limited ability to change Southern attitudes toward African Americans, and war fatigue eventually led to the withdrawal of federal troops, culminating in the Compromise of 1876.

**Civil Rights Act of 1875 and the Charged Facts**

The Civil Rights Act of 1875 guaranteed all persons within the jurisdiction of the United States the full and equal enjoyment of the accommodations, advantages, facilities, and privileges of inns, public conveyances on land or water, theatres, and other places of public amusement, subject only to conditions applicable alike to citizens of every race and color *(slide 7)*. The deck flags the resonance with later civil rights statutes by asking "Sound familiar?" *(slide 7)*. Multiple private businesses -- including opera houses in many states -- engaged in segregation and were prosecuted under the Act *(slide 7)*.

**Bradley's Majority Opinion: Fourteenth Amendment Analysis**

Justice Bradley, drawing on the scope set forth in Cruikshank, held that the Fourteenth Amendment prohibits state action but not individual invasion of individual rights *(slide 8)*. Individual actions unsupported by law, custom, executive, or judicial action were not within the amendment's scope *(slide 8)*. The law must be "adapted to the mischief and wrong which the amendment was intended to provide against," and the deck prompts students to ask whether this "sound[s] like City of Boerne?" *(slide 8)*. Congress under Section 5 did not create a federal police power *(slide 8)*.

**Bradley's Majority: Thirteenth Amendment Analysis**

On the Thirteenth Amendment, Bradley asked what, if anything, beyond clear slavery or involuntary servitude the majority might concede as violating the amendment *(slide 9)*. The acknowledged badges of slavery were: compulsory service of the slave for the benefit of the master; restraint of movement except by the master's will; disability to hold property, make contracts, have standing in court, and be a witness against a white person; and "such like burdens and incapacities" -- but not the "social rights of men and races in the community" such as segregation and denigration *(slide 9)*. A famous passage holds that when a man has emerged from slavery and shaken off the inseparable concomitants of that state, there must be some stage in his elevation when he takes the rank of a mere citizen and ceases to be "the special favorite of the laws," with rights to be protected in the ordinary modes by which other men's rights are protected *(slide 10)*. The same passage observes that thousands of free colored people enjoyed essential rights of life, liberty, and property before abolition, and that mere discriminations on account of race or color were not regarded as badges of slavery -- though the deck flags Dred Scott's contrary holding that such rights did not make them citizens *(slide 10)*. The slide also flags a connection to Grutter *(slide 10)*.

**Harlan's Dissent**

Harlan's Thirteenth Amendment dissent asks what principle determines the bounds of Section 2 power *(slide 11)*. The deck prompts students to understand why the common law would impose special duties on an innkeeper and how the licensure of amusement parks affects Harlan's reading of the Thirteenth Amendment *(slide 11)*. The deck closes with the open-ended question whether making the Thirteenth Amendment the centerpiece of civil rights legislation poses any problems *(slide 11)*.

Harlan's Fourteenth Amendment argument reads Section 5's enforcement power as going beyond the mere prohibitions of Section 1 *(slide 12)*. The deck asks whether the majority has any response to that reading, what exactly the Fourteenth Amendment provides, how Prigg comes back to help Harlan, what Harlan means by "social relations," whether his line between social relations and other businesses would work, how Ollie's Barbecue policy of no Blacks in the sit-down portion would fare under Harlan's theory, and what it says that Harlan got not even one other vote *(slide 12)*.

**Significance and Forward Connections**

The lecture connects the Civil Rights Cases to Cruikshank's earlier ruling that the right to peaceably assemble and the Second Amendment right were not rights granted by the Constitution and therefore could not be enforced against private actors under the Civil Rights Act of 1870. The Ollie's Barbecue thread -- whether a restaurant that excluded Black customers from its sit-down area would fare under Harlan's Thirteenth Amendment theory -- is teed up explicitly on the Harlan dissent slide and previews the commerce-clause workaround students will encounter in Katzenbach v. McClung *(slide 12)*.

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

The deck flags a Grutter reference in connection with the "special favorite of the laws" passage *(slide 10)*, which this page reads as inviting students to consider how the majority's reasoning in Civil Rights Cases echoes later debates about affirmative action and when remedial race-consciousness becomes impermissible.

The Ollie's Barbecue hypothetical tests Harlan's Thirteenth Amendment theory: if serving food to Black customers in the sit-down portion of a restaurant is racially segregated, is that a badge of slavery under Harlan's theory? *(slide 12)*. This page reads the hypothetical as probing whether the badges theory has a limiting principle once you move away from core slavery incidents.

## Key Takeaways

- State action doctrine originates in the Civil Rights Cases: the Fourteenth Amendment bars state denials of civil rights, not private discrimination.
- The Thirteenth Amendment's "badges of slavery" language is the doctrinal alternative to the state action doctrine for reaching private conduct, but the Civil Rights Cases defined "badges" narrowly to exclude social discrimination.
- Congress's Section 5 enforcement power is limited to correcting actual or likely state violations, not creating a general federal police power over private behavior, anticipating the congruence-and-proportionality framework of City of Boerne.
- Harlan's dissent foreshadowed the commerce-clause approach to civil rights legislation that Congress would eventually use in the Civil Rights Act of 1964.
- The decision reflects the political economy of Reconstruction's end: Northern war fatigue and the withdrawal of federal troops meant the constitutional compromise made practical sense even if the textual arguments were contestable.
