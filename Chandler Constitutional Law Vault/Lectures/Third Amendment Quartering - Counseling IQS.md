### From source

The packet identifies its starred testing topic as I.B Ripeness, with a secondary connection to I.A Standing; the substantive doctrinal hooks are Third Amendment merits, the incorporation doctrine, and state action (p. 1). ## The Hypothetical

The client is Margaret Dalton, a homeowner in Millbrook, Franklin, who lives alone in a three-bedroom house (pp. 1-2). Governor Raymond Haskell has signed Executive Order No. 2026-14, the "Quartering Order," which directs the Franklin National Guard to address a housing shortage for Guard members deployed for extended border-security operations along Franklin's southern border (pp. 1-2). The Order's operative provisions: the Adjutant General will identify private residences within 30 miles of deployment zones that contain one or more unoccupied bedrooms; owners must make one bedroom available per household for the duration of Operation Secure Frontier; homeowners receive $50/day per quartered soldier; refusal is a Class B misdemeanor punishable by up to $5,000 and 90 days' imprisonment; and the Order takes effect 60 days from signing (p. 2). Dalton's house falls within the 30-mile radius, local media reports that the Adjutant General has begun surveying her area, but she has not received individual notification and the effective date has not yet arrived (p. 3).

Franklin Attorney General Lisa Thorpe has held a press conference defending the Order on the ground that the Third Amendment is an "18th-century relic" written for the age of the Redcoats, that no Supreme Court has ever held it applies to the states, and that the right against quartering is not "fundamental to our scheme of ordered liberty" in the way free speech or right to counsel are (pp. 2-3). Dalton tells the attorney she does not care about the $50/day; she does not want a stranger living in her house, and she wants to know whether she can act before soldiers actually arrive (p. 3).

## The Doctrinal Scaffolding

### Text and incorporation status

The Third Amendment provides that "[n]o Soldier shall, in time of peace, be quartered in any house, without the consent of the Owner, nor in time of war, but in a manner to be prescribed by law" (p. 3). The packet pairs this with the Fourteenth Amendment Section 1 text as the incorporation vehicle (pp. 3-4). The incorporation framework comes from *Duncan v. Louisiana* (1968) and *McDonald v. City of Chicago* (2010): a right is incorporated if it is among the "fundamental principles of liberty and justice which lie at the base of all our civil and political institutions" (*Duncan*) and if it is "fundamental to our scheme of ordered liberty" or "deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition" (*McDonald* plurality) (p. 4).

### Engblom v. Carey — the only circuit-level application

*Engblom v. Carey*, 677 F.2d 957 (2d Cir. 1982), arose when New York's Governor housed National Guard troops in residential facilities at a state prison that had previously been occupied by striking correction officers (p. 4). The Second Circuit held three things relevant here: the Third Amendment is incorporated into the Fourteenth Amendment for application to the states; the property-based privacy interests it protects extend beyond fee-simple ownership to lawful occupation or possession with a legal right to exclude others; and National Guardsmen are "Soldiers" within the meaning of the Third Amendment (p. 4).

### The ripeness framework

The packet supplies two paradigm cases for ripeness analysis (pp. 4-5). *Abbott Laboratories v. Gardner* (1967) establishes the two-prong test — fitness for judicial decision and hardship from withholding review — and is described in the instructor notes as the "paradigm case for pre-enforcement review" (pp. 4, 14). *Ohio Forestry Ass'n v. Sierra Club* (1998) is the counter-example: a general forest management plan that did not command anyone to act, did not impose liability, and did not create legal rights or obligations was held not ripe (pp. 5, 14).

## Counseling Walkthrough

### Issue 1 — most direct constitutional basis

The packet's first question asks which provision provides the most direct basis for Dalton's challenge (p. 5). The model answer is the Third Amendment itself: it directly prohibits quartering of soldiers in private homes without the owner's consent during peacetime, and the Order directs precisely that conduct (p. 6). Fourteenth Amendment Due Process is a secondary theory and the vehicle through which incorporation operates; the Fourth Amendment addresses searches and seizures, not quartering; and the Fifth Amendment Takings Clause is not the right doctrinal match because the $50/day compensation does not cure a Third Amendment violation — peacetime quartering requires consent, not just compensation (p. 6).

### Issue 2 — incorporation status

The most accurate statement of the Third Amendment's incorporation status is that one federal circuit court (the Second Circuit in *Engblom*) has held it incorporated against the states, but the Supreme Court has never addressed the question (p. 6). Treating it as definitively incorporated overstates the law; treating it as definitively unincorporated misreads *Engblom*; and treating it as applying directly to the states without incorporation gets the pre-Fourteenth-Amendment baseline wrong (p. 6).

### Issue 3 — ripeness (fitness prong)

Dalton's challenge is ripe under *Abbott Laboratories* because both prongs are satisfied (pp. 6-7). On fitness, the constitutionality of the Order under the Third Amendment is a purely legal question that does not require further factual development (p. 6).

### Issue 4 — ripeness (hardship prong and the State's counter-argument)

The State's likely counter-argument is that no individual notification has been sent to Dalton and the effective date has not arrived, so she faces no concrete imminent harm (p. 7). The packet's model answer says that argument fails because the Order applies by its terms to all residences with unoccupied bedrooms within 30 miles, Dalton's three-bedroom Millbrook home falls within that description, the Adjutant General has begun surveying her area, and the Order's criminal penalties — a Class B misdemeanor, up to $5,000 and 90 days' imprisonment — create the *Abbott Labs* compliance/noncompliance dilemma now, well before the effective date (p. 7). The packet flags the *Ohio Forestry* distinction: unlike a general management plan, the Quartering Order commands specific action, imposes criminal liability, and modifies legal obligations (p. 7).

### Issue 5 — Third Amendment merits

The packet's strongest merits argument has three dispositive facts (pp. 7-8). First, National Guard members are "Soldiers" — *Engblom* held that "National Guardsmen are 'Soldiers' within the meaning of the Third Amendment" (p. 8). Second, this is peacetime quartering: although the Order invokes border security, Franklin is not in a state of war (p. 8). Third, the Order overrides consent — Section 2 compels homeowners to make bedrooms available, and Section 4 criminalizes refusal (p. 8). The $50/day compensation is irrelevant because the Third Amendment in peacetime provides an absolute prohibition on nonconsensual quartering, not a requirement of just compensation (p. 8).

### Issue 6 — applying the Duncan/McDonald incorporation framework

This is the packet's centerpiece question and the close call (pp. 8, 14). Two distinct reasons satisfy the *Duncan*/*McDonald* standard (p. 9):

- *Historical rootedness.* The Third Amendment is "deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition" as *McDonald* requires: it was a direct response to the British Quartering Acts of 1765 and 1774, which were specifically cited in the Declaration of Independence; the prohibition was included in the original Bill of Rights in 1791; and virtually every state constitution adopted after independence contained a similar provision (p. 9). The packet observes that Attorney General Thorpe's characterization of the right as an "18th-century relic" actually underscores its deep historical roots — the depth of the history is exactly what *McDonald* asks for (p. 9).
- *Fundamental to ordered liberty.* The right satisfies *Duncan*'s test because it protects the sanctity of the home against government-compelled cohabitation with military personnel — a value that underpins the Fourth Amendment's search-and-seizure protections and the broader constitutional commitment to domestic privacy (p. 9). The packet rebuts the AG's "civilized society could function without this right" line by noting that the *Duncan*/*McDonald* framework does not require frequent litigation: the Second Amendment had virtually no Supreme Court case law for two centuries before *McDonald* incorporated it, and the current Order demonstrates the risk of quartering is not merely theoretical (p. 9).

### Issue 7 — merits defenses other than non-incorporation

The packet's example State defense is the wartime exception (p. 10). The State might argue the border-security deployment constitutes a "time of war" or military emergency that triggers the Third Amendment's wartime exception, under which quartering is permitted "in a manner to be prescribed by law"; the Executive Order would then count as "prescribed by law" (p. 10). The packet's model rebuttal: border-security operations, even militarized ones, are not "war" within the meaning of the Third Amendment — Franklin has not declared war, Congress has not declared war, and no armed hostilities are occurring; the Amendment distinguishes sharply between peacetime (absolute prohibition on nonconsensual quartering) and wartime (quartering permitted if prescribed by law); and expanding the wartime exception to cover domestic deployments would swallow the peacetime rule (p. 10).

### Issue 8 — counseling strategy

The packet's recommended course of action is a pre-enforcement challenge in the United States District Court for the District of Franklin, seeking a declaratory judgment that EO 2026-14 violates the Third Amendment (as incorporated) and a preliminary injunction barring enforcement before the March 16 effective date (p. 10). Federal court is preferable because the claim raises a federal constitutional question under 28 U.S.C. § 1331 and the *Abbott Laboratories* analysis supports immediate review (p. 10). A preliminary injunction is appropriate because irreparable harm is established — the loss of the fundamental privacy interest in one's home cannot be adequately compensated by money damages — and the merits are strong given the Third Amendment's near-absolute peacetime prohibition (p. 10).

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

The packet's question set works as a stand-alone hypothetical: the Quartering Order itself is the fact pattern, and Questions 1-8 are the counseling moves. The instructor notes identify three close calls students typically struggle with (pp. 14-15):

- *Incorporation of the Third Amendment.* The genuine open question; students must engage the *Duncan*/*McDonald* framework rather than asserting incorporation is obvious (pp. 14-15).
- *Ripeness before individual notification.* Students may reasonably argue non-ripeness given the lack of individual notification, but the better answer rests on the Order's criminal penalties and the home's clear inclusion in the geographic scope (p. 15).
- *The "obsolescence" problem.* The AG's "18th-century relic" line has superficial appeal; the strongest rebuttal is that infrequent invocation proves the norm's strength, and *McDonald* incorporated the similarly dormant Second Amendment (p. 15).

The packet also flags common errors (pp. 15-16): confusing the Third Amendment with the Fourth (quartering vs. search); arguing the $50/day cures the violation (it does not — peacetime quartering requires consent, not compensation); confusing ripeness with standing; assuming incorporation without engaging the framework; citing *Ohio Forestry* on the *wrong* side (the correct analogy is *Abbott Labs*); and agreeing with the AG's obsolescence argument without confronting the *McDonald*/Second Amendment parallel.

## Key Takeaways

- The Third Amendment is doctrinally thin but exam-rich because it cleanly isolates the incorporation question, the ripeness inquiry, and the peacetime/wartime textual structure.
- *Engblom v. Carey* (2d Cir. 1982) is the only federal appellate decision on incorporation, on the National-Guard-as-"Soldier" question, and on the extension of the right to tenants — students should know it cold.
- The *Duncan*/*McDonald* standard is the operative incorporation test, and the strongest pro-incorporation arguments are historical rootedness (the Quartering Acts and the Declaration grievance) and fundamental-to-ordered-liberty (sanctity of the home, link to Fourth Amendment values).
- *Abbott Laboratories* supplies the two-prong ripeness test; *Ohio Forestry* is the non-ripeness counter-example and a frequent student trap if cited on the wrong side.
- In peacetime, the Third Amendment is absolute as to consent — the $50/day compensation does not cure a violation, because the constitutional rule is consent, not compensation. The wartime exception requires actual war, not militarized law enforcement.
- The packet's recommended counseling move is a pre-enforcement declaratory-judgment and preliminary-injunction action in federal court under 28 U.S.C. § 1331.
