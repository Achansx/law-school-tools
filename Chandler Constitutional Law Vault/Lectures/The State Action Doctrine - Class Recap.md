The deck opens by framing the core question: is the challenged conduct fairly attributable to the State? *(slide 1)* The deck organizes the doctrine around five explicit pathways -- enforcement (Shelley), public function (Marsh yes, Lloyd no, Halleck no), entwinement/symbiosis (Burton, Brentwood), government-created entity (Lebron), and the non-pathway of inaction (DeShaney) *(slide 3)*. ## Lecture Outline

### The Foundation: Civil Rights Cases (1883) and Shelley v Kraemer (1948)

**The Fourteenth Amendment's limitation.** The Civil Rights Cases held that the Fourteenth Amendment is "prohibitory upon the States" -- it operates against state action, not private conduct *(slide 2)*. Congress cannot create "a code of municipal law for the regulation of private rights" *(slide 2)*. The 1875 Civil Rights Act, which prohibited discrimination in inns, theaters, and common carriers, was struck down on this ground *(slide 2)*.

The doctrinal residue: the same goal was achieved eighty years later through the Commerce Clause in the Civil Rights Act of 1964 *(slide 2)*. Heart of Atlanta Motel v United States (1964) upheld the Act as regulation of interstate commerce, not as a Fourteenth Amendment enforcement statute *(slide 2)*. The constitutional hook determines the regulatory reach.

**Shelley v Kraemer.** Racially restrictive covenants -- private contractual agreements -- are facially private; the covenants themselves do not involve state action *(slide 2)*. But judicial enforcement of those covenants turns private discrimination into state action: the state machinery (courts plus the recording system) is "doing the work" of discrimination *(slide 2)*.

### Five Pathways to (or Against) State Action *(slide 3)*

**(1) Enforcement.** The state lends its coercive authority to private discrimination *(slide 3)*. Shelley v Kraemer is the paradigm: court enforcement of a racially restrictive covenant *(slide 3)*. The state need not have enacted the discriminatory rule -- if it enforces or implements the private actor's discriminatory arrangement, it acts constitutionally.

**(2) Public Function.** A private entity exercises a function "traditionally and exclusively" performed by the government *(slide 3)*. Marsh v Alabama (1946): a company-owned town counted as a public function *(slide 3)*. Lloyd Corp v Tanner (1972): a privately owned shopping mall did not *(slide 3)*. Manhattan Community Access Corp v Halleck (2019): a nonprofit managing a cable-access channel did not -- operating a public forum is not a traditional and exclusive government function *(slide 3)*. The critical qualifier is "traditionally and exclusive" -- courts apply this standard narrowly.

**(3) Entwinement and Symbiosis.** The state and private actor are structurally bound together in a way that makes separation implausible *(slide 3)*. Burton v Wilmington Parking Authority (1961): a private restaurant in a municipally owned parking garage constituted state action through structural interdependence *(slide 3)*. Brentwood Academy v Tennessee Secondary School Athletic Assn (2001): a private athletic association whose board comprised officials from 84 percent of public schools was sufficiently entwined with the state to constitute a state actor *(slide 3)*.

**(4) Government-Created Entity.** Special creation plus continuing control *(slide 3)*. Lebron v National Railroad Passenger Corp (1995): Amtrak is a government entity for First Amendment purposes despite a federal statute characterizing it as a private corporation *(slide 3)*.

**(5) Inaction is Not State Action.** No constitutional duty to protect from private violence *(slide 3)*. DeShaney v Winnebago County (1989): county child protective services knew Joshua DeShaney's father was beating him and took no action; the Court held no constitutional violation because the Due Process Clause does not impose an affirmative duty on the government to protect citizens from private violence *(slide 3)*. Exception: custody or special relationship (prisoners, involuntary psychiatric patients) *(slide 3)*.

### The Post-Warren Court Contraction *(slide 4)*

The deck states the trend directly: since the demise of the Warren Court, the notion of what constitutes state action has shrunk *(slide 4)*. Expansion era: Shelley (1948), Marsh (1946), Burton (1961) *(slide 4)*. Contraction era: Lloyd (1972), Rendell-Baker (1982), Blum (1982), Halleck (2019) *(slide 4)*. Brentwood (2001) is the entwinement exception that kept the door ajar *(slide 4)*.

**Funding and regulation are not enough** *(slide 4)*. Rendell-Baker v Kohn: a private school received 92 percent of its operating budget from state sources and was extensively regulated by the state; still not a state actor for employment decisions *(slide 4)*. Blum v Yaretsky: a private nursing home received the overwhelming majority of its revenue from Medicaid; patient transfer decisions not state action *(slide 4)*.

The rule: the plaintiff must tie the specific challenged action to the state, not just the institution's general relationship *(slide 4)*. A global entanglement does not transform every decision into state action.

### The Franklin Hypothetical *(slide 5)*

The State of Franklin creates an Agricultural Quality Commission -- a body with governor-appointed members, state funding, and authority to certify produce *(slide 5)*. The Commission adopts a rule requiring that all certified produce be grown within Franklin *(slide 5)*. An out-of-state grower challenges the rule.

**Threshold: Is the Commission state action?** *(slide 5)* For state action under Brentwood/entwinement: governor-appointed board, state funding, operation within the state's regulatory framework *(slide 5)*. Against state action under Halleck/public function: agricultural certification is not a "traditional and exclusive" government function *(slide 5)*.

**If state action is established:** the rule facially discriminates against out-of-state producers; under the Dormant Commerce Clause, explicit discrimination against interstate commerce is virtually per se invalid *(slide 5)*. Courts almost never find that the Pike v Bruce Church balancing test would rescue a facially discriminatory rule *(slide 9)*; the per se invalidation framework is the correct analysis.

### No State Action Does Not Mean No Law *(slide 6)*

The deck's threshold reframe: "no state action" means the Constitution isn't directly prohibiting the private actor, not that no regulation exists; the regulation must come from another source of law *(slide 6)*. Alternative sources include:

**Commerce Clause.** Heart of Atlanta Motel/McClung: Congress banned private discrimination in public accommodations not as a Fourteenth Amendment violation but as regulation of interstate commerce *(slide 6)*. Congress achieved under the Commerce Clause what it could not achieve under § 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment.

**Spending Clause.** Congress attaches civil-rights conditions to federal funds, covering universities, hospitals, and contractors *(slide 6)*. Not a direct ban -- conditional regulation through the federal purse *(slide 6)*.

**Thirteenth Amendment.** No state-action requirement *(slide 6)*. Jones v Mayer: Congress can reach private racial subordination as "badges and incidents" of slavery, but the power is limited to race, not a roving federal power *(slide 6)*.

**Section 5 -- The Trap.** Students' most common error *(slide 6)*. Section 5 enforces the Fourteenth Amendment but, per Morrison, cannot create a general federal remedy for private violence -- it must target state violations and satisfy Boerne congruence *(slide 6)*.

**State police power.** States have the general police power and can regulate private conduct for health, safety, welfare, and morals without a special hook *(slide 7)*. The federal government is the one with limited powers; even clearing state action doesn't guarantee a remedy -- sovereign immunity, qualified immunity, and Monell create further obstacles *(slide 7)*. The deck's "exam move" frames the analytic sequence: first ask whether the private conduct is attributable to the state via the five pathways; if not, ask what other source of law can regulate it; finally ask what is gained or lost by proceeding outside the Constitution *(slide 7)*.

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

**The Voucher Strategy as State Action Problem.** The state action doctrine is what made the Massive Resistance playbook legally sophisticated: close public schools and fund private segregated academies. If education is private choice, it escapes the Fourteenth Amendment. Griffin v Prince Edward County (1964) rejected this move for districts with a history of state-enforced segregation -- a state that used coercive machinery to produce the segregated system cannot wash its hands by converting to private choice. The doctrinal boundary between Griffin and the general rule (no state action required to maintain an integrated school) remains contested.

**The Self-Test: The Hospital Problem.** A private hospital receives 85 percent of its revenue from Medicaid; it terminates a patient's care without a hearing; the patient sues under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 alleging a due process violation *(slide 8)*. The strongest basis for dismissal is Blum v Yaretsky: the patient must show the specific decision to terminate care was compelled or directed by the state, not merely that the hospital operates in a heavily funded and regulated environment *(slide 8)*. Funding and regulation alone do not make every decision state action; the nexus must run to the specific challenged decision *(slide 4)*.

## Key Takeaways

- The Fourteenth Amendment operates against the state, not private parties -- but the line between state and private action is porous and doctrinally contested.
- Five pathways to state action: judicial enforcement of private discrimination (Shelley), public function that is traditionally and exclusively governmental (Marsh yes, Lloyd no), entwinement/symbiosis (Burton, Brentwood), government-created entity with continuing control (Lebron), and the non-pathway of state inaction (DeShaney).
- Funding and regulation are not sufficient: the plaintiff must connect the state to the specific challenged decision, not merely the institutional relationship (Rendell-Baker, Blum).
- No state action means not this constitutional theory -- not that no regulation exists. Commerce Clause, Spending Clause, Thirteenth Amendment, and state police power remain available.
- The Section 5 trap: Section 5 enforces the Fourteenth Amendment's prohibitions on state actors; it cannot create federal remedies for purely private discrimination (Morrison).
