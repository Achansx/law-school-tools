# Sex Discrimination and Equal Protection

## Professor Emphasis

### From source

The deck organizes sex-discrimination doctrine as a four-arc structure: Arc I from divine ordinance to constitutional skepticism (Bradwell, Reed, Frontiero), Arc II the framework taking shape (Craig, Geduldig), Arc III boundaries and limits (Rodriguez, Cleburne, Footnote 4), and Arc IV the modern capstone (Virginia, Morales-Santana) *(slides 2, 9, 15, 19)*. Craig's intermediate-scrutiny holding is presented as the landmark sex-discrimination standard arriving in a case about young men buying low-alcohol beer — Ginsburg's deliberate ACLU strategy of choosing male plaintiffs to reframe sex discrimination as role-assignment that harms everyone *(slide 10)*. The Carolene Products / Footnote Four framework is flagged as unable to explain Craig, because young men buying beer are paradigmatic political insiders, not a discrete and insular minority; the anti-stereotyping principle is doing the work, not political-process theory *(slides 12, 30)*. Virginia is framed as tightening every component of intermediate scrutiny via the "exceedingly persuasive justification" gloss, with Scalia's dissent accusing Ginsburg of smuggling strict scrutiny in under intermediate scrutiny's name *(slides 21, 25)*. Geduldig's pregnancy/sex distinction is flagged as constitutionally surviving despite the 1978 Pregnancy Discrimination Act and as the framework that "will return in Skrmetti" *(slide 14)*.

### Editor's framing

This page treats the lecture as a doctrinal-evolution narrative organized around Ginsburg's anti-stereotyping principle, with the tier-of-scrutiny structure as the central puzzle. The Bayesian-prior reading of the tiers is the deck's own substantive theory, but the page's gloss that this reveals "the tier structure's instability" sharpens the deck's "disguised Lochnerism" prompt into a stronger editor's claim *(slide 31)*. Cross-references to Skrmetti and to the displaced-Lochnerism critique of tiers are editor's bridges to other lectures.

## Lecture Outline

**Arc I: From Divine Ordinance to Constitutional Skepticism**

Bradwell v. Illinois (1873) arose when Myra Bradwell, a married woman in Illinois, applied for a license to practice law and the Illinois Supreme Court denied her application *(slide 3)*. The majority (Miller, J.) ducked the sex question entirely by holding that the right to practice law is not a privilege of national citizenship — the same reasoning the Court applied in the Slaughter-House Cases that same year to confine the Privileges or Immunities Clause to a narrow set of federal rights *(slide 3)*. Bradley's concurrence — not the majority — is the opinion that matters for sex discrimination law *(slide 6)*. Bradley wrote that "the civil law, and nature itself, have always recognized a fundamental difference in the respective spheres and destinies of men and women. Man is woman's protector and defender" *(slide 4)*, and that "the very structure of the family, which rests on the divine ordinance and the nature of things itself, points to the domestic sphere as the proper domain of womanhood" *(slide 4)*. He added that "the natural and proper timidity and delicacy which belongs to the female sex evidently unfits it for many of the occupations of civil life" and that "the paramount destiny and mission of woman are to fulfil the noble and benign offices of wife and mother" is "the law of the Creator" *(slide 5)*. The lecture frames Bradley's concurrence as the "pedestal or the cage" tension — history was cloaked in language of protection, but the practical result was confinement, not uplift *(slide 6)*. Brennan's later Frontiero plurality would invoke the same image: "Not on a pedestal, but in a cage" *(slide 6)*.

The doctrinal silence lasted 98 years *(slide 6)*. Reed v. Reed (1971) was the first invalidation of a gender-based classification under Equal Protection *(slide 7)*. Idaho's mandatory preference for men over women as estate administrators fell because sex bore no rational relationship to estate-administration competence *(slide 7)*. Burger wrote for a unanimous Court, but no heightened scrutiny was formally announced — the opinion reads like rational basis with real bite, failing a test that almost nothing fails *(slide 7)*.

**The Push for Strict Scrutiny -- Frontiero v. Richardson (1973)**

Sharron Frontiero, a military officer, had to prove her husband was financially dependent to claim benefits; male officers faced no such requirement *(slide 8)*. Brennan's four-Justice plurality argued sex should be treated as a suspect classification like race: it is an immutable characteristic that "frequently bears no relation to ability to perform or contribute to society" *(slide 8)*. Four Justices would have declared sex a suspect classification requiring strict scrutiny *(slide 8)*. But Powell concurred separately: the ERA had been approved by Congress and was pending before the states; to declare sex a suspect class while the American people were deliberating the question "through proper democratic channels" seemed "premature" *(slide 8)*. Powell wanted institutional restraint *(slide 8)*. The ERA ultimately failed, and strict scrutiny for sex never arrived *(slide 8)*.

**Intermediate Scrutiny -- Craig v. Boren (1976)**

Oklahoma permitted females to buy 3.2% beer at 18 but required males to wait until 21 *(slide 10)*. The landmark sex-discrimination standard emerged in a case brought by a young man about low-alcohol beer — Ginsburg's strategic move, choosing male plaintiffs to frame sex discrimination as a system of role-assignment that harms all members of both sexes, not just women seeking access to male-dominated spheres *(slide 10)*. Earlier male-plaintiff cases included Weinberger v. Wiesenfeld (widower denied child-care benefits) and Califano v. Goldfarb (widower denied survivor benefits) *(slide 10)*. Brennan announced intermediate scrutiny: "Classifications by gender must serve important governmental objectives and must be substantially related to achievement of those objectives" *(slide 11)*. Oklahoma's statistics — 0.18% of females versus 2% of males arrested for drunk driving — could not justify a sex-based classification *(slide 11)*. The deck flags Brennan's arithmetic as bad math *(slide 11)*.

The lecture draws attention to the distance from Slaughter-House: the same Fourteenth Amendment that was originally read as existing primarily for the newly freed slaves now protects young men's beer-purchasing rights *(slide 12)*. Process theory (Carolene Products Footnote Four) cannot explain Craig, because young men buying beer are the paradigmatic political insiders, not a discrete and insular minority *(slide 12)*. The anti-stereotyping principle — the prohibition on relying on overbroad generalizations about the different roles and capacities of men and women — is doing the work *(slide 12)*. The symmetry principle Craig announces — Equal Protection protects all persons regardless of sex, whether the disadvantage falls on men or women — will echo through VMI and Morales-Santana *(slide 13)*.

**The Pregnancy Anomaly -- Geduldig v. Aiello (1974)**

California's disability insurance excluded pregnancy-related disabilities from coverage *(slide 14)*. Stewart, writing for the Court, held this was not sex discrimination: the program "divides potential recipients into two groups — pregnant women and nonpregnant persons. The first group is exclusively female; the second includes members of both sexes" *(slide 14)*. Only rational basis review applies *(slide 14)*. Brennan dissented: women face a limitation on sex-linked disabilities while men receive full compensation for all of theirs, and "such dissimilar treatment of men and women, on the basis of physical characteristics inextricably linked to one sex, inevitably constitutes sex discrimination" *(slide 14)*. The deck notes that men receive coverage for prostatectomies, circumcision, hemophilia, and gout, while women lose coverage for the one condition unique to them *(slide 14)*. Congress agreed with the dissent and passed the Pregnancy Discrimination Act (1978) amending Title VII, but Geduldig's constitutional holding stands *(slide 14)* — and the deck flags that it "will return in Skrmetti" *(slide 14)*.

**Arc III: Boundaries -- Rodriguez, Cleburne, and the Tier Structure**

San Antonio Independent School District v. Rodriguez (1973) closed off heightened scrutiny for wealth-based classifications and rejected education as a fundamental right *(slide 16)*. Powell's 5-4 majority held that wealth is not a suspect classification — the poor lack the traditional indicia of suspectness — and that education is not explicitly or implicitly guaranteed by the Constitution *(slide 16)*. Rational basis applies; Texas's system survives *(slide 16)*. The lecture frames this as the moment when the liberal hope that equal protection would address structural economic inequality died: the tiers of scrutiny froze, with wealth at rational basis, sex at intermediate, and race at strict *(slide 16)*.

City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center (1985): the city denied a special-use permit for a group home for people with intellectual disabilities, when the permit was required for such homes but not for fraternity houses, hospitals, or nursing homes *(slide 17)*. White's opinion refused to elevate disability to a quasi-suspect class — only rational basis applies — but struck the ordinance anyway because the city's justifications (negative neighbor attitudes, flood-plain concerns, student harassment) applied equally to other permitted uses *(slide 17)*. The city's reasons rested on "an irrational prejudice against the mentally retarded" — exactly the kind of animus rational basis is supposed to catch *(slide 17)*. Cleburne thus manages to fail a test that almost nothing fails — "rational basis with bite," an intermediate-in-effect standard that approximates heightened scrutiny in outcome *(slide 17)*. The Footnote Four paradox is highlighted: people with intellectual disabilities are a textbook discrete and insular minority — small, identifiable, with a long history of discrimination and limited political power — yet they receive only rational basis, while young men buying beer in Craig received intermediate scrutiny *(slide 18)*. The deck offers a Bayesian explanation: the Court believes disability-based classifications are sometimes rational (special education, guardianship), so the prior is weaker than for sex *(slide 18)*.

**Arc IV: Tightening Intermediate Scrutiny -- Virginia and Morales-Santana**

United States v. Virginia (1996): the Virginia Military Institute's 157-year all-male tradition of producing "citizen-soldiers" through an adversative "pressure cooker" method was challenged by the United States *(slide 20)*. Virginia's remedy — VWIL at Mary Baldwin College, with different method, different mission, far fewer resources, SAT scores ~100 points lower, fewer faculty doctorates, lower salaries, and no engineering — was dismissed as inadequate *(slide 20)*. The deck likens VWIL to TSUN in Sweatt v. Painter *(slide 20)*. Ginsburg, writing for a 7-1 majority (Rehnquist concurring in judgment, Scalia dissenting), announced that parties defending sex-based government action must demonstrate an "exceedingly persuasive justification" for the classification *(slide 21)*. "The justification must be genuine, not hypothesized or invented after litigation has begun. It cannot rest on post-hoc rationalizations. And it must not rely on overbroad generalizations about the different talents, capacities, or preferences of males and females" *(slide 21)*. While the formal test remained "important governmental objectives" plus "substantially related," Ginsburg tightened every component, and after Virginia, intermediate scrutiny for sex is more demanding than intermediate scrutiny for any other classification *(slide 21)*. Ginsburg's pivotal passage: "The notion that physical differences between the sexes remain 'enduring' is certainly true — the two sexes are not fungible. But inherent differences between men and women are no longer accepted as a ground for artificial constraints on an individual's opportunity" *(slide 22)*. And: "State actors controlling gates to opportunity may not exclude qualified individuals based on fixed notions concerning the roles and abilities of males and females" *(slide 23)*. VWIL fails because no amount of careful program design can replicate what takes generations to build — the separateness is not equal, and the unequal starting point cannot be overcome *(slide 24)*.

Scalia's dissent argued that "exceedingly persuasive justification" is not the test announced in Craig v. Boren and that Ginsburg was smuggling strict scrutiny in under intermediate scrutiny's name *(slide 25)*. The deck's counter-argument: the phrase first appeared in Hogan (1982), so Virginia applies rather than invents, and doctrinal evolution through emphasis is how common-law constitutional adjudication works *(slide 25)*.

Sessions v. Morales-Santana (2017) applied intermediate scrutiny to a federal citizenship statute imposing different residency requirements on unwed fathers versus unwed mothers *(slide 26)*. The differential requirements reflected impermissible assumptions about parental roles — Congress assumed unwed mothers will care for children while unwed fathers will not *(slide 26)*. Ginsburg leveled down rather than up: the longer physical-presence requirement now applies to all parents, because the one-year exception for unwed mothers was always the departure from Congress's general rule *(slide 27)*. The symmetry principle established in Craig — intermediate scrutiny applies regardless of which sex bears the disadvantage — was confirmed, and the anti-stereotyping principle was synthesized through a full doctrinal narrative (Reed, Frontiero, Weinberger, Goldfarb, Westcott) *(slide 28)*. The deck notes the line drawn against Nguyen v. INS (2001): biological differences yes, behavioral assumptions no *(slide 28)*.

**Why Intermediate Scrutiny? -- The Theoretical Puzzle**

The Carolene Products Footnote Four framework does not work for sex, the deck argues: women are a majority of the population, they vote, and they are not socially isolated from the legislative majority; men are even harder to explain (Craig, Weinberger, Orr) *(slide 30)*. Anti-stereotyping is doing the work — but anti-stereotyping is a substantive theory about what classifications are prohibited, not a structural theory about when courts should intervene *(slide 30)*.

The deck offers the Bayesian-prior theory as an alternative explanation *(slide 31)*. Rational basis = Court's prior is that classifications based on this trait are usually rational (defer to legislature); intermediate scrutiny = prior is that sex-based classifications are usually irrational but sometimes track real differences; strict scrutiny = prior is that race-based classifications are almost never rational *(slide 31)*. This explains why sex gets intermediate (not strict), why the doctrine protects men (the prior applies to the classification, not the group), and why the standard tightens over time as more classifications prove baseless (Reed → Virginia) *(slide 31)*. The deck flags the Lochner problem: this is a substantive judgment about legislative rationality hidden in a "level of scrutiny" label *(slide 31)*.

The closing arc summary: 1873 Bradley's "divine ordinance"; 1971 Reed's first crack; 1973 Frontiero's four-vote bid for strict scrutiny derailed by Powell's ERA-pending rationale; 1976 Craig's intermediate scrutiny via male plaintiffs buying beer; 1996 Virginia's "exceedingly persuasive justification"; 2017 Morales-Santana's full synthesis of the anti-stereotyping principle *(slide 32)*.

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

The lecture raises the Geduldig paradox as a forward reference to Skrmetti: if the State classifies by gender transition rather than by sex itself, does the Geduldig two-classes logic insulate the classification from heightened scrutiny? *(slide 14)*.

The Cleburne discussion poses the tier-proliferation problem: if the Court applies rational basis with bite to achieve intermediate-scrutiny outcomes for disability, what is the point of the tier structure? *(slide 18)*. Is rational basis with bite a secret third tier, or is it just honest intermediate scrutiny without the label? The displaced-Lochnerism critique of tiers (developed in a separate lecture) is foreshadowed here.

## Key Takeaways

- The evolution from Bradwell to Craig to Virginia spans over a century and reflects the Court gradually breaking from the naturalistic assumptions embedded in early sex-role jurisprudence.
- Intermediate scrutiny (Craig, 1976) requires classifications by gender to serve important governmental objectives and be substantially related -- a higher bar than rational basis but lower than strict scrutiny.
- Ginsburg's VMI opinion tightens intermediate scrutiny to require an "exceedingly persuasive justification" -- no post-hoc rationalization, no reliance on statistical generalizations.
- Geduldig's pregnancy logic is constitutionally anomalous and survives despite congressional rejection; it may resurface in transgender-classification cases.
- Rational basis with bite (Cleburne) approximates heightened scrutiny in outcome while refusing to create a new suspect class -- a doctrinal hedge that reveals the tier structure's instability.
