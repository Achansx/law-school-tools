## Lecture Outline

### Youngstown Sheet and Tube Co v Sawyer (1952) -- Review and Extension

The lecture revisits the main Youngstown themes with emphasis on three aspects less developed in the companion lecture.

**The Gridlock Problem.** The lecture frames the steel seizure partly as a product of congressional gridlock: Congress had considered and rejected giving the President seizure authority *(slide 3)*. The deck flags the gridlock problem alongside the question of "Relevance to DACA" and "Relevance to The Wall" *(slide 2)*. Jackson's twilight zone (Category 2) is specifically designed for this situation, but Taft-Hartley's express rejection of seizure pushed the seizure into Category 3. This has direct application to DACA and the border wall: when Congress considers and fails to enact a comprehensive immigration statute, is the executive branch in Category 2 (congressional silence, some flexibility) or Category 3 (Congress's failure to act implicitly incorporates the legislative status quo)?

**Frankfurter's Anti-Textualist Approach.** The "frog in warm water" theory: dangerous power does not accumulate in a day but comes "from the generative force of unchecked disregard of the restrictions that fence in even the most disinterested assertion of authority" *(slides 4-5)*. Frankfurter's gloss-of-practice principle: "a systematic, unbroken, executive practice, long pursued to the knowledge of the Congress and never before questioned … may be treated as a gloss on 'executive Power' vested in the President by § 1 of Art. II" *(slide 6)*. This is the deck's McCulloch acquiescence theme *(slide 6)*. Frankfurter declines to join Black's majority because Black's categorical formalism is too rigid -- the deck even flags Frankfurter's opinion as a possible "subtle response to Jackson's field theory of presidential power" *(slide 5)*.

**Jackson's Background and Methodology.** The deck flags Jackson's New Deal experience, including service as SG with a 44-6 record before the Supreme Court and as Nuremberg trial chief counsel *(slide 7)*. Jackson's three-part structure: actions pursuant to express or implied authorization of Congress, actions where Congress is silent, and measures incompatible with the expressed or implied will of Congress *(slide 8)*. Jackson warned that "emergency powers tend to kindle emergencies" -- Congress needs to step up *(slide 9)*.

**The Vinson Dissent and the Historical Catalog.** Vinson compiles an extensive catalog of unilateral executive action: Washington's response to the Whiskey Rebellion and his Proclamation of Neutrality *(slide 13)*; Jefferson's Louisiana Purchase, the Monroe Doctrine, and Jackson's removal of deposits from the Bank *(slide 14)*; Lincoln's troop summons, naval blockade of the Confederacy (the Prize Cases), seizure of rail and telegraph lines to Washington, and Emancipation Proclamation issued "wholly without statutory authority" *(slide 15)*; Hayes's use of troops to stop strikes and Taft's stopping public land purchases over a Congressional statute (approved in United States v. Midwest Oil, 1915) *(slide 16)*; and FDR's pre-Pearl Harbor seizures of an aviation plant, a shipbuilding plant, and an aircraft parts plant -- with then-Justice Jackson writing the defense *(slide 17)*. This catalog is raw material for the Dames factors (history of acquiescence).

### Dames and Moore v Regan (1981) -- Review

The lecture revisits Dames with emphasis on Rehnquist's methodological move *(slide 23)*: replacing Jackson's categorical "pigeonholes" with a spectrum (a retrofit reminiscent of Baker v. Carr) *(slide 23)*. The Dames factors: inferences from the character of legislation Congress has enacted; history of acquiescence; length and numerosity; congressional discussion without disapproval; and the unspoken factor that an opposite ruling "would lead to chaos" *(slide 24)*. The Belmont connection sets up the next section: the Algiers Accords were analogous to earlier executive settlement agreements like the Roosevelt-Litvinov Agreement.

### Missouri v Holland (1920)

**The Statutory History.** Congress enacted the Weeks-McLean Act (1913) to protect migratory birds by federal statute *(slide 25)*. Lower courts struck it down as beyond Congress's commerce power: "It is thus clear no reliance whatever may be placed by the plaintiff on the commerce clause of the Constitution to uphold the act in question" *(slide 27)*.

**The Treaty Solution.** Rather than amend the Constitution, Congress took a different route: the United States and Great Britain signed the Migratory Bird Treaty in 1916, agreeing to close hunting seasons from March 10 to September 1; the Senate ratified later that year *(slide 28)*. Congress then enacted implementing legislation.

**The Holding (Holmes, J.).** Treaty-making powers may have qualifications, but they must be ascertained "in a different way," because "it is obvious that there may be matters of the sharpest exigency for the national well being that an act of Congress could not deal with but that a treaty followed by such an act could" *(slide 30)*. Holmes's organic-constitution methodology: "when we are dealing with words that also are a constituent act, like the Constitution of the United States, we must realize that they have called into life a being the development of which could not have been foreseen completely by the most gifted of its begetters. It was enough for them to realize or to hope that they had created an organism; it has taken a century and has cost their successors much sweat and blood to prove that they created a nation" *(slide 31)*. The treaty "does not contravene any prohibitory words to be found in the Constitution"; the only question is whether the Tenth Amendment's "invisible radiation" blocks it *(slide 31)*. A national interest "of very nearly the first magnitude" can be protected only by national action in concert with another power *(slide 31)*.

**The Structural Implication.** Congress can assert power as against the states via treaty that it cannot do via ordinary law *(slide 32)*. This is a significant expansion of federal power: the treaty power becomes a workaround for Commerce Clause limitations.

**The Reid v. Covert Qualification.** The deck flags this with a hypothetical: "Do you mean that the US could treat with Germany to dispense with protections of the bill of rights? No! Missouri v. Holland 'just' involved the 10th amendment" *(slide 32)*.

### United States v Belmont (1937)

**The Facts.** The Bolsheviks took over and confiscated private property in 1917; the USSR couldn't reach property that Russian businesses such as Petrograd Metal Works had squirreled away in American banks like August Belmont & Co. ($25,000) *(slide 33)*.

**The Roosevelt-Litvinov Agreement.** FDR and the Soviet Commissar of Foreign Affairs, Mr. Litvinov, exchanged letters in which the USSR assigned to the U.S. all claims it had by virtue of the 1918 confiscation, the U.S. agreed to use that money to help settle up disputes with the USSR, and the two countries recognized each other as legitimate governments *(slide 34)*. The deck flags that there was no "emergency" *(slide 34)*. The creditors' claims were otherwise governed by New York law *(slide 35)*.

**The Holding.** "An international compact, as this was, is not always a treaty which requires the participation of the Senate. There are many such compacts, of which a protocol, a modus vivendi, a postal convention, and agreements like that now under consideration are illustrations" *(slide 36)*. "Plainly, the external powers of the United States are to be exercised without regard to state laws or policies … State Constitutions, state laws, and state policies are irrelevant to the inquiry and decision" *(slide 36)*.

**The Structural Implication.** Not only can Congress use treaties to expand federal power; the federal executive can use Executive Agreements "to expand federal power vis-à-vis states and bypass the super-majority check the Constitution imposes for treaties" *(slide 38)*.

**The Extraterritoriality Note.** The Court rejected a takings objection because "our Constitution, laws, and policies have no extraterritorial operation, unless in respect of our own citizens" *(slide 37)*. The deck flags Guantanamo as an open question *(slide 37)* -- Boumediene v. Bush (2008) later held that the Suspension Clause applies to Guantanamo precisely because the United States exercises de facto sovereignty there.

### Treaty Termination (Goldwater v Carter, 1979)

**The Question.** Historical practice is mixed: Congress (not George Washington) declared the country "freed and exonerated" from the 1778 Alliance with France; there was a 1911 Senate debate about the power to terminate an 1832 treaty with Russia; while the Senate was debating, in 1939 FDR terminated the 1911 Treaty of Commerce and Navigation with Japan *(slide 39)*.

**The Facts.** In 1979 President Carter terminated the defense treaty with the Republic of China (Taiwan) as part of recognizing the PRC as the government of China; Senator Barry Goldwater challenged *(slide 39)*.

**The Holding.** The Supreme Court refused to hear the case: four votes saying it was a political question, two saying it might be justiciable if the Senate had formally objected to the termination *(slide 39)*. The question of unilateral treaty termination by the President remains formally unresolved.

### Live Applications

**DACA.** The lecture asks students to classify DACA under the Jackson framework -- a Category 2/3 question the deck flags directly on slide 2 alongside "Relevance to The Wall" *(slide 2)*. The DACA cases (including Biden v. Nebraska's major questions analysis) track this exact question.

**The Border Wall Emergency Declaration (2019).** Trump declared a national emergency at the southern border under sections 201 and 301 of the National Emergencies Act and section 2808 of title 10, invoking construction authority *(slides 40-42)*. The administration argued that the border presented a security and humanitarian crisis constituting a national emergency *(slide 41)*. The deck lists the legal issues: presidential authority to direct the military, authority to build wall with existing funds, authority to divert funds from other sources, property-owner rights, environmental issues, and treaties *(slide 43)*. The Fifth Circuit was identified as the relevant venue *(slide 45)*.

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

**The DACA Question.** Classify DACA under Jackson. The lecture asks: when Congress passes no comprehensive immigration reform, has it "expressed or implied" its will that the executive not act (Category 3 -- the Senate bill that died in the House might count as express rejection), or is the field simply unoccupied (Category 2 -- the President has flexibility to fill the vacuum with executive policy)? Biden v. Nebraska's major questions analysis adds a further constraint: even in Category 2, executive action of "vast economic or political significance" requires a clear congressional statement.

**The Missouri v. Holland Extension Problem.** If the treaty power allows Congress to regulate migratory birds over Tenth Amendment objections, can the treaty power allow Congress to regulate purely domestic matters if a foreign sovereign is willing to sign a treaty about them? Where are the limits? Reid v. Covert provides one limit (individual rights are not subject to treaty waiver), but what stops a sufficiently creative administration from using treaties to accomplish whatever domestic regulation it wants?

**The Executive Agreement Bypass.** Belmont allows the President to preempt state law via executive agreement without Senate ratification. What stops a President from circumventing the two-thirds Treaty Clause requirement on all significant international matters? Is there a principled distinction between an executive agreement and a treaty? The lecture identifies the practical answer (the Senate can refuse to implement, Congress can withdraw funding, foreign governments may demand the treaty form for enforceability) but acknowledges no firm constitutional line.

## Key Takeaways

- The foreign affairs domain gives executive power its greatest scope. The combination of Missouri v. Holland (treaty power expands federal authority beyond ordinary legislation) and Belmont (executive agreements have the same effect as treaties) means the President can accomplish through international channels what Congress alone could not authorize domestically.
- Frankfurter's gloss-of-practice principle is explicitly anti-textualist: constitutional meaning is supplemented by long-standing institutional practice that Congress has known about and not challenged.
- The treaty power is not unlimited: Reid v. Covert establishes that treaties cannot override individual constitutional rights. Missouri v. Holland "just" involved the Tenth Amendment's reserved powers.
- Emergency claims must be scrutinized: Jackson's "emergency powers tend to kindle emergencies" warning applies as much to foreign affairs emergencies as domestic ones. The President's emergency declarations (border wall, IEEPA tariffs) are live applications.
- Treaty termination remains a constitutional gap: Goldwater v. Carter left the question formally unresolved, and executive practice has proceeded in the assumption that the President has unilateral authority -- an assumption Congress has chosen not to test.
- The gridlock problem is structurally central: when Congress is unable to legislate on a pressing issue (immigration, trade, defense), courts must decide whether executive action fills a vacuum (Category 2 flexibility) or usurps a legislative function Congress implicitly chose to leave unexercised (Category 3 prohibition).
