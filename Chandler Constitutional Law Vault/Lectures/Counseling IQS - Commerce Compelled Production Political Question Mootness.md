# Counseling IQS - Commerce Compelled Production Political Question Mootness

<!-- 2026-05-26: stub completion under convention -->

## Professor Emphasis

### From source

The packet is a single five-page client-counseling problem built around Marcus Chen, sole owner of Precision Silicon Corp. (PSC), a Franklin semiconductor fabricator confronting a federal "production mandate" under the (fictional) Critical Commodities Stabilization Act (CCSA) (p. 1). The problem layers three doctrinal angles onto one fact pattern: (1) a Commerce Clause challenge to a statute that compels a manufacturer to make goods it would not otherwise produce (p. 2); (2) a political question defense that the existence of a "national economic emergency" is textually committed to the President (p. 2); and (3) a mootness fight after the President rescinded the underlying emergency declaration two weeks before the questions are answered (p. 2). The packet's chosen authorities are tightly curated to telegraph the doctrinal moves: NFIB v. Sebelius for the compel-versus-regulate distinction (p. 3), Gonzales v. Raich for the comprehensive-regulatory-scheme rescue (p. 3), Baker v. Carr for the political question factors (p. 3), and Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw for voluntary-cessation mootness (p. 3).

### Editor's framing

This page treats the packet as a counseling exercise rather than a doctrinal lecture: the deliverable is what to tell Chen, not a synthesis of the cited cases. The four doctrines are not equally weighted — the packet front-loads the Commerce Clause issue (one multiple-choice and one Raich/NFIB short-answer slot) and gives mootness comparable space (one multiple-choice and one short-answer slot), while political question gets a single short-answer and the "compelled production" label in the title is really a substantive Commerce Clause label, not a Fifth-Amendment-style documentary-compulsion problem. The original stub's front matter listed cases (Lopez, Nixon v. United States, DeFunis v. Odegaard) that do not appear in the packet; the front matter has been corrected to match the four authorities the packet actually supplies.

## The Client Scenario

PSC is a small semiconductor fabrication company incorporated in Franklin and operating a single facility in Franklin City (p. 1). PSC manufactures specialty analog chips used in medical devices and sells exclusively to three medical-device manufacturers, all located in Franklin (p. 1). PSC has never sold products outside Franklin, has no out-of-state customers, and does not advertise outside the state (p. 1).

Six months ago, Congress enacted the Critical Commodities Stabilization Act, which authorizes the President to declare a "national economic emergency" with respect to any commodity the President determines is "essential to the economic security of the United States" (p. 1). On declaration, the Secretary of Commerce may issue production mandates requiring domestic manufacturers of the designated commodity to produce specified quantities at prices set by the Department, with $50,000-per-day civil penalties and potential criminal sanctions for noncompliance (p. 1).

Three months ago, the President declared a national economic emergency for semiconductors, citing global supply-chain disruptions and national security concerns (p. 1). The Secretary issued a mandate requiring PSC to produce 40,000 units per quarter of a standardized logic chip PSC has never manufactured; retooling would cost roughly $2.3 million and would require suspending PSC's existing specialty-chip production for at least four months (p. 1).

PSC sued in the District of Franklin on the theory that the Commerce Clause does not authorize Congress to compel an unwilling manufacturer to produce goods it would not otherwise produce (p. 2). The government moved to dismiss, arguing in part that the existence of a "national economic emergency" is a nonjusticiable political question (p. 2). After the District Court denied the motion and signaled skepticism about the Commerce Clause defense at a preliminary-injunction hearing, the President — two weeks before Chen's consultation — rescinded the semiconductor emergency declaration, and the Secretary suspended all production mandates including PSC's (p. 2). The government has now moved to dismiss as moot (p. 2).

The CCSA itself remains in effect, the President retains statutory authority to redeclare the emergency at any time, and the President's press-conference statement promised reinstatement if supply-chain conditions deteriorate (p. 2). Chen wants to know whether the Commerce Clause challenge has merit, whether the political question argument has any force, and whether the case can survive the mootness motion (p. 2).

## Issue-Spotting by Doctrine

### Commerce Clause: compel vs. regulate

The packet's central Commerce Clause move is the compulsion-versus-regulation distinction from NFIB v. Sebelius (p. 3). The supplied NFIB excerpt frames the question as whether Congress can regulate an entity "precisely because" it is not engaged in the activity Congress wants to compel: the Framers gave Congress "the power to regulate commerce, not to compel it" (p. 3). The packet's facts are deliberately structured to invite this move — PSC is not selling out of state, the federal command is to produce new goods PSC has never produced, and the operative verb in the statute is "compel."

The government's likely response, also previewed in the packet, is Gonzales v. Raich's comprehensive-regulatory-scheme doctrine: Congress may reach purely intrastate non-commercial activity when failure to do so would undercut regulation of an interstate market (p. 3). The exam tension is whether a production mandate is regulatory machinery embedded in a larger interstate semiconductor regime (Raich-style) or an act of conscription that creates rather than regulates commerce (NFIB-style). Question 5 puts this Raich-versus-NFIB contest into the answer template directly (p. 5).

A secondary Commerce Clause angle hides in Question 1's distractors (p. 4): a pure jurisdictional argument that PSC has no interstate-commerce nexus, a Tenth Amendment "traditional state function" argument, and a nondelegation/"intelligible principle" argument. The packet treats none of these as the strongest argument — the strongest argument is the NFIB compel-versus-regulate framing — but the spotted doctrines are still on the table.

### Political question doctrine

The political-question issue is framed narrowly: does the existence vel non of a "national economic emergency" fall under a Baker v. Carr factor (p. 3)? The supplied Baker excerpt gives the two factors that matter for this packet: a "textually demonstrable constitutional commitment of the issue to a coordinate political department" and "a lack of judicially discoverable and manageable standards" (p. 3). Question 4 asks for one argument on each side (p. 5).

The government's textual-commitment argument leans on the CCSA's statutory delegation to the President and the President's foreign-affairs / national-security primacy. The against argument leans on the absence of any constitutional commitment — the CCSA is a statutory grant, not a constitutional one — and on the manageability of asking whether the President's determination was based on the statutory factors (supply-chain vulnerabilities, foreign dependency, potential for significant economic disruption) the Act itself enumerates (p. 2).

### Mootness — voluntary cessation and capable-of-repetition

The mootness angle is the packet's most fact-intensive piece. The Laidlaw excerpt supplies the operative test: a case may become moot only if subsequent events make it "absolutely clear" the wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur, and the "heavy burden" of persuasion is on the party asserting mootness (p. 3).

Question 2 (multiple choice) frames the choice as a contest between voluntary cessation (B) and capable-of-repetition-yet-evading-review (A), with two distractors that misstate the doctrine (C, treating already-incurred costs as defeating mootness; D, treating a facial CCSA challenge as the live case) (p. 4). Question 3 (short answer) then asks for two specific facts that support a voluntary-cessation argument (p. 4) — the obvious candidates are (i) the President's express press-conference reservation of the right to reinstate (p. 2), and (ii) the CCSA's continued existence with the President's unrestricted statutory authority to redeclare (p. 2). The rescission's tactical timing — two weeks after the District Court signaled skepticism — is a third supporting fact, though the packet does not require it as one of the two.

## The Counseling Frame

Question 6 asks Chen what to tell the client: should PSC pursue the lawsuit or accept the rescission and move on? (p. 5). The packet directs the student to identify one specific reason to continue and explain it. The packet itself does not supply an answer; reasons available on the facts include the $2.3 million retooling exposure already incurred or threatened (p. 1), the in-terrorem effect of the CCSA's continued validity, and the value of a merits ruling as insurance against redeclaration. As an issue-spotting exercise this is not a doctrinal question — it is a risk-disclosure-and-strategy question that tests whether the student can translate the surviving litigation posture into client-facing advice.

## Key Takeaways

- The exam template here is one fact pattern, three doctrines (Commerce Clause, political question, mootness), and four supplied authorities — the answer is in the cases the packet hands you (p. 3).
- Commerce Clause analysis is keyed to NFIB's compel-versus-regulate distinction, with Raich as the government's rescue (pp. 3, 5).
- Political-question analysis is keyed to two Baker factors — textual commitment to a coordinate branch and judicially manageable standards (p. 3).
- Mootness analysis is keyed to Laidlaw voluntary-cessation: the moving party carries a heavy burden, and the President's press-conference reservation plus the CCSA's continued validity are the two strongest non-mootness facts (pp. 2, 3).
- The counseling question (Question 6) is risk-and-strategy, not pure doctrine: identify a concrete reason for Chen to keep litigating (p. 5).
