## Lecture Outline

**What Is the Dormant Commerce Clause?**

Article I, § 8 grants Congress power to regulate interstate commerce, and courts have implied a negative command: states cannot enact laws that unduly burden or discriminate against interstate trade *(slide 2)*. The three core prohibitions are discrimination (laws favoring in-state over out-of-state interests), undue burden (burdens on commerce exceeding local benefits), and extraterritoriality (regulating commerce wholly outside state borders) *(slide 2)*. The deck stresses that the antidiscrimination principle lies at the "very core" of the doctrine, quoting National Pork Producers Council v. Ross directly *(slide 2)*.

**The Traditional Framework**

The pre-2023 framework had three buckets *(slide 3)*: facial discrimination against out-of-state interests triggered strict scrutiny and was virtually per se invalid; Pike balancing applied when a law was facially neutral but imposed incidental burdens, asking whether those burdens were "clearly excessive" relative to local benefits; and extraterritoriality, derived from Healy v. Beer Institute (1989), treated state statutes regulating wholly out-of-state commerce as per se violations -- though the deck flags the status of this third bucket after Pork Producers as "unclear" *(slide 3)*.

**The Internet's Unique Challenge**

The internet "does not recognize geographic boundaries," making it "difficult, if not impossible, for a state to regulate internet activities without projecting its legislation into other States" *(slide 4)*. The deck identifies three structural effects: compliance impossibility (content is accessible to all states; the operator cannot selectively block by geography); the "leveling up" effect (the most restrictive state's law becomes a de facto national standard); and inconsistent obligations across 50 potentially conflicting regulatory regimes *(slide 4)*.

**Early Cases: Per Se Extraterritoriality**

Before 2023, courts treated state internet regulations as per se violations *(slide 5)*. American Libraries Ass'n v. Pataki (S.D.N.Y. 1997) struck a New York statute criminalizing internet transmission of indecent material to minors because the internet was "wholly insensitive to geographic distinctions" *(slide 5)*. American Booksellers Foundation v. Dean (2d Cir. 2003) struck a Vermont harmful-to-minors statute because Vermont "projected its legislation into other States, and directly regulated commerce therein" *(slide 5)*. ACLU v. Johnson (10th Cir. 1999) struck a New Mexico internet obscenity statute as "an attempt to regulate interstate conduct occurring outside New Mexico's borders... a per se violation of the Commerce Clause" *(slide 5)*.

**National Pork Producers Council v. Ross (2023) -- The Game-Changer**

California's Proposition 12 prohibited the sale of pork from pigs whose housing did not meet certain animal welfare standards, even if raised entirely outside California *(slide 6)*. Pork producers argued the law unconstitutionally regulated extraterritorial farming practices *(slide 6)*. The Court rejected any "almost per se rule forbidding enforcement of state laws that have the practical effect of controlling commerce outside the State," holding that extraterritorial effects alone are insufficient to invalidate state regulations *(slide 6)*.

**What Pork Producers Changed**

Before Pork Producers, extraterritorial effects could trigger per se invalidity, internet regulation was seen as inherently extraterritorial, states had limited power to regulate online conduct, and Pike balancing was often bypassed *(slide 7)*. After Pork Producers, extraterritoriality is limited to price-control contexts, discrimination is the "core" of the doctrine, plaintiffs must show "substantial burdens" on interstate commerce, and geographic filtering may enable state-specific compliance *(slide 7)*.

**Post-Pork Producers Internet Cases**

Lower courts have begun upholding state internet regulations *(slide 8)*. Free Speech Coalition v. Knudsen (D. Mont. 2024) upheld a Montana age-verification law for websites with "harmful to minors" content, rejecting the argument that "any regulation of the internet is effectively a regulation governing commerce across the entire nation" as "not tenable," with geographic filtering enabling compliance *(slide 8)*. Defense Distributed v. Platkin (D.N.J. 2023) upheld New Jersey's ban on digital firearm manufacturing files, reasoning that the dormant Commerce Clause does not prevent a state from criminalizing behavior originating in another state that might produce detrimental effects within the state, and that web providers "frequently control content flows from state to state" *(slide 8)*.

**Post-Pork Producers Analytical Framework**

The deck's four-step framework *(slide 9)*: (1) Discrimination Analysis -- does the law discriminate against out-of-state interests; facial discrimination triggers strict scrutiny; (2) Extraterritoriality Assessment -- does the law tie in-state conditions to out-of-state prices; after Pork Producers, mere effects are insufficient; (3) Pike Balancing -- plaintiff must allege substantial burdens on interstate commerce as a whole, not just on particular firms; (4) Technological Feasibility -- can geographic filtering enable state-specific compliance; if yes, the extraterritoriality argument weakens.

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

The deck uses California's Proposition 12 as the anchor *(slide 6)*: a state welfare standard for farm products sold in-state that has the practical effect of imposing requirements on farms in other states. The technological-feasibility step *(slide 9)* poses the discussion question: if geographic filtering identifies a user's state and blocks content accordingly, can the state still be said to be regulating commerce wholly outside its borders?

This page extends the "leveling up" effect *(slide 4)* into the normative question of whether it is appropriate for the most restrictive state to set de facto national standards in the internet age, or whether that undermines the constitutional design of a unified national market -- an editorial sharpening of the deck's brief leveling-up bullet.

## Key Takeaways

- Pork Producers fundamentally changed dormant Commerce Clause analysis by rejecting the broad extraterritoriality doctrine.
- Discrimination against out-of-state economic interests remains the "core" of the doctrine and must always be analyzed first.
- State internet regulations are no longer per se invalid; courts now apply fact-specific Pike balancing.
- Technology matters: geographic filtering may enable state-specific compliance, weakening extraterritoriality arguments.
- Frame challenges in terms of discrimination and substantial burden on interstate commerce as a whole, not extraterritorial effects alone.
