## Lecture Outline

### I. The Article III Baseline

Article III limits federal judicial power to "Cases" and "Controversies" -- a constitutional limit on which disputes courts may adjudicate, not a formality *(p. 2)*. The deck flags three major justiciability doctrines that enforce this limit, with its focus explicitly on the constitutional (not prudential) limitations *(p. 2)*:

- **Standing**: Does this plaintiff have a sufficient personal stake in the outcome? *(p. 3)*
- **Ripeness**: Is the controversy sufficiently developed for judicial resolution now? *(p. 9)*
- **Mootness**: Has the controversy been resolved, so that a judicial decision would have no effect? *(p. 11)*

The deck adds the **political question doctrine** (Baker v. Carr factors) and **adequate-and-independent state grounds** (Michigan v. Long) as additional constitutional limits *(pp. 17-19)*. Advisory opinions are categorically prohibited.

---

### II. Standing: The Lujan Test

***Lujan v Defenders of Wildlife* (1992):** Plaintiffs challenged a regulation interpreting the Endangered Species Act not to apply to projects funded by the U.S. abroad; the plaintiffs planned to visit project sites "someday" but had no concrete travel plans *(p. 4)*.

**Holding (Scalia, J.):** Plaintiffs lacked standing. The Court articulated the three constitutional requirements *(p. 4)*:

1. **Injury-in-fact**: The plaintiff must have suffered a concrete and particularized invasion of a legally protected interest, actual or imminent *(p. 4)*. Speculative or hypothetical future injuries do not qualify; the "someday" affidavits were insufficient *(p. 4)*.

2. **Causation/Traceability**: The injury must be fairly traceable to the defendant's challenged action, not the result of independent third-party action *(p. 4)*.

3. **Redressability**: It must be likely (not merely speculative) that a favorable judicial decision will remedy the injury *(p. 4)*.

**Concrete injury (Spokeo, Inc. v. Robins, 2016):** A bare procedural violation, without concrete harm, is insufficient -- Spokeo sued for FCRA violations alleging inaccurate credit report information, and the Court held the procedural violation alone did not establish standing *(p. 5)*. Intangible injuries can be concrete (e.g., reputational harm, emotional distress) *(p. 5)*.

**Particularized injury (TransUnion LLC v. Ramirez, 2021):** The plaintiff must be personally and individually affected, not the bearer of a generalized grievance shared by all citizens *(p. 6)*. Class members whose reports were not disseminated to third parties lacked standing because they suffered no concrete harm *(p. 6)*.

**Exception 1 -- Third-Party Standing (Singleton v. Wulff, 1976):** Doctors challenged a state law excluding abortion costs from Medicaid, arguing the rights of pregnant women; the three-part test requires (1) the plaintiff's own injury in fact, (2) a close relationship with the third party, and (3) a hindrance to the third party suing on their own behalf *(p. 7)*.

**Exception 2 -- Organizational Standing (Hunt v. Washington State Apple Advertising Comm'n, 1977):** An organization can sue on behalf of its members if (1) at least one member would have standing individually, (2) the interests are germane to the organization's purpose, and (3) individual member participation is not required *(p. 8)*.

**Editor's note on prudential limits:** Standing doctrine also includes court-imposed limits not reproduced on these slides -- no third-party standing (Singleton's exception aside), no generalized grievances (e.g., *U.S. v. Richardson* on the CIA budget), and a zone-of-interests requirement under particular statutes. *Flast v. Cohen* (1968) carved a narrow taxpayer-standing exception for Establishment Clause challenges to congressional Taxing and Spending Clause exercises; later cases (*Hein*, *Winn*) declined to expand it. The deck itself does not catalog these prudential moves under the "constitutional limitations" focus it set on slide 2.

### III. Ripeness: Abbott Labs Two-Part Test

***Abbott Laboratories v Gardner* (1967):** Drug manufacturers challenged an FDA regulation requiring them to list generic names of drugs on labels before the regulation was enforced *(p. 10)*.

**Holding:** The case was ripe. The Court articulated a two-part test *(p. 10)*:

1. **Fitness of the issue for judicial decision**: Are the issues purely legal? Purely legal issues are more fit for judicial review; the court asks whether it would benefit from further factual development *(p. 10)*.

2. **Hardship from withholding review**: Is the hardship direct and immediate if review is delayed? Does the challenged action create a dilemma now -- e.g., comply or risk penalties? *(p. 10)*

In *Abbott Labs*, the manufacturers faced the immediate practical dilemma of either reprinting all their labels (at great expense) or violating the regulation and risking enforcement -- this hardship weighed strongly for ripeness.

**Contrast (editor's note):** Pure "threat of future enforcement" without immediate hardship or a fully developed legal question may not be ripe. *United Public Workers v Mitchell*: employees who had not violated the Hatch Act had no ripe controversy because the threat of enforcement was speculative. The deck itself does not name this case.

### IV. Mootness and Its Exceptions

A case must present a live "case or controversy" at all stages of litigation; federal courts decide ongoing disputes, not resolved matters *(p. 11)*. The deck enumerates three exceptions to the general rule that moot cases are dismissed *(pp. 12-13)*.

**Editor's note -- *DeFunis v Odegaard* (1974):** This page treats DeFunis as the canonical mootness illustration -- a law student whose challenge to his rejection became moot when he was about to graduate regardless of outcome. The deck does not name DeFunis; the slides illustrate the doctrine through the trademark and election-law lead cases below.

**Voluntary cessation exception (*Already, LLC v. Nike, Inc.*, 2013):** When a defendant stops the challenged conduct but could restart, the case is moot only if there is no reasonable expectation of recurrence *(p. 12)*. Nike sued Already for trademark infringement, then declared it would never challenge Already's trademarks; the Court held Nike's claim moot because there was no reasonable expectation of Already resuming the challenged behavior *(p. 12)*.

**Editor's note on *Friends of the Earth v Laidlaw* (2000):** Laidlaw argued mootness because it had closed the facility during litigation; the Court applied the voluntary cessation test stringently: Laidlaw failed to show the violations would not recur. The deck names Already as its lead voluntary-cessation case, not Laidlaw.

**Capable-of-repetition yet evading review exception (*FEC v. Wisconsin Right to Life*, 2007):** The issue is too short to litigate fully before ending, and there is a reasonable expectation that the same party will be subjected to the same action again *(p. 13)*. WRTL challenged FEC regulations restricting "electioneering communications" close to elections; short election cycles meant challenges could become moot before judicial review was completed *(p. 13)*.

**Class actions exception (*U.S. Parole Comm'n v. Geraghty*, 1980):** A class action is not moot if the named plaintiff's claim is moot but the class members' claims remain live *(p. 13)*.

## Key Takeaways

- **Standing** (constitutional): injury-in-fact (concrete + particularized + actual or imminent), causation (fairly traceable to defendant), redressability (likely that a favorable decision redresses). All three required.
- **Standing** (prudential): no third-party standing, no generalized grievances, zone of interests. These can be waived or overridden by Congress.
- **Taxpayer standing (*Flast*)**: narrow exception for Establishment Clause + Taxing and Spending Clause. Does not extend to challenges to executive spending.
- **Ripeness (*Abbott Labs*)**: fitness for decision + hardship from withholding review. Pre-enforcement challenges can be ripe when the legal issue is clear and compliance imposes immediate costs.
- **Mootness**: controversy must remain live throughout litigation. Two key exceptions: (1) voluntary cessation (defendant must prove conduct absolutely will not recur); (2) capable of repetition yet evading review.
- Justiciability doctrine determines who gets into federal court and what claims courts will hear -- it is not a neutral procedural filter but a substantive shaping force on constitutional law.
