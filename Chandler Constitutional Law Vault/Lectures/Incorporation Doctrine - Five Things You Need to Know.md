## Lecture Outline

**I. Thing 1: Barron → Slaughterhouse → Due Process Workaround**

The problem has three chapters. Barron v. Baltimore (1833) established that the Bill of Rights constrains only the federal government (slide 2). The Fourteenth Amendment's Privileges or Immunities Clause was the natural fix (slide 2). Slaughter-House Cases (1873) gutted P&I, reducing it to trivial federal rights (slide 2). Cruikshank (1876) slammed the door: the First and Second Amendments do not apply to states (slide 2).

The workaround: CB&Q Railroad (1897) reframed just compensation as "inherent in due process" (slide 2). Gitlow v. New York (1925) "assumed" free speech is protected by the 14th Amendment -- a revolutionary shift buried in an aside (slide 2). The Court routed incorporation through the Due Process Clause, sidestepping Slaughterhouse entirely, and Slaughterhouse was never overruled -- just silently abandoned (slide 2).

**II. Thing 2: The Incorporation Test — From Palko to McDonald**

Palko v. Connecticut (1937): a right is incorporated only if "neither liberty nor justice would exist if [it] were sacrificed" -- the negative test asks whether a "fair and enlightened system of justice" would be "impossible" without it (slide 3). The deck characterizes this as a universality test: Cardozo asks whether ANY civilized society could function without the right; under this demanding standard, jury trial and double jeopardy protection failed (slide 3).

Duncan v. Louisiana (1968) shifted to "fundamental to the American scheme of justice," a move "from universal to national" that is "quiet but significant" -- the question is no longer whether any civilized society needs the right, but whether American legal traditions include it (slide 3). Under this relaxed standard, jury trial was incorporated, and the jot-for-jot rule attached: incorporated rights apply with full federal force (slide 3).

McDonald v. City of Chicago (2010) added "deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition" -- "explicitly parochial rather than cosmopolitan" -- in incorporating the Second Amendment (slide 3). Thomas's concurrence urged returning to P&I, calling substantive due process "a jurisprudence devoid of a guiding principle"; Gorsuch joined this position in Timbs (2019) (slide 3).

**III. Thing 3: Three Theories — Only One Won**

Selective Incorporation (THE WINNER) via Palko/Duncan/McDonald: rights are incorporated one by one if "fundamental to ordered liberty," most of the Bill of Rights now applies to states, and jot-for-jot means full federal force once incorporated (slide 4).

Total Incorporation (NEVER ADOPTED) was Justice Black's position in his Adamson dissent (1947): all of Amendments 1-8 apply, period; a bright-line rule with no judicial discretion and no room for unenumerated rights -- Black opposed SDP (slide 4).

Fundamental Fairness (REJECTED) was Frankfurter's approach in Rochin, with Harlan II: due process = case-by-case "shocks the conscience," no jot-for-jot so states could vary from federal standard, and overlap with the Bill of Rights was "largely coincidental" (slide 4).

**IV. Thing 4: The Deep Tension**

Incorporation runs through Due Process -- "a clause about 'process' protecting substantive rights" -- which entangles it with substantive due process (Lochner, Griswold, Roe, Obergefell) (slide 5). If SDP is illegitimate, incorporation is theoretically illegitimate too (slide 5). Thomas (McDonald) and Gorsuch (Timbs) urge using P&I instead -- at least two votes to revisit (slide 5). Dobbs complicates things: its "deeply rooted" test may narrow what counts as fundamental (slide 5).

**V. Thing 5: What Is Left Unincorporated**

Grand jury indictment (5th Am.) -- Hurtado (1884) (slide 5). Civil jury trial (7th Am.) -- Walker v. Sauvinet (1876) (slide 5). Quartering of soldiers (3rd Am.) -- never squarely addressed by SCOTUS (slide 5). Excessive bail (8th Am.) -- assumed but never held (slide 5).

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

**The Wrong Clause Problem.** If incorporation through Due Process is textually unsound, and P or I is the correct home, what happens to the existing incorporation decisions if Slaughterhouse is overruled? Must every right be re-incorporated under the new framework? Could the Court grandfather existing incorporation precedents while routing future cases through P or I? Is this doctrinally coherent?

**Black's Total Incorporation.** Hugo Black argued for incorporating everything in Amendments 1-8, no more and no less. What would constitutional law look like under this rule? Would grand jury indictment apply to California? Would it reduce or increase judicial discretion compared to selective incorporation?

**The Dobbs Implication.** If the Glucksberg "deeply rooted" test is now the operative methodology for both SDP and incorporation, could a future Court use it to unincorporate previously incorporated rights? Or does stare decisis protect incorporated rights even if the methodology would yield a different answer today?

## Key Takeaways

The Bill of Rights was originally held to apply only to the federal government (Barron). The Fourteenth Amendment's P or I Clause was the natural vehicle for making it apply to states, but Slaughterhouse gutted that clause and Cruikshank slammed the door. The workaround—incorporation through Due Process—begins with CB&Q Railroad (1897) and Gitlow (1925) and has never been textually moored. The operative test evolved from Palko's universality standard through Duncan's American-tradition standard to McDonald's "deeply rooted" formulation. Selective incorporation won; total incorporation (Black) and pure fundamental-fairness (Frankfurter) lost. The deep tension is that incorporation uses a procedural clause to protect substantive rights, entangling it with SDP—a problem Thomas and Gorsuch propose to resolve by returning to P or I. Four provisions remain unincorporated: grand jury indictment, civil jury trial, quartering of soldiers, and (formally) excessive bail.
