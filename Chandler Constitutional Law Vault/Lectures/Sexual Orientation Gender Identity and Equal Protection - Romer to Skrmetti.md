### From source

The deck traces sexual-orientation and gender-identity doctrine from Romer (1996) through Skrmetti (2025) and explicitly groups the Kennedy trilogy as a pattern of reaching results through frameworks that resist easy replication *(slide 15)*. Romer's analytic move is presented as bypassing the suspect-class framework entirely: Amendment 2's "sheer breadth is so discontinuous with the reasons offered for it" that it could only be explained by animus, and "a bare desire to harm a politically unpopular group cannot constitute a legitimate governmental interest" *(slides 2-3)*. ## Lecture Outline

### I. Romer v. Evans (1996) — "Rational Basis with Bite"
- Facts: Colorado's Amendment 2, passed by statewide referendum in 1992, repealed all state and local laws protecting homosexuals from discrimination and prohibited future enactment of any such protections; targeted Denver, Boulder, and Aspen ordinances *(slide 2)*
- Holding: Kennedy, 6-3, struck down Amendment 2 under rational basis review *(slide 2)*
- The analytic move: Kennedy bypassed the traditional suspect-class framework entirely; Amendment 2's "sheer breadth is so discontinuous with the reasons offered for it" that it could only be explained by animus — the desire to make homosexuals "unequal to everyone else" *(slides 2-3)*
- Core principle: "A bare desire to harm a politically unpopular group cannot constitute a legitimate governmental interest" *(slide 3)*
- Creates "rational basis with bite": operates below heightened scrutiny yet strikes down legislation — what scholars call a distinct mode of constitutional review *(slide 3)*

#### Scalia's Dissent: Kulturkampf
- "The Court has mistaken a Kulturkampf for a fit of spite" *(slide 4)*
- Democratic argument: Amendment 2 was a permissible response by Colorado voters in the political arena; moral disapproval of homosexuality is a legitimate basis for legislation, as with polygamy or drug use *(slide 4)*
- Invokes Bowers v. Hardwick (1986): if the state could criminalize the conduct, it could surely deny special protections to those who engage in it *(slide 4)*

#### Scholarly Criticism: The Concealed Classification Problem
- Araiza and Pollvogt argue the animus doctrine conceals an implicit classification hierarchy: Kennedy never decided whether sexual orientation is a suspect class, yet the result in Romer is identical to what heightened scrutiny would produce *(slide 5)*
- The bank-robber problem: laws against bank robbery also reflect "animus" toward bank robbers, yet no one thinks such laws are unconstitutional; the doctrine works only if we believe, sub silentio, that homosexuals deserve more protection than bank robbers — but that conclusion requires exactly the suspect-class analysis Kennedy avoided *(slide 5)*

#### Administrability Problems
- Identification: whose mental state counts? Individual legislators? The median voter? The lobbying coalition? Kennedy never specifies *(slide 6)*
- Proof: how does a court prove animus? Flagg argues moral disapproval and animus exist on a continuum, not as discrete categories *(slide 6)*
- Threshold: how much animus is enough? Does one bigoted legislator taint an otherwise legitimate law? Must animus be the sole motivation? *(slide 6)*

#### The Status/Conduct Salvage Attempt
- Argument: animus toward immutable status differs from animus toward voluntary conduct; cf. Robinson v. California (1962) — criminalizing a status violates the Eighth Amendment *(slide 7)*
- Why it unravels: the distinction smuggles in the suspect-class factor of immutability; if animus is impermissible only when directed at an immutable trait, the court must first decide if the trait is immutable — the very inquiry the animus doctrine was designed to avoid; moreover, Amendment 2 targeted both identity and conduct *(slide 7)*

### II. Lawrence v. Texas (2003) — Liberty Without a Scrutiny Tier
- Facts: Texas Penal Code § 21.06 criminalized "deviate sexual intercourse with another individual of the same sex" — unlike the Georgia law in Bowers, this statute singled out same-sex conduct while leaving identical heterosexual conduct lawful *(slide 9)*
- Holding: Kennedy framed the case primarily under substantive due process (liberty) rather than equal protection, even though the statute's sex-based targeting invited an EP analysis (which O'Connor pursued in her concurrence) *(slide 9)*
- Core principle: "Liberty presumes an autonomy of self that includes freedom of thought, belief, expression, and certain intimate conduct" *(slide 9)*
- Overruled Bowers v. Hardwick: "was not correct when it was decided, and it is not correct today" — overruled on its own terms; historical premise found false *(slide 10)*

#### The Scrutiny Evasion
- Kennedy's "masterful evasion": the opinion never identifies a tier of scrutiny for laws burdening homosexuals *(slide 10)*
- The standard actually applied: certain "liberty interests" protected from laws "not rationally related to any legitimate state interest" — language that sounds like rational basis but with a substantive due process foundation that could support something more demanding *(slide 10)*
- Why the evasion was deliberate: naming a scrutiny tier for sexual orientation would have transformed equal protection doctrine; Kennedy left that question for another day *(slide 10)*

#### The Dissent and the EP Shadow
- Scalia: the decision "logically entails" same-sex marriage — prediction ultimately vindicated by Obergefell twelve years later *(slide 11)*
- O'Connor's concurrence: would have struck down only the Texas statute under equal protection (it singled out same-sex conduct) without overruling Bowers; narrower path that would have left the liberty question untouched *(slide 11)*

### III. Obergefell v. Hodges (2015) — The "Synergy" of Rights
- Facts: consolidated challenges from four states denying same-sex couples the right to marry
- Holding: the Fourteenth Amendment requires states to license and recognize same-sex marriages

#### Kennedy's Four Principles of Marriage
1. Individual autonomy: the right to personal choice regarding marriage is inherent in individual autonomy *(slide 13)*
2. Unique union: marriage supports a two-person union unlike any other in its importance to the committed individuals *(slide 13)*
3. Children and families: marriage safeguards children and families; draws meaning from related rights of childrearing and education *(slide 13)*
4. Social order: marriage is a keystone of social order; no difference between same- and opposite-sex couples *(slide 13)*

#### Substantive Due Process: Rejecting Glucksberg
- Kennedy grounds the marriage right in the Due Process Clause's protection of fundamental liberties *(slide 14)*
- Rejects the narrow Glucksberg approach — which would define the right as "same-sex marriage" and find no historical support — in favor of broader principles of liberty and dignity *(slide 14)*
- "If rights were defined by who exercised them in the past, then received practices could serve as their own continued justification" *(slide 14)*
- Kennedy's invocation of "dignity" drew criticism: Thomas argued dignity is innate and cannot be granted by government; others questioned whether dignity is judicially administrable *(slide 14)*

#### Equal Protection and the "Synergy"
- Kennedy's innovation: interweaves substantive due process and equal protection into a single analysis; the two clauses have a "synergy — each informing and reinforcing the other" *(slide 15)*
- Court again avoided deciding whether sexual orientation is a suspect or quasi-suspect classification *(slide 15)*
- Critics: analytically opaque; Roberts in dissent — the majority's reasoning "has no basis in principle or tradition"; the interweaving of EP and SDP obscures what doctrinal test actually applies *(slide 15)*

#### Voices in Dissent
- Roberts ("Just who do we think we are?"): democratic process, not judicial fiat, should resolve the marriage question; Glucksberg's historical discipline prevents "a naked judicial claim to legislative power" *(slides 16-17)*
- Scalia ("A judicial Putsch"): the majority's reasoning threatens self-governance and reflects an elite legal culture *(slide 17)*
- Thomas ("Liberty has long been understood as freedom from government action, not as a right to government entitlements"): dignity is innate; government cannot bestow or diminish it *(slide 17)*

#### The Methodology Contest
- Kennedy (Reasoned Judgment): fundamental rights identified through "reasoned judgment" that considers history, tradition, and evolving understanding — not by asking whether a specific practice was accepted at ratification *(slide 16)*
- Roberts (Glucksberg Tradition): "deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition" is the established test; "reasoned judgment" without Glucksberg's discipline is simply a license for judges to impose personal preferences *(slide 16)*

### IV. United States v. Skrmetti (2025) — Rational Basis Returns
- Facts: Tennessee SB1 prohibits healthcare providers from prescribing puberty blockers or hormones to minors for the purpose of enabling them to identify with, or live as, an identity inconsistent with their biological sex; exception for "a congenital defect, precocious puberty, disease, or physical injury" *(slide 19)*
- Facial classifications: age (minors only) + medical use (gender-related diagnoses only); no explicit mention of sex *(slide 19)*
- Roberts, 6-3: applied rational basis review *(slide 19)*

#### The Geduldig Framework: Diagnosis vs. Sex
- Roberts cites Geduldig v. Aiello (1974): just as excluding pregnancy from disability insurance did not discriminate based on sex (because the distinction turned on the condition, not the person's sex), SB1's distinction turns on diagnosis, not sex *(slide 20)*
- Both boys and girls with gender dysphoria are equally barred from the excluded treatment; both sexes can receive the same medications for other conditions *(slide 20)*
- Bostock contained: Bostock v. Clayton County (2020) held Title VII protects transgender employees; Roberts distinguished it — Bostock interpreted a statute ("because of sex"); the Equal Protection Clause uses different text ("equal protection"); the Court declined to decide whether Bostock's reasoning extends to constitutional law *(slide 20)*

#### Sotomayor's Dissent: Facial Sex Discrimination
- Sotomayor (joined by Jackson; Kagan joining Parts I-IV): SB1 facially classifies by sex *(slide 21)*
- The law explicitly specifies which medicines can be given and to whom: male adolescents can receive testosterone for gender dysphoria; female adolescents cannot; female adolescents can receive estrogen for gender dysphoria; male adolescents cannot — this is a difference based on the patient's sex, not just diagnosis *(slide 21)*
- Challenges the Geduldig analogy: in Geduldig, pregnancy appeared in only one sex; gender dysphoria appears in both, yet the law permits treatment for one sex and denies it to the other; the excluded condition is inextricably tied to the patient's sex *(slide 21)*
- Because the law facially classifies by sex, intermediate scrutiny should apply; the state's true objective (enforcing conformity to biological sex norms) is not a legitimate governmental interest *(slide 21)*

#### Rational Basis and Tennessee's Legitimate Interests
- Under rational basis, a law is constitutional if the state identifies any conceivable legitimate interest and the law is rationally related to it *(slide 24)*
- Tennessee's four interests: (1) protecting minors from irreversible consequences (including permanent infertility); (2) recognizing that gender dysphoria may resolve through less invasive interventions; (3) accounting for minors' developmental limitations in decision-making; (4) addressing scientific debate over long-term outcomes *(slide 24)*
- The Court need not find the law is best policy — only that it is not irrational *(slide 24)*

#### The Carolene Products Framework and Trans Identity
- History of discrimination: pervasive; appears to satisfy this factor *(slide 22)*
- Political powerlessness: trans individuals remain a small minority; recent legislative restrictions may reinforce this conclusion *(slide 22)*
- Immutability: the most contested factor; if gender identity is innate and stable, it supports heightened scrutiny; if it is a condition amenable to treatment, the analysis shifts; the Court has not resolved this question *(slide 22)*
- The Classification Dilemma: if gender dysphoria is classified as a mental health condition (as DSM-5 does), then medical regulation may be appropriate, weakening the immutability argument; if transgender identity is an innate characteristic (like sexual orientation), then restrictions targeting it look more like the status-based classifications Carolene Products was designed to protect *(slide 23)*
- Thomas concurring: questions whether the entire framework of heightened scrutiny for sex-based classifications reflects the original understanding of the Fourteenth Amendment *(slide 23)*
- Medical uncertainty: the Cass Review (England's NHS) described the evidence base as "remarkably weak"; Finland, Sweden, and Norway have restricted these interventions *(slide 23)*

### V. Pending: Sports, Sex, and the Next Frontier
- West Virginia v. B.P.J.: transgender girl challenged WV's categorical ban on transgender athletes in girls' sports; Fourth Circuit found a Title IX violation; EP claim remanded; oral argument January 13, 2026 *(slide 25)*
- Little v. Hecox: transgender collegiate athlete challenged Idaho's Fairness in Women's Sports Act; Ninth Circuit struck the law down; argued alongside B.P.J. on January 13, 2026 *(slide 25)*
- The EP question after Skrmetti: is a categorical ban on transgender sports participation a sex-based classification subject to intermediate scrutiny, or a regulation of eligibility criteria subject to rational basis? The Court's answer will determine whether transgender identity receives independent constitutional protection or remains refracted through sex classification and medical regulation *(slide 25)*

## Hypotheticals and Class Discussion

**The Bank Robber Problem**: Romer's animus doctrine says "bare desire to harm a politically unpopular group" is not a legitimate interest *(slide 3)*. But laws against bank robbery also reflect "animus" toward bank robbers *(slide 5)*. What distinguishes the two? The only principled answer involves some implicit judgment that homosexuals are more like a racial minority than like criminals — but that judgment requires exactly the suspect-class analysis Kennedy claimed to avoid.

**Kennedy's Evasion Strategy**: Lawrence deliberately does not identify a scrutiny tier *(slide 10)*. What are the costs and benefits of this approach? The benefit: avoiding a doctrinal commitment that could have major downstream effects. The cost: lower courts don't know how to apply it, and the next case will require another non-answer. Did Kennedy succeed?

**Obergefell's "Synergy"**: Roberts argues that interweaving SDP and EP produces no administrable standard *(slide 15)*. Is he right? What would a "pure" substantive due process approach look like here? What would a "pure" equal protection approach look like? Does combining them achieve something neither could alone?

**Skrmetti's Geduldig Move**: Sotomayor argues the Geduldig analogy fails because in Geduldig only one sex could be pregnant, whereas here both sexes can have gender dysphoria but the law treats them differently based on sex *(slide 21)*. Is she right that this is a distinction that makes a difference? Is the majority's response — both sexes are equally barred from gender-dysphoria treatments — persuasive *(slide 20)*?

## Key Takeaways

1. The Kennedy trilogy (Romer, Lawrence, Obergefell) reaches outcomes through frameworks that deliberately avoid naming scrutiny tiers for sexual orientation — "rational basis with bite," unnamed liberty interest, and "synergy" — each analytically non-replicable.
2. Romer's animus doctrine is unstable because it requires an implicit judgment about which groups deserve protection that the doctrine purports to avoid.
3. Lawrence's failure to name a scrutiny tier for sexual-orientation classifications left a doctrinal gap that Obergefell filled through the "synergy" approach, which itself raised similar concerns.
4. Skrmetti represents a retreat from Kennedy's expansive framework: the Roberts Court applies conventional Geduldig analysis to transgender health regulation, classifying the challenged law as targeting a diagnosis rather than sex, and upholding it under rational basis.
5. The immutability question — is transgender identity an innate characteristic or a condition amenable to treatment — is the key unresolved variable that would determine whether heightened scrutiny ever applies to transgender classifications.
6. B.P.J. and Little v. Hecox (argued January 2026, decided by late June 2026) will further define whether transgender identity receives independent constitutional protection.
