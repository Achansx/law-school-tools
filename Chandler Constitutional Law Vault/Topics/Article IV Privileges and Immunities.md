# Article IV Privileges and Immunities

## Overview

The Article IV Privileges and Immunities Clause (U.S. Const. art. IV, sec. 2, cl. 1) is one of the Constitution's structural anti-discrimination glue clauses, alongside the Full Faith and Credit Clause and (historically) the Fugitive Slave Clause. It directs that "The Citizens of each State shall be entitled to all Privileges and Immunities of Citizens in the several States." The doctrinal point Professor Chandler stresses, and the single most common confusion in this area, is that this clause is distinct from the Fourteenth Amendment Privileges or Immunities Clause covered in [[Topics/Privileges or Immunities|Privileges or Immunities]]. The Article IV clause is an equality right, not an absolute right: it does not require any state to provide a given right at all, but it forbids a state from extending that right to its own citizens while denying it to citizens of sister states.

The doctrine has a stable two-step structure. First, the activity at issue must be a fundamental privilege or immunity of interstate citizenship in the Corfield v Coryell (1823) sense, which has been read narrowly to cover economic livelihood, court access, and similar rights but to exclude recreation and common-property entitlements. Second, if the threshold is met, a state may discriminate against non-residents only on a showing of substantial reason and substantial relationship under the Supreme Court of New Hampshire v Piper (1985) test, often phrased in Hicklin v Orbeck (1978) terms as the requirement that non-residents be a peculiar source of the evil the statute addresses. The clause protects natural United States citizens; corporations and aliens are not within its coverage (Paul v Virginia (1869)). Congress cannot waive Article IV restrictions, and there is no general market participant exception of the kind available under the Dormant Commerce Clause.

The clause's relationship to the right to travel is deliberately unstable. Saenz v Roe (1999) parses the right to travel into three components and locates them in three different textual sources: the structural right to enter and leave a state, the Article IV right to be treated as a welcome visitor when temporarily present, and the Fourteenth Amendment Privileges or Immunities right of a new permanent resident to be treated like other citizens of that state. Article IV thus does live work in the second component but cedes the third to the Fourteenth Amendment, which is the doctrinal pivot that has kept the Article IV clause confined largely to durable economic-discrimination cases rather than welfare migration cases.

---

## Governing Rule

> **Rule:** A state may not discriminate against non-resident citizens of other states with respect to privileges and immunities that are fundamental to interstate citizenship, unless (1) the non-residents are a peculiar source of the evil the statute addresses (or, equivalently, there is a substantial reason for the differential treatment beyond bare non-residency), and (2) the discrimination bears a substantial relationship to that objective. Activities outside the fundamental category, such as recreational hunting (Baldwin) or access to a state's common property (Corfield), fall outside the clause and may be allocated to residents without Article IV scrutiny.

## Doctrinal Development

### Corfield v Coryell (1823): The Fundamental Rights Gloss

Justice Bushrod Washington, sitting as Circuit Justice, supplied the foundational interpretive move that has anchored every later Article IV case. Confronting a New Jersey statute that barred non-residents from harvesting oysters from state waters, Washington confined the clause's protection to those privileges and immunities "in their nature, fundamental; which belong, of right, to the citizens of all free governments; and which have, at all times, been enjoyed by the citizens of the several states." His enumeration covered protection by government, life and liberty, the right to acquire and possess property, the right to pass through or reside in another state for trade or professional purposes, access to the courts (habeas corpus), and exemption from higher taxes than residents pay. The result, however, was that the New Jersey oyster prohibition did not violate the clause. Oyster beds are common state property, and a state may exclude outsiders from sharing in its common property without offending Article IV. See [[Cases/Corfield v Coryell (1823)|full brief]].

### Dred Scott v Sandford (1857): The Clause's Structural Shadow

A leading case on Article IV Privileges and Immunities, in the lecture's framing, for a grim reason. Chief Justice Taney's majority opinion held that Black Americans, including free Black Americans, could never have been intended as eligible for United States citizenship in part because conferring that citizenship would have required slave states to extend the full privileges and immunities of citizenship to free Black persons entering from other states, an outcome Taney argued the framers could not have intended. Article IV thus played a structural role in Taney's argument for excluding Black Americans from constitutional citizenship altogether. The case is overruled by the Reconstruction Amendments, but it remains a fixture in this doctrinal area because it shows the clause's anti-discrimination logic operating in reverse. See [[Cases/Dred Scott v Sandford (1857)|full brief]].

### Hicklin v Orbeck (1978): The Modern Anchor

Alaska's "Alaska Hire" statute required employers in the oil and gas industry, including contractors and subcontractors with no direct connection to state-owned resources, to give hiring preference to Alaska residents. Justice Brennan's unanimous opinion struck the statute under Article IV. The state may discriminate against non-residents in pursuit of a common calling only if non-residents are a peculiar source of the evil the statute addresses and the discrimination bears a substantial relationship to that evil. Alaska failed both prongs. Much of the in-state unemployment lay in rural villages geographically inaccessible to the oil-and-gas jobs, so non-resident workers were not shown to be the evil's source, and a blanket preference across an entire industry was not tailored to the asserted problem. The opinion also limits any state-participant analogue to the market-participant exception under the Dormant Commerce Clause: state ownership of the underlying resource does not exempt the conditioning of private employment opportunities downstream. See [[Cases/Hicklin v Orbeck (1978)|full brief]].

### Baldwin v Fish and Game Commission of Montana (1978): Recreation Is Not Fundamental

Montana charged out-of-state hunters dramatically higher fees for elk hunting licenses than it charged residents (a ratio of roughly 7.5:1). Justice Blackmun upheld the discrimination. Elk hunting is recreational, not a fundamental privilege of interstate citizenship; the clause "does not preclude discrimination against nonresidents where the activity in question is not fundamental to the promotion of interstate harmony." The case crystallizes the doctrine's threshold: economic livelihood and court access are protected; recreation and common-property entitlements are not. A state may charge non-residents more for elk licenses; it may not charge them more for a license to practice law or medicine.

### Supreme Court of New Hampshire v Piper (1985): The Two-Prong Test in Modern Form

Piper synthesized the clause's discrimination test into the two-part formulation used today. The clause "does bar discrimination against citizens of other States where there is no substantial reason for the discrimination beyond the mere fact that they are citizens of other States." A state may discriminate only if (1) there is a substantial reason for the difference in treatment beyond bare citizenship difference, and (2) the discrimination bears a substantial relationship to that objective. The case applied the test to invalidate a state bar's residency requirement for admission, treating the practice of law as a fundamental economic privilege of interstate citizenship.

### McBurney v Young (2013): Political-Accountability Functions Are Not Economic Privileges

Virginia's FOIA statute restricted right-of-access provisions to Virginia residents. Justice Alito's unanimous opinion upheld the residency restriction. Virginia's FOIA is a mechanism for political accountability of state government to its own citizens, an inherently political and citizenship-anchored function rather than an economic privilege of interstate citizenship. The Court found no protectionist purpose: Virginia was not advantaging its citizens economically by excluding non-residents but was defining the scope of an internal accountability tool. Together with Baldwin, McBurney marks the doctrine's outer boundary on the threshold side: rights bound up with state-political-community membership are not within the clause's coverage even when they look like discrimination on the face of the statute.

### Saenz v Roe (1999): The Right to Travel and the Article IV / Fourteenth Amendment Pivot

California limited new residents' welfare benefits to whatever they would have received in their prior state for the first year of residency. Justice Stevens's majority struck the limitation, but on Fourteenth Amendment Privileges or Immunities and right-to-travel grounds rather than Article IV. The right to travel has three components: the right to enter and leave another state, the right to be treated as a welcome visitor when temporarily present, and the right of a person who elects to become a permanent resident of a new state to be treated like other citizens of that state. The first two implicate Article IV indirectly; the third flows from the Fourteenth Amendment Privileges or Immunities Clause and was the basis on which Saenz invalidated the welfare scheme. The case is included in this Topic because it draws the doctrinal boundary between Article IV (which governs visitors and short-term presence) and the Fourteenth Amendment (which governs new permanent residents). The Rehnquist and Thomas dissents agreed an Article IV ground would have been available for plaintiffs who had not yet established California domicile but objected to reviving the Fourteenth Amendment Privileges or Immunities Clause for the new-resident class. See [[Cases/Saenz v Roe (1999)|full brief]].

## Comparison: Article IV Privileges and Immunities versus the Dormant Commerce Clause

| Attribute | Article IV Privileges and Immunities | Dormant Commerce Clause |
|-----------|--------------------------------------|-------------------------|
| Protected parties | Natural United States citizens only (Paul v Virginia) | Corporations and aliens included |
| Congressional waiver | Congress cannot authorize states to discriminate | Congress can authorize discriminatory state laws |
| Market participant exception | No general exception; Hicklin recognized a narrow state-participant analogue tied to genuine resource ownership | States acting as market participants are exempt from DCC scrutiny |
| Standard | Substantial reason plus substantial relationship (Piper); peculiar-source-of-the-evil framing (Hicklin) | Strict scrutiny for facial discrimination; Pike balancing for incidental burdens |
| Threshold | Activity must be a fundamental privilege of interstate citizenship; recreational and common-property activities are outside the clause | No fundamental-rights threshold; protection runs to all interstate commerce |

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side

1. **Hypo:** State X enacts a statute requiring all attorneys admitted to its state bar to maintain a primary residence in State X and forbidding non-resident attorneys from sitting for the bar exam unless they relocate first. **Why:** The practice of law is a fundamental economic privilege of interstate citizenship under Piper, and a flat residency requirement fails the substantial-reason prong because there is no peculiar non-resident evil at which the rule is aimed beyond the bare fact of non-residency.

2. **Hypo:** State Y enacts an "in-state contractor preference" statute requiring private construction firms operating in the state to give hiring priority to State Y residents on all projects, including projects with no nexus to state-owned land or state contracts. **Why:** This is the Hicklin pattern applied beyond oil and gas. The statute sweeps across an entire industry without showing non-residents are the source of the in-state unemployment problem, fails the substantial-relationship prong, and cannot rest on a state-participant theory because the state has no proprietary interest in the underlying private projects.

### Opposite-Side

3. **Hypo:** State Z charges non-resident anglers a fee three times higher than the resident fee for a sport-fishing license valid only in state-owned lakes. **Why:** This is the Baldwin pattern. Sport fishing is recreational rather than a fundamental privilege of interstate citizenship, and the activity targets a state-owned common-property resource. Article IV does not reach the discrimination at the threshold.

4. **Hypo:** State A's open-records statute provides that only state residents may file requests under the law to obtain agency emails and meeting minutes. Non-resident journalists challenge under Article IV. **Why:** Under McBurney, FOIA-style access is bound up with the political-accountability of state government to its own citizens rather than with interstate economic livelihood, and the Court has held this kind of state-citizenship function is not an interstate privilege at all. The threshold is not met.

### Fence-Sitter

5. **Hypo:** State B enacts a statute that imposes a 20 percent recovery-fund surcharge on real estate sales to non-residents in counties recently devastated by wildfire, but does not impose the surcharge on sales to long-time residents. The state defends the surcharge as preventing post-disaster speculation that displaces local rebuilding. **Why:** The activity, real-estate purchase, edges toward a fundamental economic privilege under the Corfield enumeration ("right to acquire and possess property"), so the threshold is likely met. The substantial-reason prong is plausible because non-resident speculators arguably are a peculiar source of the displacement problem in disaster zones, but the substantial-relationship prong is contestable: a flat surcharge across an entire county sweeps in non-residents who would have rebuilt and held, not flipped, and there are narrower remedies (a recapture tax on sale within five years applicable to all owners, residency-neutral) that the state did not try.

## How to Spot on an Exam

The trigger fact pattern is a state statute that draws an explicit residency line and the challenger is a natural person from a sister state (not a corporation, which sends you to the Dormant Commerce Clause exclusively, and not an alien, which sends you to equal protection). Two threshold filters come first. (1) Is the state's measure conditioning a fundamental privilege of interstate citizenship in the Corfield sense (livelihood, court access, taxation parity, bar admission, professional licensure, real-estate acquisition for trade), or is it allocating recreation, common-property access, or a state-political-community function (Baldwin, McBurney)? If the latter, Article IV does no work and you write a short paragraph explaining why. (2) Is Congress purporting to authorize the discrimination by statute? Saenz holds that congressional authorization does not cure Article IV (or Fourteenth Amendment Privileges or Immunities) defects, so the federal hook does not save the state. If both filters point toward the clause, run the Piper/Hicklin two-prong test. The substantial-reason prong asks whether non-residents are a peculiar source of the evil being addressed, with overbreadth across an industry tipping toward the challenger (Hicklin). The substantial-relationship prong asks whether a less discriminatory means would do the same work; if the state had non-residency-neutral tools available (residency verification rather than benefit reduction; a recapture tax rather than a surcharge), the prong fails. Always pair the analysis with a parallel Dormant Commerce Clause analysis when commerce is implicated, and remember the comparison table above: corporations and aliens make the Article IV claim disappear and shift the entire analysis to DCC and equal protection.

## Critique

The clause's textual command is one of the most unambiguous anti-discrimination provisions in the original Constitution, but the doctrine has tolerated more state discrimination than its text suggests. Three lines of critique recur. First, the Corfield "fundamental rights" gloss is contested in both directions. Slaughter-House Cases (1873) read Corfield narrowly when constructing the Fourteenth Amendment Privileges or Immunities Clause, while modern originalists (Barnett, Lash) read Corfield as an Articles-of-Confederation-era catalogue of national-citizenship rights and use it to argue both Privileges or Immunities clauses reach more broadly than current doctrine permits. Second, the Baldwin line that recreation is outside the clause produces line-drawing problems. The clause's text reaches "Privileges and Immunities" without a fundamentality qualifier, and the cases have struggled to explain why elk hunting falls outside but oyster harvesting (in Corfield, before the common-property carve-out does the work) was treated as a serious clause question. Third, the McBurney political-accountability rationale risks swallowing the rule in a state that frames any state-administered benefit as a political-citizenship function. The Court has not articulated a principle that distinguishes legitimate state-political-community functions from facial economic protectionism dressed in citizenship language; future cases will have to draw the line under pressure from state efforts to revive Hicklin-style residency preferences with citizenship-function gloss.

A separate critique runs to the Saenz pivot. By relocating the new-resident equality right to the Fourteenth Amendment Privileges or Immunities Clause, the Court left Article IV doing duty primarily for short-term-visitor cases and economic-livelihood cases involving non-domiciled plaintiffs. This is doctrinally tidy but has hollowed the Article IV clause's significance in welfare-migration and benefit-allocation cases, the very area where its anti-protectionism logic would most plainly apply. Whether Article IV remains a viable alternative ground for plaintiffs who have not established new-state domicile (the Rehnquist and Thomas position in Saenz) is unsettled and matters for litigation strategy after Saenz.
