# Sex Discrimination

## Overview

Sex discrimination doctrine under the Equal Protection Clause runs a century from Bradwell v Illinois (1873), where Justice Bradley's concurrence treated women's confinement to the domestic sphere as the "law of the Creator," to United States v Virginia (1996), where Justice Ginsburg's majority demanded an "exceedingly persuasive justification" for any sex-based government action. In between, Reed v Reed (1971) was the first invalidation of a sex classification under equal protection, Frontiero v Richardson (1973) produced four votes for strict scrutiny that never became a fifth, and Craig v Boren (1976) settled on intermediate scrutiny as the compromise that could hold a majority. The doctrine rests not on Carolene Products' political-process theory (young men buying beer are political insiders, not a discrete and insular minority) but on anti-stereotyping: the prohibition on relying on overbroad generalizations about the different talents, capacities, or preferences of men and women.

The arc matters for exam purposes because every sex-classification question asks two things: which tier of scrutiny applies, and what the fit between the sex line and the state's asserted interest actually looks like. Intermediate scrutiny sits between rational basis and strict scrutiny by design, and Virginia's "exceedingly persuasive justification" formulation tightened every component without formally moving the tier. Geduldig v Aiello (1974) remains the boundary-case anomaly: pregnancy exclusions are not sex discrimination at the constitutional level because the classification runs between "pregnant women" and "nonpregnant persons," a categorical logic that may resurface in transgender-classification cases.

## Governing Rule

> **Rule (Intermediate Scrutiny for Sex):** A classification that distinguishes on the basis of sex must serve important governmental objectives and must be substantially related to achievement of those objectives (Craig v Boren, 1976). The government bears the burden of justification.

> **Rule (VMI Tightening):** Parties defending sex-based government action must demonstrate an "exceedingly persuasive justification" (United States v Virginia, 1996). The justification must be genuine, not hypothesized or invented after litigation has begun, it cannot rest on post-hoc rationalizations, and it must not rely on overbroad generalizations about the different talents, capacities, or preferences of males and females. The symmetry principle from Craig controls: intermediate scrutiny applies whether the classification disadvantages women or men (Sessions v Morales-Santana, 2017).

> **Rule (Anti-Stereotyping Engine):** The doctrinal work of intermediate scrutiny is done by an anti-stereotyping principle that prohibits the government from entrenching sex roles or relying on generalizations about sex-linked behavior, even when those generalizations are statistically accurate on average (Craig; Virginia). Administrative convenience is not a sufficient justification (Reed; Frontiero).

> **Rule (Pregnancy Exception):** A classification between "pregnant women" and "nonpregnant persons" is not sex discrimination at the constitutional level, even where the disadvantaged group is exclusively female (Geduldig v Aiello, 1974). Rational basis review applies. Congress rejected this reasoning by statute in the Pregnancy Discrimination Act (1978), but the constitutional holding stands.

## Doctrinal Development

### Arc I: From Divine Ordinance to Constitutional Skepticism

Bradwell v Illinois (1873) was the first sex-discrimination case. Myra Bradwell, editor of the Chicago Legal News and the first woman admitted to the Illinois bar on merit, was refused a license solely because she was a woman. Justice Miller's majority ducked the sex question entirely by holding that the right to practice law was not a privilege of national citizenship, applying the narrow Slaughter-House reading of the Privileges or Immunities Clause decided the same week. But Justice Bradley's concurrence is the opinion that matters for sex-discrimination doctrine. Bradley wrote that the "civil law, and nature itself, have always recognized a fundamental difference in the respective spheres and destinies of men and women," that the "paramount destiny and mission of woman are to fulfil the noble and benign offices of wife and mother," and that this was "the law of the Creator." The "natural and proper timidity and delicacy which belongs to the female sex," he wrote, "evidently unfits it for many of the occupations of civil life." See [[Cases/Bradwell v Illinois (1873)|full brief]].

The lecture frames Bradley's concurrence as the "pedestal or cage" tension that Justice Brennan would later invoke in Frontiero: the language of protection was in practice a language of confinement. The doctrinal silence that followed Bradwell lasted 98 years. Between 1873 and 1971, the Supreme Court did not invalidate a single sex-based classification on equal-protection grounds.

### Arc II: The First Invalidation and the Push for Strict Scrutiny

Reed v Reed (1971) broke the silence. Idaho's probate code contained a mandatory preference: when two persons equally entitled to administer a decedent's estate were of different sexes, the male had to be preferred. Sally and Cecil Reed both applied to administer their deceased son Skip's modest estate; the probate court appointed Cecil solely on the strength of the sex-preference. Chief Justice Burger, writing for a unanimous Court, held that a classification must rest "upon some ground of difference having a fair and substantial relation to the object of the legislation." Idaho's asserted interest in reducing probate-court workload was legitimate, but sex bore no fair and substantial relation to fitness to administer an estate. The Court did not formally announce a new tier of scrutiny, but the formulation Burger pulled forward from Royster Guano Co v Virginia (1920) looked more like rational basis with bite than the forgiving review Idaho had expected. See [[Cases/Reed v Reed (1971)|full brief]].

Frontiero v Richardson (1973) two years later tried to make the doctrine explicit. Lt. Sharron Frontiero, an Air Force officer, had to prove her husband Joseph was financially dependent to claim housing and medical benefits; male servicemembers' wives received benefits automatically. Justice Brennan's plurality (Douglas, White, and Marshall joining) argued sex was "inherently suspect" on three grounds: it is an immutable characteristic that frequently bears no relation to ability, women have faced pervasive historical discrimination, and Congress itself had just proposed the Equal Rights Amendment. Applying strict scrutiny, the plurality held that administrative convenience could never be a compelling interest. Four votes were not five. Justice Powell concurred separately: with the ERA pending before the states, it was "premature" for the Court to declare sex a suspect class while the American people were deliberating through proper democratic channels. Powell wanted institutional restraint. The ERA ultimately failed to ratify. Strict scrutiny for sex never arrived. See [[Cases/Frontiero v Richardson (1973)|full brief]].

### Arc III: Intermediate Scrutiny Announced and the Boundary Cases

Craig v Boren (1976) is the pivot. Oklahoma permitted females to buy 3.2% beer at 18 but required males to wait until 21. The landmark sex-discrimination standard emerged in a case brought by a young man about low-alcohol beer, by design: this was Ruth Bader Ginsburg's strategic move, using male plaintiffs (Craig; earlier Weinberger v Wiesenfeld, Califano v Goldfarb) to reframe sex discrimination as a system of role-assignment that harms all members of both sexes, not just women seeking entry to male-dominated spheres. Justice Brennan announced the test: "Classifications by gender must serve important governmental objectives and must be substantially related to achievement of those objectives." Oklahoma's statistics (2% of males versus 0.18% of females aged 18-20 arrested for drunk driving) described a real correlation, but left more than 98% of young men and 99.82% of young women entirely innocent of the conduct the state invoked. That fit was too loose for substantially related. See [[Cases/Craig v Boren (1976)|full brief]].

The strategic logic is important. Brennan had only mustered four votes in Frontiero; Powell was the ceiling. A strict-scrutiny rule would have lost Powell. A rational-basis rule would have left the doctrine incoherent. Intermediate scrutiny sat precisely where a seven-Justice majority could coalesce: strong enough to strike overinclusive sex stereotypes, weak enough to retain a Justice who resisted treating sex like race. That diplomatic positioning is a large part of why Craig's rule proved durable.

Craig also rejected a Twenty-First Amendment shield for alcohol regulation: the Amendment's commerce-clause protection does not confer on states a power to violate equal protection. And Craig granted vendor Carolyn Whitener third-party standing to raise her male customers' claims, preserving jurisdiction once Craig himself turned 21 during the appeal.

Geduldig v Aiello (1974), decided two years before Craig, remains the pregnancy anomaly. California's disability insurance excluded pregnancy-related disabilities from coverage. Justice Stewart, writing for the Court, held this was not sex discrimination: the program divided recipients into "pregnant women and nonpregnant persons," and the second group includes members of both sexes. Only rational basis review applied. Justice Brennan dissented, arguing that dissimilar treatment of physical characteristics "inextricably linked to one sex" inevitably constitutes sex discrimination. Congress agreed with the dissent and passed the Pregnancy Discrimination Act (1978), but the constitutional holding stands. The lecture flags Geduldig as potentially resurfacing in transgender-classification cases, where a state might argue (as California did) that a classification by gender-transition status is not a classification by sex.

San Antonio Independent School District v Rodriguez (1973) closed off heightened scrutiny for wealth-based classifications and for education as a fundamental right. The poor are not a suspect class, education is not explicitly or implicitly guaranteed by the Constitution, and rational basis applies. The decision marked the moment when liberal hopes that equal protection would address structural economic inequality collapsed. The tiers froze with wealth at rational basis, sex at intermediate, and race at strict.

City of Cleburne v Cleburne Living Center (1985) completes the boundary story. The city denied a special-use permit for a group home for persons with intellectual disabilities. Justice White's majority refused to elevate disability to quasi-suspect status (only rational basis applies) but struck the ordinance anyway because the city's stated justifications (neighbor hostility, flood-plain concerns, student harassment) were indistinguishable from the justifications one could offer for other permitted uses like fraternity houses and nursing homes. The law rested on "an irrational prejudice against the mentally retarded." Cleburne thus exemplifies "rational basis with bite," an intermediate-in-effect standard that approximates heightened scrutiny in outcome. The Footnote Four paradox is highlighted: people with intellectual disabilities are a textbook discrete-and-insular minority, yet they receive only rational basis, while young men buying beer received intermediate scrutiny under Craig. The anti-stereotyping principle is doing the doctrinal work, not process theory. See [[Cases/City of Cleburne v Cleburne Living Center (1985)|full brief]].

### Arc IV: Tightening Intermediate Scrutiny to "Exceedingly Persuasive Justification"

United States v Virginia (1996) tightened every component of intermediate scrutiny without formally moving the tier. Virginia Military Institute's 157-year all-male tradition was challenged by the United States. Virginia's proposed remedy (a parallel women's institute at a lower-ranked college with far fewer resources) was dismissed as inadequate. Justice Ginsburg, writing for the Court in a 7-1 outcome (Rehnquist concurring in the judgment, Scalia in dissent, Thomas not participating), announced that parties defending sex-based government action must demonstrate an "exceedingly persuasive justification." That justification "must be genuine, not hypothesized or invented after litigation has begun. It cannot rest on post-hoc rationalizations. And it must not rely on overbroad generalizations about the different talents, capacities, or preferences of males and females." While the formal Craig test (important objectives substantially related) remained, Ginsburg tightened each element: post-hoc rationalization is disallowed, statistical generalizations carry less weight, and the fit must be closer. After Virginia, intermediate scrutiny for sex is more demanding than intermediate scrutiny for any other quasi-suspect classification.

Sessions v Morales-Santana (2017) applied intermediate scrutiny to a citizenship statute imposing different residency requirements on unwed fathers versus unwed mothers. The symmetry principle established in Craig (intermediate scrutiny applies regardless of which sex bears the disadvantage) was confirmed. The remedy the Court selected, incidentally, "leveled down" by removing the benefit from both groups rather than extending it, a remedial choice that drew its own critique.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side

1. **Hypo:** A federal agency adopts a policy assigning only male field officers to overseas combat-zone embassy postings, relying on a legislative history noting that female officers have not historically been posted abroad and citing unspecified "operational security" concerns. The justification is first articulated in the government's litigation brief after a female officer sues. **Why:** This is a textbook VMI violation. Post-hoc rationalization is disallowed under United States v Virginia, and the "operational security" premise rests on an overbroad generalization about female officers' capacity to serve abroad. The agency cannot manufacture its justification after the classification was challenged, and sex is not substantially related to the actual task of embassy duty. The federal government is bound by intermediate scrutiny through the Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause (reverse-incorporation via Bolling v Sharpe).

2. **Hypo:** A state adopts a statute setting the age of financial majority at 18 for men and 16 for women, citing statistical studies showing that women at 16 exhibit maturity metrics comparable to men at 18. The correlation is robust, at roughly a 15-to-1 ratio. **Why:** Under Craig v Boren and Virginia, this likely fails. The statistical correlation is stronger than Oklahoma's DUI data, but the classification still treats every 16-year-old woman as presumptively mature and every 16-year-old man as presumptively not, without any individualized inquiry. Intermediate scrutiny bars the government from relying on overbroad generalizations about maturity, and "exceedingly persuasive justification" demands a tighter fit than statistical averaging. The state has individualized alternatives available (competency testing, case-by-case judicial emancipation) that would serve its legitimate interest in contractual capacity without drawing the sex line.

### Opposite-Side

3. **Hypo:** A state correctional officer academy applies different pull-up and run-time physical fitness standards for male and female candidates, drawing on independently validated physiological studies showing average differences in upper-body strength and cardiovascular profile between the sexes. Each differential standard has been tied to specific job tasks the officer must perform. **Why:** This would likely survive intermediate scrutiny. Officer safety and inmate control are important governmental objectives, and a classification that tracks actual, independently validated physiological differences relevant to the specific tasks of corrections work is substantially related to those objectives. Virginia's prohibition on "overbroad generalizations" does not bar the government from relying on documented sex-linked differences that genuinely correlate with job performance, so long as the correlation is not serving as a pretext for stereotype-based exclusion. The job-relatedness of each standard does the work that Oklahoma's beer statute lacked.

4. **Hypo:** A state statute provides paid medical leave to employees who have given birth, with a separate bonding-leave program available to all parents. **Why:** Women-only medical leave survives intermediate scrutiny because childbirth is a biological condition that affects only birthing parents, and the state's objective of permitting recovery from childbirth is substantially related to a classification tracking the actual physical condition. The Court has repeatedly treated the line between biological sex and social role as the line marking legitimate sex-specific classifications. And Geduldig's "pregnancy is not sex discrimination" logic, whatever its continuing constitutional vitality, reinforces that a pregnancy-linked benefit does not itself trigger intermediate scrutiny.

### Fence-Sitter

5. **Hypo:** A state department of insurance permits private life insurers to charge women 15% lower premiums than men based on actuarial tables documenting that women on average live roughly five years longer than men. The state defends the practice on the important objective of accurate risk-pricing and argues that sex is a reliable, well-documented proxy for mortality. **Why:** This hypo sits exactly where Craig v Boren is blurriest. The sex-linked correlation with mortality is far stronger than Oklahoma's DUI correlation, and accurate risk-pricing is an important objective. But the classification still uses sex as a categorical proxy when more granular underwriting data (smoking, exercise, occupational hazard, family history) is available. Under a strict anti-stereotyping reading of Virginia, the availability of individualized risk-assessment alternatives should defeat the substantially-related prong. Under a more permissive reading, actuarial sex-differentials that track biological averages survive because they are not "overbroad generalizations" but documented empirical regularities. Title VII treats sex-based insurance pricing in employer-sponsored plans as sex discrimination (Los Angeles Dept of Water and Power v Manhart, 1978; Arizona Governing Committee v Norris, 1983), but the constitutional analysis for state-permitted private-market pricing is different, and the Court has not squarely resolved it.

## How to Spot on an Exam

**Trigger facts:** any facially sex-based classification (rules that differentiate between men and women on their face); any facially neutral rule that turns on pregnancy, childbearing, or traditionally sex-linked physical characteristics; any government benefit, license, or criminal rule that depends on sex; any military, correctional, or athletic rule that uses sex as a classifying variable.

**Analytical framework:**

1. Identify the classification. Is the law facially sex-based, or facially neutral? For sex discrimination, facial neutrality with disparate impact alone is not enough (Washington v Davis analogues) unless the challenger can show a discriminatory purpose.

2. Is it pregnancy? If the classification runs between "pregnant persons" and "nonpregnant persons," Geduldig still formally governs at the constitutional level: only rational basis applies. Flag the transgender-classification angle Professor Chandler raised as a potential resurfacing of the Geduldig categorical logic.

3. Apply intermediate scrutiny. Important governmental objective? Substantially related to the objective? Under Virginia, check for (a) post-hoc rationalization (invented after litigation began, therefore disqualified), (b) reliance on overbroad generalizations about the talents, capacities, or preferences of men and women (disqualified), and (c) whether the fit is tight enough that sex is actually the appropriate classifying variable rather than a proxy for a job-relevant trait.

4. Check the symmetry principle. Intermediate scrutiny applies whether the classification disadvantages women (Reed, Frontiero, Virginia) or men (Craig, Morales-Santana). Ginsburg's male-plaintiff strategy frames sex discrimination as mutual role-assignment, and the doctrine is symmetric in application.

5. Consider the boundary cases. Rodriguez (wealth, education) and Cleburne (disability) show where heightened scrutiny does and does not attach. Cleburne's rational-basis-with-bite approach is the fallback when a group presents as a discrete-and-insular minority but the Court declines to elevate the tier.

The exam move that trips students up is mistaking Virginia's "exceedingly persuasive justification" language for a new tier of scrutiny. It is not. The formal Craig test remains. Virginia tightens every component within intermediate scrutiny without formally replacing it. Write the rule as Craig + VMI: important objective, substantially related, and the justification must be genuine, not post-hoc, and not grounded in overbroad sex generalizations.

## Critique

*Progressive feminist critique.* Intermediate scrutiny is a second-class tier for a second-class equality claim. The test invites courts to accept a government's self-declared "important" objective without asking hard questions about whether the objective rests on sex stereotypes or on the subordination of women. Catharine MacKinnon and Reva Siegel have argued that the tier's doctrinal elasticity has allowed laws that entrench gender roles (pregnancy exclusions from insurance, differential treatment of mothers and fathers in family law) to survive under the guise of substantially-related fit. The Frontiero plurality was right: sex is an immutable characteristic frequently bearing no relation to ability, and the historical pattern of sex-based discrimination is at least as severe as the pattern for national origin or alienage, both of which receive strict scrutiny. The failure to elevate sex to suspect-class status in 1973 was a doctrinal choice driven by Powell's deference to an ERA process that never completed, not by any principled distinction between sex and race classifications.

*Originalist critique.* The generation that ratified the Fourteenth Amendment had just approved the Fifteenth Amendment specifically to extend the franchise on race but conspicuously declined to extend it on sex. The First Congress to draft the Fourteenth Amendment rejected proposals to include "sex" in the equal-protection language. The record suggests the original public meaning of the Equal Protection Clause did not reach state statutes that classified by sex, particularly in domestic-relations contexts traditionally left to state police power. Justices Scalia and Thomas have drawn on that record to object to Virginia's "exceedingly persuasive justification" formulation as unmoored from text and history. From this vantage, the entire sex-equality arc from Reed through Virginia is a series of judicial improvisations, and the Court's interventions to strike sex classifications on constitutional grounds lie outside the judicial role that originalism prescribes.

*Anti-stereotyping as the actual engine.* Professor Chandler's diagnostic is that neither process theory (Footnote Four) nor anti-subordination (Slaughter-House "one pervading purpose") explains the cases. Craig v Boren is the proof: young men buying 3.2% beer are paradigmatic political insiders, not a discrete-and-insular minority. Cleburne is the inverse proof: people with intellectual disabilities are a textbook Footnote Four group, yet they receive only rational basis. The actual work is being done by an anti-stereotyping principle that prohibits the government from relying on overbroad generalizations about sex-linked roles and capacities. That principle is defensible, but it is not what the cases say they are doing, and the gap between the formal process-theory rhetoric and the operative anti-stereotyping rule is a source of ongoing doctrinal confusion.

*Institutional critique of the tier system.* Justice Rehnquist's Craig dissent is still the cleanest doctrinal objection: the intermediate tier is "diaphanous and elastic," a standard so loose that judges can strike down any classification they dislike under the label of "important governmental objectives." In some decisions (Virginia), the tier approaches strict-scrutiny-grade demands. In others (Michael M v Superior Court, sustaining sex-differentiated statutory rape laws), the tier produces deferential outcomes. The inconsistency is a function of the elasticity that made intermediate scrutiny politically achievable in 1976. For practitioners, the result is that Craig provides no reliable rule of decision; for theorists, it is evidence that the tier system masks ad hoc judicial balancing.

*The Geduldig problem and transgender classifications.* Geduldig's constitutional holding (pregnancy exclusion is not sex discrimination because the classification runs between "pregnant women" and "nonpregnant persons") has been severely criticized and largely displaced by Title VII's Pregnancy Discrimination Act at the statutory level. But the constitutional reasoning has not been overruled, and Professor Chandler has flagged that the Geduldig categorical logic could resurface in transgender-classification cases (Skrmetti being the explicit forward-reference). If a state argues that a classification by gender-transition status is not a classification by sex, Geduldig's two-classes framework supplies the doctrinal template. Whether the Court adopts that analysis or treats it as foreclosed by later anti-stereotyping jurisprudence is an open question that will shape the next generation of sex-and-gender doctrine.
