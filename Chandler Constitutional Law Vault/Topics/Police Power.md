# Police Power

## Overview

Police power is the residual sovereign authority of the states to regulate for the public health, public safety, public morals, and general welfare. It is not enumerated in the Constitution because it predates the Constitution: when the states ratified the Tenth Amendment they reserved their pre-existing regulatory authority and granted only specific enumerated powers to the new federal government. The Supreme Court has therefore treated the police power as the default state-law category against which constitutional liberty and equality challenges are tested. Almost every Fourteenth Amendment substantive due process case in the first century of the Amendment is, in form, a question about the limits of the police power.

The doctrine has moved through three major periods. From 1873 (Slaughter-House) to 1905 (Lochner and Jacobson), the Court recognized the police power as a broad and largely deferentially-reviewed authority. From 1905 to 1937, the Lochner era treated certain economic regulations as exceeding the police power because they intruded on a constitutionally-protected liberty of contract. After 1937 (West Coast Hotel and Carolene Products), the Court returned to deferential review for ordinary economic and social regulation while reserving heightened scrutiny for laws that touch specific constitutional prohibitions, the political process, or discrete and insular minorities. The modern doctrine therefore folds most police-power questions into rational basis review, with substantive due process and equal protection providing the heightened-scrutiny exits.

---

## Governing Rule

> **Foundational Rule (Jacobson):** The police power extends to reasonable regulations protecting public health, safety, and the common good. A health regulation survives constitutional challenge unless it has no real or substantial relation to the public end pursued, or constitutes a plain, palpable invasion of rights secured by the fundamental law. Liberty under the Constitution is regulated liberty, not absolute freedom from restraint.

> **New Deal Reformulation (West Coast Hotel):** Liberty under the Constitution is not absolute and uncontrollable. It is liberty in a social organization that requires the protection of law against evils that menace the health, safety, morals, and welfare of the people. Regulation reasonable in relation to its subject and adopted in the interests of the community satisfies due process.

> **Modern Default (Carolene Products / Williamson):** Regulatory legislation affecting ordinary commercial transactions is presumed constitutional unless the legislature could have had no rational basis for it. The existence of supporting facts is presumed, and the courts will not reweigh the legislature's policy judgment.

> **Footnote Four Carve-Out (Carolene Products):** The presumption of constitutionality may be narrower for legislation that on its face violates a specific constitutional prohibition, restricts the political processes that ordinarily bring about repeal of undesirable laws, or is directed at religious, national, or racial minorities whose status as discrete and insular minorities curtails the political safeguards that normally protect them.

## Doctrinal Development

### Slaughter-House Cases (1873): The Police Power as Residual State Authority

The Court's first sustained reading of the Fourteenth Amendment treated a Louisiana statute granting an exclusive slaughterhouse monopoly as a valid exercise of the police power. Justice Miller framed the police power as a residual authority predating the Constitution, broad enough to encompass health regulation of butchering and not displaced by the new Fourteenth Amendment. The opinion's narrow reading of the Privileges or Immunities Clause meant that for most of the next half century, federal constitutional review of state regulatory power ran through the Due Process Clause. See [[Cases/Slaughter-House Cases (1873)|full brief]].

### Jacobson v Massachusetts (1905): The Foundational Test

Decided three months before Lochner, Jacobson upheld a $5 fine for refusing compulsory smallpox vaccination in Cambridge during a public-health emergency. Justice Harlan's majority articulated the police-power test that anchored doctrine for the next thirty years: a state health regulation survives constitutional challenge if it bears a real and substantial relation to the protection of public health and is not a plain, palpable invasion of fundamental rights. The opinion's framing principle, that constitutional liberty is regulated liberty, became the template for every later police-power case. See [[Cases/Jacobson v Massachusetts (1905)|full brief]].

### Lochner v New York (1905): The First Limit

Decided two months after Jacobson, Lochner struck down New York's sixty-hour-per-week limit on bakery employees as an interference with the liberty of contract protected by the Due Process Clause. Justice Peckham's majority did not deny the existence of the police power; it held that this particular regulation was not a genuine health measure because baking was not a sufficiently dangerous trade and that the law was, in substance, a labor regulation dressed in police-power clothing. The opinion's methodological move (the Court independently reviews whether a regulation is genuinely a health measure) became the engine of three decades of judicial second-guessing. Holmes's three-paragraph dissent planted the seed that ultimately destroyed the doctrine. See [[Cases/Lochner v New York (1905)|full brief]].

### The Same-Term Paradox: Lochner and Jacobson (1905)

The most stable insight from Chandler's Economic Substantive Due Process lecture is that Lochner and Jacobson were decided in the same Term and produced opposite outcomes on what should have been parallel liberty questions. The chart below makes the asymmetry visible.

| Dimension | Lochner v New York (1905) | Jacobson v Massachusetts (1905) |
|-----------|---------------------------|-------------------------------|
| Liberty interest claimed | Liberty of contract (bakers' freedom to work long hours) | Bodily integrity (refusal of compulsory vaccination) |
| Severity of intrusion | Cap on weekly hours | Forced injection or fine |
| State interest invoked | Worker health and bargaining-power equalization | Public health (smallpox prevention) |
| Court's posture toward legislature | Independent review of whether the trade is genuinely dangerous | Deferential review for any real and substantial relation |
| Outcome | Statute struck down | Statute upheld |
| Methodological signal | The police power has a contestable economic-liberty boundary | The police power has a deferentially-reviewed health-and-safety core |

The lecture's recurring teaching question is whether the asymmetry can be explained on neutral principles or whether it tells us only that the Court valued economic liberty more than bodily autonomy. The post-1937 settlement collapsed the two cases onto the Jacobson side of the line for any regulation affecting ordinary commercial transactions, while Footnote Four reopened a non-economic exit through heightened scrutiny for fundamental rights and suspect classes.

### Meyer v Nebraska (1923) and Pierce v Society of Sisters (1925): The Liberty Limits Outside the Economic Sphere

The Lochner-era Court did not confine its police-power scrutiny to economic regulation. Meyer struck down Nebraska's ban on teaching foreign languages to children below the eighth grade; Pierce struck down Oregon's compulsory-public-school law backed by the Ku Klux Klan. Both opinions located the limit in Fourteenth Amendment liberty as catalogued in Meyer, and both treated the police power as the default state authority that had to be overcome. The two cases survived the New Deal repudiation of Lochner because their liberty interests (parental direction of education, teachers' right to teach) were eventually relabeled as fundamental rights triggering heightened scrutiny rather than ordinary rational basis review. See [[Cases/Meyer v Nebraska (1923)|Meyer brief]] and [[Cases/Pierce v Society of Sisters (1925)|Pierce brief]].

### Buck v Bell (1927): The Lochner-Era Low Point

Holmes, writing for an 8-1 Court, extended Jacobson's compulsory-vaccination rationale to compulsory sterilization, upholding Virginia's eugenical sterilization act with the now-infamous line that three generations of imbeciles are enough. The opinion treated sterilization as a police-power measure parallel to vaccination, citing Jacobson directly. The doctrine's defenders point out that the case has procedural-safeguards holdings that arguably distinguish it; its critics note that no court has ever formally overruled it, and that Skinner v Oklahoma (1942) limited it sub silentio rather than confronting it. The case is the cautionary tale Chandler returns to repeatedly: deference to legislative judgment under the police power has a floor that should not be drawn by the legislature alone. See [[Cases/Buck v Bell (1927)|full brief]].

### West Coast Hotel Co v Parrish (1937): The End of Lochner

Chief Justice Hughes's 5-4 majority upheld Washington State's minimum wage law for women, expressly overruling Adkins v Children's Hospital (1923). The opinion made three doctrinal moves that ended the Lochner era. First, freedom of contract appears nowhere in the constitutional text. Second, the Constitution speaks only of liberty, and liberty under the Constitution is liberty in a social organization that requires the protection of law against evils that menace the health, safety, morals, and welfare of the people. Third, regulation that is reasonable in relation to its subject and adopted in the interests of the community satisfies due process. Roberts's vote switch from the Adkins coalition produced the majority. The decision did not formally overrule Lochner but it took away the methodological framework on which Lochner depended. See [[Cases/West Coast Hotel Co v Parrish (1937)|full brief]].

### United States v Carolene Products (1938): The Replacement Architecture

Justice Stone's opinion the next Term provided the framework that replaced Lochner-era review. The body of the opinion announced a strong presumption of constitutionality for regulatory legislation affecting ordinary commercial transactions, with the existence of supporting facts presumed and the courts forbidden to reweigh legislative policy. Footnote Four created three carve-outs: legislation that appears on its face to violate a specific constitutional prohibition, legislation that restricts the political processes that ordinarily bring about repeal of undesirable laws, and legislation directed at discrete and insular minorities whose political safeguards have been curtailed. The combination of body and footnote produced the modern architecture: deferential rational basis for ordinary police-power exercises, with heightened scrutiny carved out only for the three categories Footnote Four identified. See [[Cases/United States v Carolene Products (1938)|full brief]].

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side

1. **Hypo:** A state legislature, responding to a measles outbreak in unvaccinated school-age children, passes a statute requiring measles vaccination as a condition of public school attendance and providing only a narrow medical exemption. A parent challenges the law as exceeding the police power and violating substantive due process liberty. **Why:** This sits squarely on the Jacobson axis. The interest is public health, the means is a vaccination requirement responding to a contagious-disease emergency, and Jacobson's real-and-substantial-relation test would apply. The narrow medical-exemption design tracks the safeguards Holmes identified in Buck v Bell as relevant to procedural fairness; the school-attendance condition is a less invasive design than Jacobson's direct-fine model. Under both Jacobson and post-1937 rational basis review, the police-power exercise is valid.

2. **Hypo:** A municipality enacts a comprehensive housing code requiring landlords to install smoke detectors, repair structural defects, and meet minimum sanitation standards. A landlord challenges the code as exceeding the police power because it imposes substantial costs without addressing any imminent public-health emergency. **Why:** West Coast Hotel established that economic regulation in the public interest survives ordinary due process review without need for emergency conditions. Carolene Products's body-text presumption of constitutionality applies because no Footnote Four carve-out is implicated. The legislature could rationally conclude that minimum housing standards protect health, safety, and welfare. The landlord's economic-cost objection is precisely the sort of policy disagreement Williamson said must be resolved through elections rather than litigation.

### Opposite-Side

3. **Hypo:** A state legislature, citing concern about declining birth rates and population quality, passes a statute authorizing involuntary sterilization of individuals identified by a state board as hereditarily unfit, with a hearing and an appeal. A challenger argues the statute exceeds the police power. **Why:** Buck v Bell's narrow textual reading would arguably uphold the statute, but every modern doctrinal current cuts the other way. Skinner v Oklahoma identified procreation as a fundamental right triggering strict scrutiny; Footnote Four reserves heightened review for laws touching political-process and discrete-and-insular-minority concerns; Cruzan and Dobbs reframe bodily autonomy outside the Jacobson logic; and Buck's empirical premise (that the targeted disabilities were heritable) is now known to be false. The police power cannot survive strict scrutiny because compulsory sterilization is not narrowly tailored to any compelling interest a contemporary court would credit.

4. **Hypo:** A state legislature, citing harm to children from "indecent" speech, prohibits booksellers from selling any book that depicts profanity to a customer under twenty-one and imposes criminal penalties on the seller. A bookseller challenges the law as exceeding the police power. **Why:** This implicates the First Amendment, a specific constitutional prohibition that Footnote Four's first carve-out singles out for heightened scrutiny. The police-power label cannot insulate the statute from First Amendment analysis. Even framed as an exercise of the historical state interest in protecting minors from harmful content, the statute would have to survive strict scrutiny under the modern free-speech framework, which the underinclusiveness and overbreadth of the twenty-one-year cutoff would defeat.

### Fence-Sitter

5. **Hypo:** During a viral pandemic, a state's Department of Health issues an emergency order closing all houses of worship to in-person services for ninety days while permitting grocery stores, hardware stores, and big-box retailers to remain open with capacity limits. A church challenges the order as exceeding the police power and violating the Free Exercise Clause. **Why:** Two doctrinal frames pull in opposite directions and resist easy reconciliation. The Jacobson public-health frame would defer broadly to state health officials during a contagion emergency, treating the order as a paradigmatic exercise of the police power. The Footnote Four / modern free-exercise frame would apply heightened scrutiny because the order discriminates between religious assembly and commercial assembly, triggering Fulton's individualized-exemptions analysis (see [[Topics/Free Exercise of Religion|Free Exercise Topic]]). Whether the police-power deference of Jacobson survives a comparator-driven free-exercise challenge is genuinely contested in the post-2020 case law, with Diocese of Brooklyn pulling toward strict scrutiny and South Bay Pentecostal initially pulling toward deference. The fact pattern is designed to force the student to choose between Jacobson's deferential frame and Fulton's neutrality-and-general-applicability frame and to defend the choice on principled grounds.

## How to Spot on an Exam

Police-power language usually appears in the prompt as a state regulation challenged on Fourteenth Amendment substantive due process or equal protection grounds. The first move is always to identify the state interest the regulation purportedly serves and to ask whether it falls within the historical police-power categories of public health, public safety, public morals, or general welfare. The second move is to apply the right level of scrutiny.

For ordinary economic and social regulation, the post-1937 default is rational basis review under Williamson and Carolene Products. The state interest can be conjectured by the court; the means need not be the best available; the legislature's empirical judgments are presumed correct. A challenger almost never wins under this framework.

The exit ramps are Footnote Four's three carve-outs. A specific constitutional prohibition (free exercise, free speech, an enumerated criminal-procedure protection) triggers the relevant clause's own scrutiny standard, not police-power deference. A political-process restriction (voting rights, ballot access) triggers the apportionment and election cases' framework. A law directed at a discrete and insular minority triggers equal protection's tiered scrutiny depending on the classification.

For substantive due process challenges, the modern question is whether the regulation burdens a fundamental right (privacy, marriage, parental autonomy, intimate conduct, contraception, in some formulations abortion before Dobbs). If yes, strict scrutiny applies and the state interest must be compelling and the means narrowly tailored. If no, rational basis applies and the police-power label essentially decides the case.

The trap pattern is to assume that the Lochner-era cases (Meyer, Pierce, Buck) are still authoritative on what counts as a police-power exercise. Meyer and Pierce survive only because their liberty interests have been recategorized as fundamental rights. Buck has never been formally overruled but no court would apply it today. Cite these cases for their role in the doctrinal arc, not as direct authority for what is or is not a permissible exercise of the police power in 2026.

## Critique

The police power is the doctrinal placeholder for everything the Constitution does not federalize. That makes it both indispensable and impossibly broad. Critics from a libertarian-textualist orientation argue that the Carolene Products move (deference for ordinary economic regulation, heightened scrutiny only for the Footnote Four carve-outs) made the police power coextensive with whatever the legislature labels it, leaving courts no principled basis to push back when the legislature gets economic policy badly wrong. From this perspective, the West Coast Hotel framework abandons the Court's structural role of policing legislative judgment.

Critics from a New Deal and democratic-process orientation argue the opposite: the Lochner-era treatment of the police power as a contested category in every economic case let unelected judges substitute their economic theories for those of the legislature, and West Coast Hotel correctly returned that judgment to the political branches. On this view, the modern doctrine's main defect is not that it is too deferential but that it is not deferential enough in cases where the Court still finds substantive due process exits (Lochner, Roe, Casey, the modern punitive-damages cases) without acknowledging that it is doing exactly what Lochner did.

A separate line of critique attacks the police power's historical concept itself. Slaughter-House described the police power as a sovereign authority predating the Constitution, but the Reconstruction Amendments arguably reframed the relationship between citizen and state in a way that the Slaughter-House majority refused to confront. On this view (revived by the contemporary scholarly interest in the Privileges or Immunities Clause), the police power should have been narrowed by the Fourteenth Amendment's affirmative protection of national citizenship rights against state abridgment, and Slaughter-House's residual-sovereignty framing left the Amendment without the substantive content its drafters intended.

Buck v Bell remains the case that haunts every defense of police-power deference. Holmes's reasoning was internally consistent with Jacobson, the procedural safeguards Virginia provided were facially adequate, and the legislative judgment about heritability rested on the dominant scientific consensus of the day. Yet the result is universally condemned. The case is therefore the strongest evidence that police-power deference cannot be defended on procedural-correctness grounds alone; it requires an independent floor of liberty against which legislative judgments can be measured. Where that floor lives in the post-Carolene Products framework, and how robustly the Footnote Four carve-outs supply it, is the question that animates the modern individual-rights cases.
