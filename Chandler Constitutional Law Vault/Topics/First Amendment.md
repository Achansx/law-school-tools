# First Amendment

## Overview

The First Amendment houses four distinct doctrinal clusters: Free Speech, Government Speech and Spending Conditions, the Establishment Clause, and the Free Exercise Clause. Each cluster has its own test, its own foundational cases, and (in the vault) often its own dedicated Topic page. This umbrella's job is to frame the clusters and route the reader, not to absorb the per-cluster doctrine.

The eleven anchor briefs in this Topic's `key_cases` track the four clusters: Texas v Johnson, Miller, Wisconsin v Mitchell, and 303 Creative on Free Speech; Rust, Velazquez, AID v AOSI, and Walker on Government Speech and Spending Conditions; Van Orden on the Establishment Clause; and Chiles v Salazar on the speech-versus-conduct line that pulls into Free Exercise as well. Foundational free-speech cases that predate the listed anchors (Schenck, Brandenburg, NYT v Sullivan, Cohen v California) appear elsewhere in the vault as orphans awaiting Ingest.

The single most important analytical move is cluster identification. A flag-burning prosecution and a religious-display dispute and a federal funding condition all live in the First Amendment, but they run on different tests; identifying which cluster a fact pattern belongs to is half the answer.

---

## Governing Rule

> **Rule:** The First Amendment is not a single rule. The four clusters run on separate tests:
> - **Free Speech.** Content-based regulation of speech triggers strict scrutiny (compelling interest, narrow tailoring). Viewpoint-based regulation is presumptively unconstitutional and rarely survives. Content-neutral time-place-manner regulations get intermediate review under O'Brien / Ward v Rock Against Racism. Unprotected categories (incitement under Brandenburg, true threats, fighting words, obscenity under Miller, certain forms of fraud) are regulable on a category-specific test. Compelled speech is treated as an aggravated form of content-based regulation (303 Creative).
> - **Government Speech and Spending Conditions.** When the government itself is speaking, the Free Speech Clause does not constrain its message (Walker, Summum). When the government conditions a subsidy or program participation on private speech, Rust governs program-defining conditions and AID v AOSI / Velazquez govern conditions that reach beyond the program to control unrelated speech.
> - **Establishment Clause.** After Kennedy v Bremerton (2022), the test is history and tradition: the Court asks whether the challenged practice fits within the historical understanding of an establishment of religion. Lemon's three-prong test (purpose, effect, entanglement) has been formally retired, though the endorsement test still has residual force in some lower courts.
> - **Free Exercise.** Under Employment Division v Smith (1990), neutral and generally applicable laws receive rational basis even if they incidentally burden religious exercise. Non-neutral or non-generally-applicable laws (Lukumi, Tandon, Fulton) trigger strict scrutiny. The Smith framework is under sustained pressure but has not been overruled.

## Doctrinal Development

The four clusters do not march in chronological lockstep. The development is best traced cluster by cluster.

### Free Speech (Texas v Johnson, Miller, Wisconsin v Mitchell, 303 Creative)

Texas v Johnson (1989) holds that flag-burning is expressive conduct protected by the First Amendment, and content-based prohibitions of expressive conduct trigger strict scrutiny. Brennan's majority is the canonical statement that the bedrock of the speech clause is government cannot prohibit expression because society finds the idea itself offensive or disagreeable.

Miller v California (1973) sets the three-prong obscenity test (whether the average person, applying contemporary community standards, would find the work appeals to the prurient interest; whether it depicts sexual conduct in a patently offensive way; and whether it lacks serious literary, artistic, political, or scientific value). Obscene speech is unprotected; Ashcroft v Free Speech Coalition (2002) cabins Miller by holding that virtual child pornography that does not depict actual minors is not unprotected under the New York v Ferber line and that Congress's prohibition was overbroad.

Wisconsin v Mitchell (1993) holds that hate-crime sentencing enhancements directed at the defendant's discriminatory motive (rather than at speech as such) are not constitutionally barred. The case is the principal counter-cite to RAV v St Paul (1992) and stands for the proposition that motive evidence is conduct evidence, not speech.

303 Creative LLC v Elenis (2023) holds that the First Amendment forbids a state from using a public-accommodations law to compel a website designer to create speech she disagrees with. The case marks the modern high-water mark of the compelled-speech doctrine and produces the open question of where expressive conduct ends and ordinary commercial conduct begins. See [[Topics/Compelled Speech|Compelled Speech]] for the cluster's modern center of gravity.

### Government Speech and Spending Conditions (Rust, Velazquez, AID v AOSI, Walker)

Rust v Sullivan (1991) holds that the government may selectively fund a program (here, family-planning counseling that did not include abortion as a method of family planning) without thereby creating an unconstitutional condition on private speech: the government is defining the program's content, not penalizing speech outside the program. Rust is the program-defining anchor.

Legal Services Corp v Velazquez (2001) and AID v AOSI (2013) hold the line: a funding condition that prohibits LSC-funded attorneys from challenging the validity of existing welfare law (Velazquez) or that requires HIV/AIDS grantees to adopt an explicit policy opposing prostitution (AID v AOSI) reaches beyond the program to control speech the grantee would say with its own funds. Conditions that operate that way are unconstitutional under the unconstitutional-conditions doctrine.

Walker v Sons of Confederate Veterans (2015) holds that Texas specialty license plates are government speech, not private speech, and Texas may therefore decline to issue a Confederate-flag plate without triggering viewpoint-discrimination doctrine. Walker imports the Pleasant Grove City v Summum framework into the license-plate context. Critics argue that Walker is the upper bound of the government-speech doctrine; below that line, the speaker is private and viewpoint-discrimination doctrine applies.

### Establishment Clause (Van Orden)

Van Orden v Perry (2005) is the best vault anchor on the post-Lemon transition. Justice Breyer's controlling concurrence treated a Ten Commandments monument on Texas State Capitol grounds as constitutionally permissible on a "passive monument with longstanding history" theory, distinguishing the contemporaneous McCreary County v ACLU result striking down newer courthouse displays. Van Orden read with Kennedy v Bremerton (2022) marks the doctrinal shift toward history-and-tradition reasoning that displaced Lemon and the endorsement test. See [[Topics/Establishment Clause|Establishment Clause]].

### Free Exercise (Chiles v Salazar)

Chiles v Salazar (2026) reaches the speech-versus-conduct line in the Free Exercise / Compelled Speech cluster: Colorado labeled certain conversion-therapy practices "conduct" rather than "speech," and the Court rejected that label as a means of sidestepping First Amendment review. The case sits at the cross-cluster boundary between Free Speech (compelled-speech / professional-speech) and Free Exercise (religious counseling). See [[Topics/Free Exercise of Religion|Free Exercise of Religion]] for the cluster's full development.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side

1. **Hypo:** A state enacts a law making it a misdemeanor to burn the state flag at a political protest. A protester convicted under the law appeals. **Why:** Direct application of Texas v Johnson. State flag-burning regulation is content-based (it singles out the message of disrespect), so it triggers strict scrutiny and almost certainly fails.
2. **Hypo:** A federal grant program for sex-education curricula limits funding to programs that teach abstinence as the only fully effective prevention method. **Why:** Direct application of Rust v Sullivan. The condition defines the program's content rather than reaching beyond the program to control private speech; constitutional under the unconstitutional-conditions doctrine.

### Opposite-Side

3. **Hypo:** A federal grant program for legal services prohibits grantees from using their own non-federal funds to challenge state restrictions on voter registration. **Why:** Velazquez controls. The condition reaches beyond the program to control speech (and litigation) the grantee would conduct with private funds. The condition is unconstitutional as an unconstitutional condition on protected speech.
4. **Hypo:** A small bakery, refusing on religious grounds to bake a wedding cake for a same-sex couple, is sued under the state's public-accommodations law. The bakery argues the cake design is expressive speech. **Why:** Distinct from 303 Creative. The Court's compelled-speech doctrine requires that the regulated activity be speech in a meaningful sense; standardized bakery products are likely conduct rather than speech under Masterpiece's narrow ruling and 303 Creative's design-as-speech reasoning. The case turns on the speech-versus-conduct line and on whether the bakery's product is sufficiently expressive to be analogized to a custom website design.

### Fence-Sitter

5. **Hypo:** A public university art-history department, using a state-funded museum, declines to display a student-curated exhibit that critiques a sitting governor's policies, citing the institution's editorial discretion as a public museum. **Why:** This pulls into Walker. If the museum's curation is government speech, the state may exercise viewpoint discretion; if the museum operates a forum for student speech, viewpoint-neutrality applies. The case turns on factors the Court has flagged but not synthesized: who controls the message, whether private speakers reasonably understand the curated work as the museum's own, and how much latitude state institutions have to invoke government-speech status to shield their funding decisions from First Amendment review.

## How to Spot on an Exam

The first analytical move is cluster identification.

- **Trigger facts for Free Speech:** A government actor restricts, punishes, or compels private expression. Identify whether the regulation is content-based, viewpoint-based, or content-neutral; identify whether the speech falls in an unprotected category; run the appropriate tier of scrutiny.
- **Trigger facts for Government Speech / Spending Conditions:** The government is itself speaking, or it is funding a speaker on conditions. Identify whether the speaker is the government or a private grantee, then run Walker / Rust / Velazquez as appropriate.
- **Trigger facts for the Establishment Clause:** Government action with religious content, government symbol, government-funded religious activity, government-coerced religious participation. Run the post-Bremerton history-and-tradition test, with Van Orden's passive-monument reasoning still controlling on long-standing displays.
- **Trigger facts for Free Exercise:** A neutral law that incidentally burdens religious exercise (Smith), a non-neutral or non-generally-applicable law that targets religious practice (Lukumi / Tandon), or a state policy that operates differently against religious practice than against secular comparators (Fulton).

Watch for cross-cluster cases (Chiles, the Masterpiece / 303 Creative line) where the right answer requires running both Free Speech and Free Exercise frames.

A rough disambiguation rule for grading: if the conduct is private and the government is not speaking, you are in Free Speech; if the government is speaking or funding, you are in the second cluster; if the conduct is religious, you are in the third or fourth depending on whether the dispute is about state endorsement (third) or state burden on the practitioner (fourth).

## Critique

Three strands of criticism dominate the modern First Amendment literature.

The first concerns the speech-versus-conduct line. 303 Creative and Chiles push hard on the question of when commercial or professional activity counts as speech for First Amendment purposes. Critics argue the Court's recent doctrine treats too much commercial activity as expressive (and thus shielded from public-accommodations and professional-regulation laws); defenders argue the Court is recovering a long-suppressed acknowledgement that custom expressive products are not interchangeable widgets.

The second concerns the government-speech doctrine after Walker and Summum. Critics fear the doctrine, taken seriously, can absorb every government-funded forum and erase viewpoint-neutrality protection in spaces that look private to ordinary speakers. Defenders point to the Court's careful framing of the doctrine and to the unconstitutional-conditions floor under Velazquez and AID v AOSI.

The third concerns the post-Lemon Establishment Clause. Kennedy v Bremerton retired Lemon for history-and-tradition reasoning, but the Court has not yet supplied a stable analytical framework for cases that do not present a Bremerton-style coercion record. Whether any test other than ad hoc "look at history" emerges is the cluster's most pressing open question. See [[Topics/Establishment Clause|Establishment Clause]] for the cluster-internal critique.

A meta-critique points out that the Free Exercise Clause's Smith framework has been under sustained pressure since Lukumi (1993) without being overruled, and that the Tandon / Fulton "comparator" doctrine has done much of the substantive work that an outright Smith reversal would have done. Whether the Clause is now de facto under strict scrutiny by any other name is contested.
