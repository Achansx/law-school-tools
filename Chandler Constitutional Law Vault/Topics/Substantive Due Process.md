# Substantive Due Process

## Overview

Substantive due process is the doctrine that the word *liberty* in the Due Process Clauses protects certain rights against government deprivation on the merits, not merely through fair procedure. In the incorporation context, it is the textual vehicle the Supreme Court has used, with a few historical exceptions, to apply provisions of the Bill of Rights against the States through the Fourteenth Amendment. The phrase itself is contested: Justice Thomas has written that "substantive due process is a contradiction in terms," yet it remains the operative route by which almost every right now enforceable against a State became enforceable.

This page treats substantive due process primarily as an incorporation vehicle. The broader doctrine has two sister arcs that now have their own dedicated Topic pages so the incorporation framework does not have to absorb the load of two unrelated doctrinal lines: see [[Topics/Substantive Due Process - Economic|Substantive Due Process - Economic]] for the Calder / Lochner / Jacobson / West Coast Hotel / Carolene Products / Williamson / BMW v Gore lineage of economic liberty, and [[Topics/Substantive Due Process - Privacy and Autonomy|Substantive Due Process - Privacy and Autonomy]] for the Meyer / Pierce / Griswold / Loving / Roe / Casey / Lawrence / Obergefell / Dobbs lineage. The three arcs share the same textual hook (the word "liberty" in the Due Process Clause) and the same historical objection ("substantive due process is a contradiction in terms"), but their live-doctrine status diverges sharply: incorporation is stable and near-unanimous; economic SDP is dead letter except for the punitive-damages pocket; privacy SDP remains the most volatile of the three after Dobbs (2022).

---

## Governing Rule

> A provision of the Bill of Rights is enforceable against the States through the Fourteenth Amendment Due Process Clause when the right is fundamental. The Court has articulated the fundamentality inquiry three different ways across the incorporation lineage, and the three formulations are not interchangeable. *Palko v Connecticut* (1937) asked whether the right is "implicit in the concept of ordered liberty," a philosophical standard benchmarked against what any civilized legal system would require. *Duncan v Louisiana* (1968) reframed the inquiry to whether the right is "fundamental to the American scheme of justice," narrowing the evidence base from abstract civilization to concrete Anglo-American practice. *McDonald v City of Chicago* (2010) and *Timbs v Indiana* (2019) package the modern test as two components, asking whether the right is "fundamental to our scheme of ordered liberty" or "deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition," foregrounding historical evidence at the Founding and at Reconstruction. Once incorporated under any of these formulations, the right applies to the States with the same scope it has against the federal government: *Duncan*'s no-watered-down principle forecloses weaker state-level versions.

---

## Doctrinal Development

The doctrine moves in three long steps. First, rejection. In *Barron v Baltimore* (1833), Marshall held the Bill of Rights binds only the federal government. For decades after the Fourteenth Amendment, the Court refused to change that result, and the P or I Clause that might have done so was shut down in *Slaughter-House* (1873) and *Cruikshank* (1876). The lecture treats *Cruikshank* as the doctrinal hinge: once P or I was foreclosed, the Court had to choose between doing nothing and routing rights through Due Process. It chose Due Process.

Second, early selective incorporation. *Chicago, Burlington & Quincy R.R. v Chicago* (1897) incorporated the Takings Clause. *Gitlow v New York* (1925) assumed speech incorporation in dictum. *Palko v Connecticut* (1937), read in this vault, provided the first general test: a right is incorporated if it is "implicit in the concept of ordered liberty" such that denial would violate "a principle of justice so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental." Cardozo used that test to deny incorporation of double jeopardy, a result later overruled.

Third, the modern test. *Duncan v Louisiana* (1968) refined the inquiry: the question is not whether a right would exist in some imaginable civilized system but whether it is fundamental to the American scheme of justice. That shift matters because it lets American history and tradition do real work. *Duncan* also locked in the "no watered-down" principle: a right enforceable against the States applies with the same scope it has against the federal government. *McDonald v City of Chicago* (2010) applied that test to the Second Amendment, and the Alito plurality framed incorporation as the default once a right is fundamental and deeply rooted. *Timbs v Indiana* (2019) extended the test to the Excessive Fines Clause, applying the "deeply rooted" prong with an extensive historical survey from Magna Carta through the state constitutions.

## Comparison: SDP Vehicle vs P or I Alternative

The table below compares the two textual vehicles for incorporation across the axes that most often decide exam questions and doctrinal debates. The left column is the operative doctrine (Alito plurality in *McDonald*, Kagan unanimous in *Timbs*). The right column is the revival argument (Thomas concurrence in *McDonald* and *Timbs*, joined by Gorsuch in *Timbs* on a bookmark basis).

| Axis | Substantive Due Process (operative) | Privileges or Immunities (revival) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Textual home | Fourteenth Amendment Due Process Clause, reading "liberty" substantively | Fourteenth Amendment Privileges or Immunities Clause |
| Foundational objection | "Substantive due process is a contradiction in terms"; procedure does not protect substance | *Slaughter-House* (1873) narrowed the Clause to a near-empty set; restoring it requires overruling 150 years of precedent |
| What the test asks | Is the right fundamental to our scheme of ordered liberty, or deeply rooted in Nation's history and tradition | Is the right among the privileges or immunities of American citizenship at the time of the Fourteenth Amendment's ratification (1868) |
| Evidence base | Anglo-American practice at the Founding and at Reconstruction, plus modern state practice under *Duncan* | Reconstruction-era sources (debates, state constitutions, contemporaneous legal treatises) foregrounded, because the Clause's meaning is fixed to what "privileges or immunities" meant to the ratifying public in 1868 |
| Typical modern vote count | Unanimous or near-unanimous (*Timbs* 9 to 0) on the existence of incorporation; splits arise on scope | Never commanded a Court majority; strongest single-Justice statement is Thomas's *Timbs* concurrence |
| Doctrinal risk if adopted | Low, because it merely continues the existing rule | High, because overruling *Slaughter-House* could reopen economic-rights arguments the modern Court has avoided since *Lochner* |
| Interaction with *Lochner* / *Griswold* / *Obergefell* lineage | Same textual vehicle carries both incorporation and non-enumerated liberty claims, which is part of the critique | Would formally separate incorporation (P or I) from freestanding substantive rights (where Thomas's own approach is to reject both) |
| Ally votes | Roberts Court majority in the incorporation cases | Thomas (sole author in *McDonald*); Gorsuch joined as a "worth revisiting" signal in *Timbs*; no other Justice has taken the position |

The practical upshot is that the two doctrines reach the same result on every incorporation case the modern Court has decided. The disagreement is methodological, and the methodological gap becomes load-bearing only if the Court reaches a non-enumerated-liberty question (*Dobbs*-style) where the SDP vehicle carries work the P or I Clause was never asked to do.

## Comparison: Glucksberg Two-Part Test vs. Kennedy Emerging-Awareness Methodology

This table covers the unenumerated-liberty lineage of substantive due process (the second doctrinal track flagged in the Overview), not the incorporation cases that otherwise dominate this page. It maps the two competing methodologies for identifying whether an unenumerated liberty interest is fundamental.

| Axis | [[Cases/Washington v Glucksberg (1997)\|Glucksberg]] Two-Part Test (controlling) | Kennedy Emerging-Awareness Approach ([[Cases/Obergefell v Hodges (2015)\|Obergefell]]) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Controlling authority | Majority in Glucksberg (1997); reaffirmed as the governing standard in Dobbs v Jackson (2022) | Kennedy majority in Obergefell (2015); available for liberty claims grounded in dignity and relational rights; scope narrowed post-Dobbs |
| Step one: framing the right | Careful description at the most specific traditional level: ask for the right as it was historically understood, not at an abstract level (e.g., "right of a terminally ill person to physician-assisted suicide," not "right to liberty") | Right defined at an abstract level capturing the broader liberty interest (e.g., "right to marry," not "right to same-sex marriage"); level of generality reflects how the Court defines the interest for all similarly situated persons |
| Step two: historical inquiry | Objectively, deeply rooted in the Nation's history and tradition AND implicit in the concept of ordered liberty; historical pedigree at the specific level claimed is required | Historical practice plus evolving understanding of liberty; tradition's arc matters even when the specific practice was historically denied to a discrete group, because denial by prior majorities does not establish a tradition of non-recognition |
| Effect on novel claims | Presumptively defeats claims for rights without a specific historical pedigree at the carefully described level; the test's framing function can defeat the claim before the historical inquiry begins | More receptive to rights denied to identifiable groups (same-sex couples, women seeking abortions), on the theory that those groups were excluded from the tradition-forming process |
| Post-Dobbs status | Alito majority explicitly reaffirmed Glucksberg as the controlling SDP methodology, rejecting Kennedy's higher-generality approach; Dobbs overrules Roe and Casey, both of which had worked around Glucksberg | Obergefell was formally distinguished in Dobbs (marriage involves no potential life); Thomas concurrence in Dobbs flagged Obergefell as a candidate for future reconsideration; majority said nothing either way |
| Key dissents | Kennedy (in Glucksberg) and Souter argue that the careful-description requirement pregames the outcome; Stevens argues history of denial is evidence against, not for, traditional protection | Roberts, Scalia, Thomas in Obergefell: Kennedy's emerging-awareness move is "unprincipled" because it lets the Court reach any result by choosing the level of generality it prefers |
| Exam application | Apply Glucksberg after Dobbs unless the claim is specifically Obergefell-pattern (same-sex marriage, Lawrence-pattern intimate liberty); always state the right at the narrowest historically defensible level before beginning the historical inquiry | Flag that Kennedy's methodology is formally available in Obergefell-pattern cases but is not available for newly asserted rights where the Court has not yet adopted it; after Dobbs, Glucksberg is the default |

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side

**1. State Criminal Defendant Invokes Grand Jury Indictment.** A State charges a defendant with a serious felony by information rather than by grand jury indictment. The defendant argues the Fifth Amendment Grand Jury Clause should be incorporated. Applying *Duncan*'s test, the court must ask whether grand jury indictment is fundamental to the American scheme of justice and deeply rooted in Nation's history and tradition. The honest answer, and the Court's longstanding answer from *Hurtado v California* (1884), is no: most States do not use grand juries for felony charges and never have, so the practice is not deeply rooted in Nation's practice. Under the *Palko* test this was already the result, and under *Duncan* it remains so. The defendant loses.

**2. Civil Jury in State Court Worth $21.** A State statute eliminates civil juries for disputes under $1,000. The litigant invokes the Seventh Amendment. Again the court applies *Duncan*, and again the historical answer is no: the Seventh Amendment has never been incorporated, and state civil jury practice varies too widely to find a deeply rooted national tradition. The same reasoning *Palko* applied to double jeopardy (the right is useful but not indispensable) disposes of this claim.

### Opposite-Side

**3. State Prohibits Interlocutory Appeal of Excessive Bail.** A State bail statute forbids pretrial appeal of a bail determination no matter how high. A detainee challenges the scheme under the Eighth Amendment Excessive Bail Clause. The *Timbs* template is directly on point: the Eighth Amendment Excessive Fines Clause was incorporated because it was deeply rooted in English and early American tradition back to Magna Carta, and the Excessive Bail Clause has the same textual home and the same historical pedigree. A court would almost certainly hold the Excessive Bail Clause incorporated and apply it with full scope, not a watered-down version. The detainee wins on incorporation, though the merits of the bail determination are a separate question.

**4. State Refuses to Let Criminal Defendant Confront Accuser.** A State evidentiary rule permits conviction on the testimony of a witness who never appears at trial, on the theory that state law allows this in child abuse cases. The defendant invokes the Sixth Amendment Confrontation Clause. The *Duncan* test is satisfied: the Confrontation Clause was incorporated in *Pointer v Texas* (1965), and under the no-watered-down principle the Clause applies to the State with the same force it has federally. The State cannot create a state-law exception narrower than the federal rule. Defendant wins.

### Fence-Sitter

**5. State Criminalizes Conduct Not Addressed Anywhere in the Bill of Rights.** A State criminalizes being present within 100 feet of any public demonstration, full stop. The challenger argues the statute violates substantive due process because liberty includes freedom of movement in public spaces. This is the hard case. It is not a Bill of Rights incorporation claim at all. It is the controversial freestanding substantive due process lineage that runs through *Lochner*, *Griswold*, *Roe*, and *Obergefell*. The incorporation cases briefed here do not resolve it. A court following the Alito plurality in *McDonald* would ask whether the asserted liberty is deeply rooted in Nation's history and tradition, and a court following a broader reading of *Lawrence v Texas* would ask whether the State has a legitimate interest. Thomas would say the entire inquiry is textually incoherent and would route any freestanding liberty argument through P or I. The case sits outside the clear incorporation template, which is exactly why law-school exams love it.

## How to Spot on an Exam

Watch for three triggers. First, the phrase "through the Fourteenth Amendment" or a state government doing the thing that would obviously be unconstitutional if the federal government did it. That is the incorporation pattern; the answer is the *Duncan* test and the no-watered-down principle. Second, a history-heavy fact pattern asking whether a newly asserted right is "deeply rooted." That is *Timbs* framing; walk through English precedent, Founding-era state constitutions, state practice at the time of the Fourteenth Amendment, and modern practice. Third, a prompt that baits you with the phrase "substantive due process" in a non-incorporation context. Flag that you recognize two different doctrines sit under that label: the incorporation line (*Palko*, *Duncan*, *McDonald*, *Timbs*) and the freestanding liberty line (*Lochner*, *Griswold*, *Obergefell*), and that only the first is covered by the incorporation cases. That distinction is worth real points.

Do not confuse *Palko*'s "ordered liberty" with *Duncan*'s "American scheme of justice." They sound similar, but *Duncan* is more demanding in one direction (it looks to American practice, not abstract civilization) and more protective in another (it rejects watered-down incorporation). *Palko* is famous for losing, *Duncan* for the rule we still apply.

---

## Critique

The textual objection, made most forcefully by Thomas in *McDonald*, is that due process protects procedure, not substance, and that reading "liberty" in the Due Process Clause as a substantive source of rights against the States is grammatically incoherent. If the framers of the Fourteenth Amendment wanted to protect substantive rights, they had a clause for that: Privileges or Immunities. The Court chose Due Process only because *Slaughter-House* and *Cruikshank* had already killed P or I, and the Thomas argument is that perpetuating a textual error is worse than correcting it.

The historical objection is narrower. *Palko* and its progeny use American history and tradition as both sword and shield, and historical answers are contested. *McDonald* is a good example: the Breyer dissent reads the history of gun regulation as broadly tolerant of state police-power regulation, while Alito reads it as protective of an individual right. The same historical materials generate opposite conclusions.

The institutional objection is that substantive due process has been, across its full history, a vehicle for the Court to strike down laws it dislikes. *Lochner* used it to invalidate economic regulation; later courts used it to protect contraception, abortion, and same-sex marriage. Whether one celebrates or condemns those results, the pattern is striking: a clause about procedure has become the Court's most flexible tool for substantive review. The lecture flags that pattern without resolving it.

The pragmatic defense, implicit in the Alito plurality and the Kagan-era unanimous *Timbs* opinion, is that the alternative (overruling *Slaughter-House* and rerouting everything through P or I) would require the Court to reopen 150 years of settled precedent. Given stare decisis, substantive due process is the doctrine we have. That is not a textual defense. It is a defense from path dependence.

## Comparison: Loving's Dual-Holding Deployment Across Downstream Marriage and Civil-Rights Cases

[[Cases/Loving v Virginia (1967)|Loving]] carried two independently sufficient grounds (Equal Protection and Due Process). Subsequent marriage and civil-rights cases pick and choose among those branches, often deploying one but not the other. The table maps the deployment, which explains why the Loving paradox matters doctrinally: a case that accepts Loving's EP branch but rejects its DP branch (Dobbs) reaches different conclusions about the availability of Loving as precedent than a case that embraces both (Obergefell).

| Axis | [[Cases/Loving v Virginia (1967)\|Loving (1967)]] | [[Cases/Obergefell v Hodges (2015)\|Obergefell (2015)]] | [[Cases/Dobbs v Jackson Women's Health Organization (2022)\|Dobbs (2022)]] | [[Cases/Students for Fair Admissions v Harvard (2023)\|SFFA (2023)]] |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Equal Protection deployment | Core holding: racial classifications in criminal statutes demand the "most rigid scrutiny" and fail absent a permissible state objective independent of racial discrimination; "equal application" is no defense | Present but secondary: Kennedy treats EP and DP as interwoven strands of the Fourteenth Amendment rather than independent grounds; the EP analysis is comparatively thin | Acknowledges Loving's EP branch and treats it as the reason Loving is distinguishable from Roe, whose single-ground SDP holding lacked Loving's racial-classification anchor | Majority invokes Loving for the colorblind-Constitution principle (no racial classifications, symmetrical or otherwise) — Sotomayor's dissent invokes Loving for the opposite proposition that race-conscious remedies correct racial hierarchy |
| Due Process deployment | Core holding: marriage is a fundamental right whose denial on racial grounds deprives citizens of liberty without due process; Meyer / Skinner are the authority chain | Primary ground: Kennedy extends Loving's fundamental-right-to-marry holding to same-sex couples by defining the right at a level of generality that does not incorporate historical restrictions on marriage partners | Distinguished and partially narrowed: Alito reads Loving's DP branch as inseparable from its EP branch, avoiding a Loving-grounded affirmative rule for non-racial SDP claims; Thomas concurrence urges reconsideration of the entire Griswold/Lawrence/Obergefell line | Not directly deployed; SFFA is an EP case with no DP holding, so Loving's DP branch is beside the point |
| Level of generality move | Warren treats the right as "marriage" without theorizing why interracial marriage is a specific exercise of it; the generality level is implicit, not defended | Kennedy makes the level-of-generality move explicit: "marriage" rather than "same-sex marriage," responding to Glucksberg's careful-description test by arguing history of exclusion is not a tradition of exclusion | Alito explicitly repudiates Kennedy's high-generality move; Glucksberg's careful-description rule is reaffirmed as controlling SDP methodology | N/A (EP case) |
| "Equal application" argument | Rejected: formal symmetry does not cure a racial classification when the statute's articulated purpose (white supremacy) is impermissible | Same-sex-marriage analog: state laws that "apply equally" to all same-sex couples regardless of individual circumstance fail for the same structural reason | Not at issue (abortion regulation applies to all pregnant persons with no asymmetric-across-race structure) | Roberts majority rejects diversity-rationale race-conscious admissions as themselves racial classifications regardless of which races are advantaged; effectively redeploys the equal-application reasoning against race-conscious remedies |
| Loving-paradox status | Originating tension: history-and-tradition test would defeat Loving on anti-miscegenation precedent dating to 1691 Virginia; Warren implicitly applies an equality filter to traditions rooted in caste | Resolved in Loving's favor: Kennedy makes the equality filter explicit by redefining the right at a level of generality that excludes tradition-based restrictions on disfavored groups | Reopened: Alito reaffirms Glucksberg without addressing how anti-miscegenation traditions fit; critics (Kavanaugh and Roberts concurrences; scholarly commentary) read the Dobbs methodology as threatening Loving's DP branch even as the majority disclaims that implication | Not engaged directly; SFFA does not ask whether historical race-consciousness was ever permissible, only whether modern race-consciousness is |
| Loving's precedential footprint | Dual-ground holding — maximally protective for subsequent plaintiffs | Expanded — both branches deployed and extended | Narrowed — DP branch read as inseparable from EP branch, limiting its reach | Contested — same text, opposite doctrinal uses by majority and dissent |

- [[Cases/Obergefell v Hodges (2015)|Obergefell v Hodges (2015)]] -- Kennedy's majority applies SDP's fundamental-rights methodology at a higher level of generality than Glucksberg's "careful description" requirement, creating methodological tension that Dobbs partially resolves in Glucksberg's favor; the methodology comparison table above maps the two approaches.
- [[Cases/Lawrence v Texas (2003)|Lawrence v Texas (2003)]] -- Kennedy's majority extends SDP to private adult consensual sexual conduct, applying "emerging awareness" rather than Glucksberg's careful-description test; overrules Bowers v Hardwick after only seventeen years, demonstrating that stare decisis operates differently in constitutional cases; Thomas's Dobbs concurrence flags Lawrence as a candidate for reconsideration under a strict Glucksberg framework.
- [[Cases/Loving v Virginia (1967)|Loving v Virginia (1967)]] -- Warren's dual-holding structure grounds anti-miscegenation invalidation in both the Equal Protection Clause (racial classifications demand the most rigid scrutiny) and the Due Process Clause (marriage is a fundamental right); the due-process branch imports the Meyer/Skinner fundamental-rights lineage into the anti-miscegenation context and becomes the doctrinal pivot for Zablocki, Turner, and Obergefell; the Loving paradox that Professor Chandler emphasizes (anti-miscegenation laws are themselves "deeply rooted in history and tradition," so Glucksberg's tradition-only test cannot be the full answer) is the structural tension Obergefell later makes explicit and Dobbs partially reopens.
- [[Cases/Dobbs v Jackson Women's Health Organization (2022)|Dobbs v Jackson Women's Health Organization (2022)]] -- Alito's majority overrules Roe v Wade and Planned Parenthood v Casey, explicitly reaffirming Glucksberg as the controlling SDP methodology; the decision identifies abortion as distinct because it involves potential life, but Thomas's concurrence reads the majority's methodology to require reconsideration of Griswold, Lawrence, and Obergefell as well; the most significant SDP overruling in modern history.
- [[Cases/Palko v Connecticut (1937)]] for the original test in full context.
- [[Cases/Duncan v Louisiana (1968)]] for the modern refinement and the no-watered-down principle.
- [[Cases/McDonald v City of Chicago (2010)]] for the Alito plurality applying the modern test and the Thomas concurrence attacking the entire doctrine.
- [[Cases/Timbs v Indiana (2019)]] for the unanimous modern application with a full historical survey.
- [[Cases/Meyer v Nebraska (1923)|Meyer v Nebraska (1923)]] as the earliest non-economic SDP decision and the root of the fundamental-liberties catalog that Glucksberg, Loving, Griswold, and Obergefell later build on: engagement in common occupations, acquisition of useful knowledge, marriage, home, children, worship, recognized common-law privileges. Meyer's survival of the 1937 repudiation is the reason SDP continued as a doctrine after West Coast Hotel.
- [[Cases/Pierce v Society of Sisters (1925)|Pierce v Society of Sisters (1925)]] as Meyer's canonical companion and the second half of the "Meyer-Pierce" parental-authority unit; together, Meyer and Pierce are the load-bearing citations by which Footnote Four Paragraph 3 (in Carolene Products) reabsorbed the Lochner era's non-economic liberty holdings into the post-1937 representation-reinforcement framework.
