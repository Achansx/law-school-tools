# Voting Rights

## Overview

The Voting Rights doctrine asks how far Congress may go in protecting the franchise against state interference, and on what constitutional source the protection rests. The answer has shifted across three doctrinal eras. In the Reconstruction era, the Fifteenth Amendment promised that the right to vote would not be denied or abridged on account of race, and Congress passed the Enforcement Act of 1870 to implement that promise; the Civil Rights Cases (1883) then narrowed the entire enforcement project by limiting federal power to state action. In the Second Reconstruction, beginning with the Voting Rights Act of 1965, the Court read the enforcement clauses generously: Katzenbach v Morgan (1966) recast Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment as a positive grant of legislative power measured by McCulloch v Maryland, and City of Rome (1980) confirmed that Section 2 of the Fifteenth Amendment lets Congress prohibit electoral changes with a discriminatory effect even where no purposeful discrimination is shown. In the modern era, City of Boerne v Flores (1997) imposed a congruence-and-proportionality limit on Section 5 enforcement legislation outside the voting-rights core, recasting Katzenbach as a voting-specific precedent rather than a general license for substantive expansion.

The structural significance of Voting Rights doctrine extends beyond the ballot. Because the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments postdate the Eleventh and by their own terms restrict the states, properly grounded voting-rights legislation is one of the few reliable paths for Congress to abrogate state sovereign immunity. The doctrine therefore sits at the intersection of Individual Rights (the franchise), Equal Protection (race-based exclusion), and Federalism (state sovereignty over voter qualifications).

## Governing Rule

> **Rule (paraphrased):** Congress may enforce the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments by appropriate legislation, including prophylactic measures that reach state conduct not itself unconstitutional. For Fifteenth Amendment Section 2 voting-rights enforcement, Katzenbach v Morgan and City of Rome supply the operative frame: Congress may legislate so long as the Court can perceive a rational basis, and may prohibit electoral changes with a discriminatory effect without proof of discriminatory purpose. For Fourteenth Amendment Section 5 legislation that reaches further, City of Boerne adds a limit: the remedy must be congruent and proportional to a documented pattern of state-actor constitutional injury, and Congress may not redefine the substantive scope of the right.

> **Marshall in City of Rome (verbatim, Midpage-verified):** "[P]rinciples of federalism that might otherwise be an obstacle to congressional authority are necessarily overridden by the power to enforce the Civil War Amendments 'by appropriate legislation.' Those Amendments were specifically designed as an expansion of federal power and an intrusion on state sovereignty." [Source: https://app.midpage.ai/document/city-of-rome-v-united-110248?utm_source=mcp&lines=158-160]

> **Brennan in Katzenbach v Morgan (verbatim, Midpage-verified):** "Correctly viewed, § 5 is a positive grant of legislative power authorizing Congress to exercise its discretion in determining whether and what legislation is needed to secure the guarantees of the Fourteenth Amendment." [Source: https://app.midpage.ai/document/katzenbach-v-morgan-107256?utm_source=mcp&lines=33-34]

## Doctrinal Development

The four briefed cases trace an arc from the Reconstruction state-action floor, through the Warren and Burger Court's deferential reading of the enforcement clauses, to the Rehnquist Court's calibration of Section 5 around a documented-injury record. Each case sits in the vault as a full brief; this section summarizes their positions in the arc and links to the briefs for the full treatment.

### Civil Rights Cases (1883): the state-action floor

The Court invalidated Sections 1 and 2 of the Civil Rights Act of 1875, holding that the Fourteenth Amendment reaches only state action, not private conduct, and that Congress's Section 5 power is limited to corrective legislation directed at prohibited state action. The decision constrained Reconstruction-era civil rights enforcement for eight decades. For voting rights specifically, the Civil Rights Cases left the Fifteenth Amendment as the more direct constitutional vehicle, since the Fifteenth Amendment by its terms prohibits state denial or abridgment of the franchise on account of race; private interference with voting required separate doctrinal grounding (and was the subject of the 1870 Enforcement Act's anti-conspiracy provisions, themselves narrowed in United States v Cruikshank (1876)). See [[Cases/Civil Rights Cases (1883)|full brief]].

### Katzenbach v Morgan (1966): Section 5 as a positive grant of power

Justice Brennan's majority upheld Section 4(e) of the Voting Rights Act of 1965, which suspended New York's English literacy requirement for voters who had completed sixth grade in an American-flag school where instruction was in a language other than English. The majority adopted the McCulloch v Maryland standard as the measure of "appropriate legislation" under Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment: Congress may enact any means appropriate and plainly adapted to enforcing the Amendment's guarantees, so long as the means are not prohibited by the Constitution. Critically, Congress did not need a prior judicial finding that the state law was unconstitutional; it was enough that the Court could perceive a rational basis for Congress's judgment that federal intervention was needed. The opinion's footnote 10 also floated a "ratchet theory" suggesting Congress could expand but not contract the substantive reach of Fourteenth Amendment rights, a view the Court would later reject in City of Boerne. See [[Cases/Katzenbach v Morgan (1966)|full brief]].

### City of Rome v United States (1980): discriminatory effect and the Fifteenth Amendment

Justice Marshall's majority sustained three features of the Voting Rights Act as applied to the city of Rome, Georgia. First, Section 5 of the Voting Rights Act validly prohibits electoral changes with a discriminatory effect, even where no discriminatory purpose is shown; the statute's conjunctive "purpose and effect" language means preclearance is denied unless both are absent, and Congress had authority under Section 2 of the Fifteenth Amendment to enact such prophylactic legislation beyond the Amendment's own Section 1 floor of intentional discrimination. Second, the 1975 extension of the Act was constitutional because Rome had not shown the Act's coverage had outlived its usefulness for the covered jurisdiction. Third, municipalities in a covered state cannot independently bail out of preclearance; bailout must be sought by the state as a whole. City of Rome confirms that voting-rights enforcement under the Fifteenth Amendment retains a McCulloch-style deferential frame even after some of the Burger Court's federalism turn. See [[Cases/City of Rome v United States (1980)|full brief]].

### City of Boerne v Flores (1997): congruence, proportionality, and the recasting of Katzenbach

Justice Kennedy's majority struck down the Religious Freedom Restoration Act as applied to the states, holding that Section 5 enforcement legislation must be remedial and preventive, not substantive. The remedy must show congruence and proportionality between the documented pattern of state-actor constitutional injury and the means adopted. RFRA failed because its legislative record contained almost no recent state religious discrimination, yet it imposed the most demanding test in constitutional law on every generally applicable state law that incidentally burdened religion. Boerne explicitly recast Katzenbach v Morgan as a remedial voting-rights case, narrowing rather than overruling it: when Congress legislates to protect the franchise on the strength of a documented record of state racial discrimination in voting, the McCulloch frame holds; when Congress legislates beyond that core to reach state conduct not connected to a documented record, congruence and proportionality apply. See [[Cases/City of Boerne v Flores (1997)|full brief]].

The arc ends with two unresolved questions that flow into the modern docket: whether the congruence-and-proportionality test reaches Fifteenth Amendment Section 2 enforcement (Boerne addressed Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment, leaving the Fifteenth Amendment frame ambiguous), and whether the Voting Rights Act's coverage formula can be revived after Shelby County v Holder (2013) invalidated the Section 4(b) formula as applied to current conditions. Both questions are listed in the open questions on this page.

## Comparison: McCulloch Frame versus Congruence and Proportionality

| Feature | Katzenbach v Morgan and City of Rome (McCulloch frame) | City of Boerne (congruence and proportionality) |
|---------|-----------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------|
| Source of enforcement power | Fourteenth Amendment Section 5 (Katzenbach), Fifteenth Amendment Section 2 (City of Rome) | Fourteenth Amendment Section 5 |
| Required predicate | Rational basis the Court can perceive for Congress's judgment | Documented pattern of state-actor constitutional injury |
| Permitted reach | Means appropriate and plainly adapted to enforcement, including prophylactic remedies | Remedies congruent and proportional to the documented injury |
| Intent versus effect | Discriminatory effect sufficient (City of Rome) | Effect alone insufficient absent record evidence of intent-driven state action |
| Treatment of substantive expansion | Initially permitted under the Katzenbach footnote 10 ratchet theory | Forbidden; Section 5 is remedial and preventive only |
| Doctrinal status | Surviving in the Fifteenth Amendment voting-rights core | Governing in non-voting Section 5 legislation; reach into Fifteenth Amendment unresolved |

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side

1. **Hypo:** Congress enacts a federal statute requiring preclearance of any change to a state's vote-by-mail rules in jurisdictions that have lost a Department of Justice voting-rights enforcement action in the last fifteen years. The statute is grounded in Section 2 of the Fifteenth Amendment and supported by a 400-page legislative record documenting recent purposeful and effect-based race discrimination in those jurisdictions. **Why:** Tracks the City of Rome frame: a documented record of state-actor voting discrimination, a remedy reaching effect alone, and Fifteenth Amendment Section 2 grounding all line up with the McCulloch deferential standard.
2. **Hypo:** Congress reauthorizes a successor to VRA Section 5 with a coverage formula tied to current-decade rates of voter list purges that disproportionately remove minority voters, plus a bailout provision keyed to ten consecutive years of compliance. **Why:** Responds directly to the Shelby County current-conditions critique and matches the congruence-and-proportionality frame even if the Court extends Boerne to Fifteenth Amendment legislation; the documented record and tailored remedy are aligned.

### Opposite-Side

3. **Hypo:** Congress enacts a federal statute imposing a least-restrictive-means review on every state regulation that incidentally burdens a citizen's ability to register to vote, including parking restrictions near polling places and absentee-ballot postage rules. The legislative record contains a handful of anecdotes and no systematic findings. **Why:** A direct Boerne replay: an extraordinarily broad remedy applied across all incidental burdens, no documented pattern of intent-based state-actor injury, and a substantive expansion of the right to vote beyond what Equal Protection or the Fifteenth Amendment recognizes. Fails congruence and proportionality.
4. **Hypo:** Congress prohibits all state voter identification requirements as conclusively presumed to violate the Fourteenth Amendment, with no factual finding of discriminatory purpose or effect. **Why:** Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment cannot be used to redefine what Equal Protection substantively requires; the Court has sustained voter ID requirements against constitutional challenge, and a flat congressional reversal would be substantive lawmaking under Boerne.

### Fence-Sitter

5. **Hypo:** Congress reauthorizes VRA Section 5 with a new coverage formula keyed to a state's overall voter participation gap (white minus minority turnout) over the last three federal cycles, with no requirement of evidence of intent and no bailout shorter than fifteen years. The record documents a turnout gap pattern but does not isolate state-actor causation for it. **Why:** The McCulloch frame from City of Rome would likely sustain it on Fifteenth Amendment Section 2 grounds because Congress is targeting a discriminatory effect on the franchise, but the absence of state-actor causation in the record raises a Boerne-style fit problem if the Court extends congruence and proportionality into the Fifteenth Amendment space. Outcome turns on whether Boerne reaches the Fifteenth Amendment.

## How to Spot on an Exam

A Voting Rights issue typically appears when a federal statute regulates state electoral conduct and a state argues Congress lacks enforcement power, or when a private plaintiff sues a state for damages and the court must decide whether the suit fits within abrogation under the Fourteenth or Fifteenth Amendment. Trigger facts include preclearance regimes, federal limits on voter qualifications (literacy, ID, age, residency), federal bans on electoral changes (redistricting, polling-place consolidation, list maintenance), and private suits against state election officials for money damages.

The analytical framework runs in four steps. First, identify the constitutional source: is the federal action grounded in Article I (most likely the Elections Clause for federal elections, or the Spending Clause for conditional funding), the Fourteenth Amendment Section 5, or the Fifteenth Amendment Section 2. Second, classify the harm Congress is targeting: race-based denial or abridgment of the franchise (Fifteenth Amendment core), unequal access tied to race or other suspect classifications (Fourteenth Amendment Section 1 plus Section 5), or some other voting-related injury. Third, choose the enforcement frame: McCulloch deference for the Fifteenth Amendment voting-rights core under Katzenbach and City of Rome; congruence and proportionality under Boerne when the legislation reaches non-voting state conduct or rewrites the substance of Section 1 rights. Fourth, evaluate the legislative record: the more recent and specific the documentation of state-actor injury, the stronger the legislation under either frame. If the question involves abrogation of state sovereign immunity, confirm that the statute rests on a Reconstruction enforcement clause and not Article I, since Seminole Tribe and Florida Prepaid foreclose Article I abrogation.

## Critique

The doctrinal arc rewards careful textualism but is in tension with itself. Katzenbach v Morgan and City of Rome read the enforcement clauses as positive grants of power measured by McCulloch's familiar deferential test, while City of Boerne demands a documented record of state-actor injury and a tailored remedy. Boerne distinguished Katzenbach by recasting it as a voting-rights case, but the recasting was retrofitted: Justice Brennan's opinion in Katzenbach was framed as a general theory of Section 5, with voting as the application rather than the limiting principle. Whether the Court's distinction is principled or pragmatic remains contested.

A second critique runs to the federalism cost. Marshall's City of Rome opinion observed that the Reconstruction Amendments were "specifically designed as an expansion of federal power and an intrusion on state sovereignty," yet the modern Court has retreated toward what Justice Stevens called a partial restoration of pre-1865 dual sovereignty. Critics argue this shift is hardest to defend in the voting-rights context, where the Fifteenth Amendment by its text addresses precisely the state-sovereignty interest at issue and Section 2 by design displaces state autonomy over voter qualifications when race discrimination is the target.

A third critique points to the Shelby County problem. Even if City of Rome's deferential frame survives, it cannot be invoked unless Congress can certify which jurisdictions remain covered. Shelby County's invalidation of the Section 4(b) coverage formula left that question to congressional action that has not arrived. The result is a doctrinal core (Section 5 preclearance) without a workable trigger, raising the question whether voting-rights enforcement has effectively reverted to the post-1883 baseline of case-by-case litigation under Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act.
