# Text History and Tradition

## Overview

Text-history-and-tradition is the methodology the Supreme Court announced in Bruen to replace the two-step framework the lower courts had developed after Heller. Under the pre-Bruen framework, a court would first ask whether the regulated conduct fell within the Second Amendment's scope as informed by text and history, and then apply means-end scrutiny (typically intermediate scrutiny) at the second step. Bruen rejected that second step as one step too many and substituted a two-part test: the challenger shows that the plain text covers the conduct; then the government must produce a relevantly similar historical analogue from the Nation's tradition of firearm regulation. Rahimi clarified what relevantly similar means in practice. The methodology is nominally a Second Amendment test, but the dissent in Bruen and the structure of related doctrines (Dobbs's history-and-tradition inquiry in substantive due process, Kennedy v Bremerton's turn away from the Lemon test) suggest that text-history-and-tradition is a methodological claim with ambitions beyond the Second Amendment.

---

## Governing Rule

> **Rule:** When the Second Amendment's plain text covers an individual's conduct, the Constitution presumptively protects that conduct. The government must then demonstrate that the challenged regulation is consistent with this Nation's historical tradition of firearm regulation. The government need not identify a historical twin. It must identify a relevantly similar historical analogue, measured by two metrics: how the regulation burdens the right (comparable burden) and why the regulation was imposed (comparable justification). Founding-era (1791) and Reconstruction-era (1868) evidence receive the most weight; later outliers carry less. Means-end scrutiny has no role.

## Doctrinal Development

### Heller (2008): Text and History as Threshold Work

Heller is the methodological precursor. Scalia's originalist parsing of the operative and prefatory clauses, the historical survey from English antecedents through post-ratification commentary, and the rejection of Breyer's interest-balancing approach all prefigure Bruen's framework. Heller stops short of articulating a general methodology for adjudicating challenges to firearm regulations. It answers the threshold individual-right question through text and history, lists presumptively lawful regulations without placing them in any analytical framework, and declines to specify a standard of review. The consensus reading in the lower courts was that Heller left room for means-end scrutiny at the second step, and that reading coalesced into the pre-Bruen two-step framework Bruen displaces. See [[Cases/District of Columbia v Heller (2008)|full brief]].

### McDonald (2010): History-and-Tradition as Incorporation Work

McDonald is not a text-history-and-tradition case in the same sense as Bruen and Rahimi, but it contributes a related formulation. The plurality's selective-incorporation test asks whether a right is fundamental to our scheme of ordered liberty and deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition. That deeply-rooted formulation is methodologically continuous with Palko's implicit-in-the-concept-of-ordered-liberty framing and with Washington v Glucksberg's deeply-rooted substantive-due-process test. McDonald uses history to establish that the right qualifies for incorporation; Bruen later uses history to adjudicate the scope and content of particular applications of the right. See [[Cases/McDonald v City of Chicago (2010)|full brief]].

### Bruen (2022): The Methodology Announced

Bruen is where text-history-and-tradition is articulated as an affirmative framework. Thomas's six-Justice majority rejects the two-step framework the courts of appeals had developed, holding it is one step too many. The governing test has two parts. Step one is the challenger's burden: when the Second Amendment's plain text covers an individual's conduct, the Constitution presumptively protects that conduct. Step two is the government's burden: the government must then demonstrate that the regulation is consistent with this Nation's historical tradition of firearm regulation. The Court acknowledges that applying the test requires analogical reasoning and specifies two metrics the analogy must satisfy: how the regulation burdens the right and why it imposes that burden. Founding-era (1791) and Reconstruction-era (1868) evidence receive the greatest weight because those are the moments at which the relevant constitutional text was adopted. The Court also notes that the historical inquiry is more demanding when a regulation addresses a general societal problem that has persisted since the eighteenth century and more flexible when the regulation addresses unprecedented societal concerns or dramatic technological changes. Breyer's dissent (joined by Sotomayor and Kagan) argues that the history-only test is indeterminate, that the choice of level of generality often determines the outcome, and that the methodology has implications far beyond the Second Amendment. See [[Cases/New York State Rifle and Pistol Association v Bruen (2022)|full brief]].

### Rahimi (2024): The Analogue, Not the Twin

Rahimi is the Court's first major post-Bruen application. After the Fifth Circuit invalidated § 922(g)(8) on the ground that the government had failed to identify a sufficiently similar founding-era statute, the Supreme Court reversed 8-1. Roberts's opinion clarifies that the Second Amendment is not trapped in amber. The test requires a historical analogue, not a historical twin. The how and why metrics ask whether the modern law is comparable to a historical regulation in the burden it imposes and in the reason for imposing that burden, without requiring identical regulatory mechanics. The two historical analogues the Court identifies for § 922(g)(8) are the surety tradition (requiring persons shown to threaten the peace to post a bond or be jailed) and the common-law prohibition on going armed to the terror of the people. Together these show a long-standing principle: when an individual has been adjudicated to pose a credible threat of physical violence, temporary disarmament is permissible. Rahimi also introduces two doctrinal stabilizers that carry significant weight in the analysis: temporariness (the disarmament lasts only while the order is in effect) and judicial process (a neutral magistrate must make a credible-threat finding after notice and a hearing). Thomas dissents alone, arguing that the majority has diluted his own Bruen framework by permitting piecemeal analogies that mix the burden of one historical law with the justification of another. See [[Cases/United States v Rahimi (2024)|full brief]].

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side

1. **Hypo:** A federal statute bars firearm possession by persons subject to a civil protective order issued after notice and a hearing that includes a specific finding of credible threat of physical violence to an identified individual. **Why:** This is Rahimi. The surety tradition and the going-armed tradition supply a historical analogue that is relevantly similar in burden and justification, and the temporariness and judicial-process features track the doctrinal stabilizers the Court emphasized.

2. **Hypo:** A state statute bans concealed carry in legislative chambers, polling places, and courthouses. **Why:** Bruen (adopting Heller's dicta) preserves a sensitive-places doctrine for exactly these paradigm locations, and the historical record of firearm prohibitions in those places is clear. The how and why metrics are satisfied because historical regulations imposed comparable location-specific bans for comparable reasons.

### Opposite-Side

3. **Hypo:** A state may-issue concealed carry regime requires applicants to demonstrate a specific and articulable threat beyond that faced by the general public. **Why:** This is Bruen. New York's proper-cause requirement failed precisely because the government could not identify a historical tradition of conditioning the right to carry at all on a showing of special need. The absence of regulation is itself evidence of tradition.

4. **Hypo:** A municipal ordinance declares every public park, subway platform, and shopping district a sensitive place and prohibits carry in each. **Why:** Bruen warns against expanding the sensitive-places category so broadly that all places of public congregation become sensitive places. That expansion would eviscerate the general right, and no relevantly similar historical tradition supports such a broad location-based prohibition.

### Fence-Sitter

5. **Hypo:** A state statute requires all firearms manufactured after a specified date to include microstamping technology that imprints a unique identifier on each cartridge case fired. **Why:** Does the statute burden conduct the plain text protects (step one)? If yes, can the government identify a relevantly similar historical tradition of regulating the identifiable characteristics of firearms (step two)? Bruen allows a more nuanced approach when a regulation addresses dramatic technological changes, but the level-of-generality question looms: at a high level the government can analogize to marking and registration regimes, while at a low level no historical regulation imposed microstamping-equivalent requirements. Lower courts have divided on technology-specific regulations after Bruen, and this is precisely the pressure point Breyer's dissent and Thomas's Rahimi dissent both flag.

## How to Spot on an Exam

A text-history-and-tradition issue arises whenever a modern firearm regulation is challenged under the Second Amendment, and increasingly in other contexts where courts are gravitating toward historical methodologies.

1. **Frame the first step correctly.** The challenger's burden is narrow: does the plain text cover the conduct? Do not import scope limitations at this step. The Second Amendment covers keep and bear for self-defense (Heller) and public carry (Bruen). Resist answering the scope question with means-end analysis.

2. **Shift the burden to the government.** Once the text covers the conduct, the government must produce a relevantly similar historical analogue. Do not accept a generic appeal to public safety. Look for specific founding-era or Reconstruction-era regulations and assess the how (burden) and why (justification) of each.

3. **Apply the how and why metrics.** The modern law need not match the historical analogue's mechanics. It must impose a comparable burden on the right for comparable reasons. Surety laws analogized to disarmament is the paradigm Rahimi example: different mechanics (bond versus confiscation), same principle (preemptive restraint of a person adjudicated to threaten the peace).

4. **Watch the level of generality.** This is the methodology's central pressure point. A judge who describes the historical regulation at a high level of generality will find more analogues. A judge who describes it narrowly will find fewer. Flag the level-of-generality question whenever the modern law is novel or the historical record is mixed.

5. **Deploy the doctrinal stabilizers.** Rahimi teaches that temporariness and judicial process do significant work. A restriction that is temporary and tied to an adjudicative finding fits more comfortably within the surety tradition than a permanent categorical disqualification. Kavanaugh's Bruen concurrence (shall-issue permissible, may-issue suspect) is the parallel stabilizer in the licensing context.

## Critique

Text-history-and-tradition's central vulnerability is administrability. The methodology claims to eliminate judicial discretion by replacing means-end balancing with historical inquiry, but the choice of level of generality often determines the outcome. Breyer's Bruen dissent argued this as a theoretical matter. Rahimi proved it concretely: the Fifth Circuit and the Supreme Court applied the same test to the same statute and reached opposite results because they chose different levels of generality. The majority's answer in Rahimi is that analogical reasoning has always required judgment, and that courts should use the how and why metrics to discipline the inquiry. Thomas's solo dissent answers that the majority's version of analogical reasoning collapses into mixing and matching, where the government is permitted to pair the burden of one historical law with the justification of another until some combination satisfies the test. The disagreement between the author of Bruen and the Court that applied Bruen two Terms later is the sharpest sign that the methodology has not yet stabilized.

A second critique is selectivity. The Court describes text-history-and-tradition as the methodology the Second Amendment demands, but the rest of the Bill of Rights is still adjudicated under means-end scrutiny. Breyer's Bruen dissent asked the right question: if means-end scrutiny is inappropriate for firearms, on what ground does the Court continue to apply it in First Amendment, equal protection, and due process cases? If the methodology is general, it portends a sweeping transformation of constitutional adjudication across many domains. If it is specific to the Second Amendment, the Court owes an explanation for why this right alone is exempt. The lecture materials suggest that Kennedy v Bremerton (Establishment Clause) and post-Dobbs substantive due process cases point toward the general reading.

A third critique is source-of-tradition. The Court emphasizes Founding-era and Reconstruction-era evidence, but the selection of which historical record counts is itself a methodological choice. Late-nineteenth-century Western territorial laws receive little weight, antebellum Southern laws are often treated as aberrational, and the absence of regulation is treated as evidence of a tradition permitting the conduct. Each of those choices is defensible but also contestable, and together they give the methodology more maneuvering room than its proponents acknowledge.

Finally, the shall-issue-versus-may-issue distinction that Kavanaugh's Bruen concurrence stabilizes and the temporariness-and-judicial-process distinction that Rahimi stabilizes are textually thin. Neither is grounded in any particular historical analogue; both are practical compromises that preserve existing regulation. That is a legitimate judicial choice, but it sits uneasily with the methodology's insistence that only text, history, and tradition may constrain modern regulation.
