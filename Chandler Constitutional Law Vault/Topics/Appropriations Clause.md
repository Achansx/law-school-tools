# Appropriations Clause

## Overview

The Appropriations Clause of Article I Section 9 Clause 7 is the constitutional cornerstone of the congressional power of the purse. Its prohibition is structural: no money may be withdrawn from the Treasury except in consequence of an appropriation made by law. The provision implements the Framers' decision to vest fiscal control in Congress as the institution most directly accountable to the people, and to constrain executive discretion over public expenditure to the role of faithful execution rather than independent direction.

The doctrine spans three connected questions. First, what counts as an "appropriation made by Law"? Consumer Financial Protection Bureau v Community Financial Services Association of America (2024) supplies the operative definition: a statutory enactment that identifies a specified source of public money and designates the purposes for which it may be spent. The Clause imposes no requirement of annual recurrence, fixed nominal amount, or temporal duration; standing or permanent appropriations satisfy it provided those three elements are present. Second, may the President withhold or refuse to spend funds Congress has appropriated? Train v City of New York (1975) supplies the answer for direct impoundment: no, the President's Article II Take Care duty obliges disbursement of funds Congress has appropriated for designated purposes. Third, may Congress delegate to the President the power to cancel enacted appropriations through a line-item veto? Clinton v City of New York (1998) answers no, on Presentment Clause grounds: cancellation of an enacted appropriation is itself a legislative act, and bicameralism and presentment are mandatory for any change to enacted law.

Read together, these three rules establish the modern doctrinal frame. Congress holds plenary power over the design of appropriations. The President's role is execution, not revision. Once Congress has spoken in an enacted statute, neither unilateral executive impoundment nor statutorily delegated executive cancellation can reach the appropriation; only another enacted law can. The doctrine is also one of the rare structural areas in which a formalist Court reaches a result that Congress affirmatively wants: the rules vindicate legislative supremacy over fiscal questions, even when individual congressional plaintiffs lack standing to litigate the issue themselves (Raines v Byrd, 1997, on the line-item veto's first round).

The CFPB ruling in 2024 added a contemporary twist by confirming that an agency funded through a permanent statutory authorization to draw on a non-Treasury source (Federal Reserve earnings, in CFPB's case) does not violate the Clause merely because the funds avoid the annual appropriations cycle. The decision crystallized a textualist reading the Clause has long supported but had never been applied so squarely to a major regulatory agency, and it foreclosed a Fifth Circuit theory that would have unwound much of the modern administrative state's funding architecture.

## Governing Rule

> **Rule (definition of an appropriation, current law, CFPB v CFSA 2024):** An "appropriation made by Law" within the meaning of Article I Section 9 Clause 7 requires three elements and only three: (1) a statutory enactment, (2) identifying a specified source of public money, and (3) designating purposes for which the money may be spent. The Clause imposes no constitutional requirement of annual appropriations, fixed nominal amounts, time limits, or any other temporal or numerical structure. Standing or permanent appropriations satisfy the Clause.

> **Thomas in CFPB v CFSA (verbatim, Midpage-verified):** "Under the Appropriations Clause, an appropriation is simply a law that authorizes expenditures from a specified source of public money for designated purposes." [Source: https://app.midpage.ai/document/consumer-financial-protection-bureau-v-9969339?utm_source=mcp&lines=151]

> **Rule (impoundment prohibition, Train v New York 1975):** Once Congress has appropriated funds for specifically designated purposes and has not granted the President discretion to spend less, the President's refusal to disburse the appropriated amounts violates separation of powers. The President's Article II Take Care duty is to execute, not to substitute his own spending priorities for Congress's. The Impoundment Control Act of 1974 supplies the statutory framework that channels permissible presidential spending flexibility through deferrals (subject to congressional override) and rescissions (requiring affirmative congressional approval).

> **Rule (line-item veto prohibition, Clinton v New York 1998):** Congress cannot by statute authorize the President to cancel individual provisions of an enacted appropriations bill. Cancellation of an enacted statutory provision is itself a legislative act, and Article I Section 7 Presentment Clause requires that any change to enacted law pass both houses of Congress and be presented to the President. The Line Item Veto Act of 1996 violated this requirement and was struck down.

> **Constitutional text:** "No Money shall be drawn from the Treasury, but in Consequence of Appropriations made by Law; and a regular Statement and Account of the Receipts and Expenditures of all public Money shall be published from time to time." (Article I Section 9 Clause 7)

## Doctrinal Development

The Appropriations Clause arc has three eras: the early ministerial-duty foundation, the Nixon-era impoundment confrontation, and the modern formalist consolidation that includes CFPB v CFSA. Each era refines a different facet of the same underlying principle that Congress controls the fiscal authority and the executive's role is execution.

### Kendall v United States ex rel Stokes (1838): the ministerial-duty foundation

The Postmaster General refused, on instructions from President Jackson, to credit funds the Solicitor of the Treasury had certified as owed to a mail contractor. The Supreme Court upheld a writ of mandamus compelling the credit. Congress had prescribed a specific duty by statute; that duty was ministerial because the statute left no room for executive discretion. Courts could compel performance of ministerial acts notwithstanding the President's general supervisory authority. Kendall is the Appropriations Clause's enforcement-mechanism foundation: when Congress has appropriated funds and prescribed the manner of their disbursement, the executive cannot shelter behind presidential authority to refuse payment, and judicial mandamus is available to compel performance. The ministerial / discretionary distinction Kendall draws runs through every later impoundment case and is the doctrinal backbone of judicial review of executive non-disbursement. (Developed in [[Lectures/Impoundment and Presidential Spending Power|Impoundment and Presidential Spending Power]]; no vault brief yet.)

### Train v City of New York (1975): direct impoundment is unconstitutional

Congress passed the Federal Water Pollution Control Act Amendments of 1972 over President Nixon's veto, appropriating specific dollar amounts for state and municipal wastewater treatment grants. President Nixon directed the EPA Administrator to allot to the states only a fraction of the appropriated funds, withholding the balance as part of a broader anti-inflation impoundment policy. New York City and other municipalities sued. A unanimous Supreme Court held the impoundment unlawful. The statute did not give the President discretion to allot less than the full appropriated amount; the President's refusal to spend was therefore a refusal to execute the law, not an exercise of statutory discretion. Train confirms that direct impoundment of specifically appropriated funds violates separation of powers, and it supplies the doctrinal predicate for the Impoundment Control Act of 1974, which Congress enacted in part to formalize the constitutional baseline Train articulated. (Developed in [[Lectures/Impoundment and Presidential Spending Power|Impoundment and Presidential Spending Power]]; no vault brief yet.)

### Clinton v City of New York (1998): the Line Item Veto Act fails Presentment

The Line Item Veto Act of 1996 authorized the President, after signing an appropriations bill, to "cancel" individual spending items, targeted tax benefits, or limited entitlement expansions. President Clinton used the Act to cancel a Medicaid provision favorable to New York and a tax benefit for food processors. New York and the affected processors sued. A 6-3 Court struck down the Act. Justice Stevens's majority reasoned that cancellation of an enacted statutory provision is an exercise of legislative power; the Constitution permits changes to enacted law only through bicameralism and presentment. Allowing the President to unilaterally cancel provisions of duly enacted statutes was the functional equivalent of permitting partial repeal without going through Article I Section 7 again. Clinton thus closes one of the two doors Congress might have used to give the President impoundment-equivalent authority: just as the President cannot impound on his own (Train), Congress cannot delegate the cancellation power by statute. (Developed in [[Lectures/Impoundment and Presidential Spending Power|Impoundment and Presidential Spending Power]] and [[Lectures/Congressional Vetoes and Line Item Vetoes - Chadha Bowsher Clinton|Congressional Vetoes and Line Item Vetoes]]; no vault brief yet.)

### Office of Personnel Management v Richmond (1990): the no-equitable-bypass corollary

Charles Richmond, a federal retiree, lost retirement benefits after relying on incorrect oral advice from a federal employee about how earnings would affect his disability annuity. The Court held that the Appropriations Clause forbade payment because no statute authorized expenditures to those who relied on erroneous government advice; equitable estoppel could not authorize a Treasury disbursement that Congress had not appropriated. Richmond is the Appropriations Clause's anti-loophole rule: even sympathetic facts and arguably tortious government conduct cannot generate a payment from the Treasury without statutory authorization. The case reinforces that the Clause is jurisdictional in the strongest sense, applying to every disbursement of public money regardless of the equities. (Doctrinal context only; no vault brief yet.)

### Consumer Financial Protection Bureau v Community Financial Services Association of America (2024): the operative definition

The Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act of 2010 created the CFPB and gave it an unusual funding mechanism: a standing statutory authorization to draw funds from the earnings of the Federal Reserve System, up to a statutory cap, for consumer-protection purposes. Unlike most federal agencies, the CFPB does not depend on the annual congressional appropriations cycle for operating funds. The Community Financial Services Association of America challenged a 2017 payday-lending rule and argued that the CFPB's entire funding structure violated the Appropriations Clause because it insulated the Bureau from congressional fiscal control. The Fifth Circuit agreed and invalidated the funding mechanism facially, marking the first time in decades that an appellate court had held a federal agency's funding structure unconstitutional. The Supreme Court reversed 7-2, with Justice Thomas writing for the majority. The opinion supplied the operative definition of "appropriation made by Law" that now controls: a statutory enactment identifying a source of public money for designated purposes is sufficient; nothing in the Clause's text mandates an annual cycle, a fixed amount, or a particular duration. Justice Thomas relied heavily on early-Republic practice (the Post Office Department, the Customs Service, the Sinking Fund) to show that permanent appropriations and standing draws on non-Treasury sources had been an accepted feature of federal fiscal architecture from the First Congress forward. Justices Alito and Gorsuch dissented, arguing that the Clause's reference to "the Treasury" and the broader structural commitment to congressional fiscal control implied a tighter accountability link than the majority's three-element test required. See [[Cases/Consumer Financial Protection Bureau v Community Financial Services Association of America (2024)|full brief]].

### Biden v Nebraska (2023): the Appropriations Clause as a Major Questions amplifier

Biden v Nebraska did not directly invalidate executive action on Appropriations Clause grounds. The Court relied principally on the Major Questions Doctrine and the HEROES Act's "waive or modify" delegation. Chief Justice Roberts's majority did, however, treat the Appropriations Clause as an independent reason for heightened scrutiny when the Executive Branch initiates large-scale spending under ambiguous statutory authority: "Congress's Appropriations Clause authority over the purse is precisely where major questions concerns are sharpest." The majority rejected the government's argument that Major Questions analysis should be softer for benefits programs, citing the Appropriations Clause as a structural reason for stricter rather than more lenient review. Biden v Nebraska is therefore relevant to this Topic in two respects: it confirms that the Appropriations Clause is doing structural work alongside the Major Questions framework rather than being subsumed by it, and it provides a doctrinal handle for future challenges to large agency-initiated spending under generic statutory authority. See [[Cases/Biden v Nebraska (2023)|full brief]].

## Comparison: Appropriations Clause versus Spending Clause

The two Article I spending provisions are easily confused. They cover overlapping fact patterns but ask categorically different questions and trigger different doctrinal frames.

| Axis | Appropriations Clause (Art I § 9 cl 7) | Spending Clause (Art I § 8 cl 1) |
|------|----------------------------------------|----------------------------------|
| Question asked | Has the money been validly authorized for disbursement by enacted law? | May Congress spend for the asserted purpose, and may it attach this condition? |
| Doctrinal frame | Procedural / formalist — three-element CFPB v CFSA test (enactment, source, designated purpose) | Substantive / four-part Dole test (general welfare, unambiguous condition, germaneness, no independent constitutional bar) plus NFIB coercion ceiling |
| Relevant actor | Polices Congress (must enact) and the President (must execute, not revise) | Polices Congress's substantive authority over money plus state acceptance of conditions |
| Lead modern case | CFPB v CFSA (2024) | South Dakota v Dole (1987); NFIB v Sebelius (2012) |
| Failure mode | No appropriation, or unilateral executive cancellation/impoundment of an appropriation | Substantive overreach, ambiguous condition, non-germane condition, or coercion of states |
| Co-implication | Both can fail in the same fact pattern: Clinton v City of New York invalidates the Line Item Veto Act under Presentment / Appropriations even before any Spending Clause question reaches the merits | When Congress overspends or coerces, both clauses may fail; when Congress fails to enact at all, only Appropriations Clause is implicated |
| Major Questions overlay | Biden v Nebraska treats unilateral large-scale spending as a heightened-scrutiny trigger within MQD | Dole and NFIB pre-date MQD's modern formulation but supply the coerciveness frame MQD later inherits |

The shorthand: the Spending Clause asks whether Congress *may* spend; the Appropriations Clause asks whether Congress *has* spent in the form the Constitution requires (a duly enacted law identifying source and purpose). Both clauses can be implicated in the same dispute, but a Spending-Clause defect does not cure an Appropriations-Clause defect, and vice versa.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (would survive Appropriations Clause challenge)

1. **Hypo: Agency Funded by a Permanent Authorization to Draw Trust-Fund Earnings.** Congress creates a new federal flood-insurance agency and authorizes it by statute to draw up to a fixed annual cap from the earnings of a previously established disaster-relief trust fund, "for the purposes of maintaining flood-insurance underwriting and claims administration." A challenger argues the agency's exemption from the annual appropriations cycle violates the Clause. **Why:** The statute satisfies the three-element CFPB v CFSA test. There is a statutory enactment (the agency's authorizing legislation), a specified source of public money (earnings of the trust fund), and a designated purpose (flood-insurance underwriting and claims). The Clause does not require the agency to come back to Congress annually. Permanent appropriations from non-Treasury sources have an early-Republic pedigree (Post Office Department, Customs Service) on which Justice Thomas's majority opinion relied, and the statutory cap is the type of accountability feature CFPB v CFSA cited as adequate.

2. **Hypo: Reprogramming Within a Lump-Sum Appropriation.** Congress appropriates $5 billion for "Department of Energy research and development" without specifying allocations among DOE program offices. The Secretary of Energy moves $200 million from a fusion-research line into an advanced-batteries line. A nongovernmental organization sues, arguing that the reprogramming amounts to a partial impoundment of fusion-research funds. **Why:** Train v City of New York forbids impoundment of specifically appropriated funds, but the lump-sum appropriation here is not specifically allocated. Where Congress has chosen to appropriate at the program level rather than the line-item level, the executive's reallocation within that program is the lawful exercise of executive discretion that the appropriation by its terms permits. The Clause is satisfied because the statute identifies a source (Treasury revenues), a purpose ("research and development"), and an enactment; the executive's use of unallocated funds within that envelope does not violate the Clause or impound any specifically designated amount.

### Opposite-Side (would fail Appropriations Clause challenge)

3. **Hypo: Direct Presidential Withholding of Specifically Appropriated Disaster Relief.** Congress appropriates $10 billion in disaster relief for hurricane-affected coastal states, with line items for each state and a directive that funds be disbursed within 180 days of enactment. The President directs the Federal Emergency Management Agency Administrator to allot only $4 billion of the appropriated funds, citing budgetary concerns. **Why:** This is Train v City of New York with the names changed. The statute specifically appropriates the funds and gives the President no discretion to allot less than the full amount. Refusing to disburse is not execution; it is unilateral revision of an enacted appropriation, which the Take Care Clause forbids. The Impoundment Control Act of 1974 supplies the lawful path: the President may propose a rescission, but if Congress does not approve within 45 days, the funds must be released. Bypassing that process is per se unlawful.

4. **Hypo: Statute Authorizing the President to Cancel Future Spending Increases.** Congress passes a statute providing that the President "may cancel" any newly enacted appropriations increase above a designated baseline. The President invokes the authority to cancel a $2 billion increase in National Institutes of Health funding. The Director of NIH sues. **Why:** This is Clinton v City of New York with stylistic relabeling. The cancellation is a legislative act because it changes enacted law; the Presentment Clause requires bicameralism and presentment for any such change. The statute's label as a "cancellation" rather than a "veto" does not save it. Congress cannot delegate to the President the power to revise enacted appropriations through a unilateral post-enactment maneuver, regardless of which formulation the statute uses.

### Fence-Sitter (genuinely unclear)

5. **Hypo: Permanent Appropriation with No Cap and a Vague Purpose.** Congress creates a new agency and authorizes it by statute to draw "such sums as the Director may deem necessary" from the General Fund of the Treasury for the purpose of "promoting public welfare." The agency draws $300 billion in the first year. **Why this is genuinely hard:** CFPB v CFSA expressly reserves whether an uncapped permanent appropriation satisfies the Clause; the CFPB's statutory cap was a feature the majority repeatedly emphasized. The statute satisfies the three named elements, but the dissent's concerns about structural accountability are at their strongest when both the amount and the purpose are open-ended. A textualist reading of the majority opinion produces a same-side answer (the three elements are present), but a structural reading that incorporates the historical dissent's emphasis on Treasury accountability points the opposite way. Layer in non-delegation concerns about "such sums as deemed necessary" and the case becomes a multi-doctrine fence-sitter that could go either way depending on how a future Court frames the threshold question.

## How to Spot on an Exam

Appropriations Clause issues typically arrive in one of three fact patterns. Identify the pattern first, then apply the matching framework.

**First, the funding-architecture challenge.** A litigant attacks the constitutionality of an agency's funding mechanism, arguing that some structural feature (permanent appropriation, non-Treasury source, lack of annual review, uncapped draw) violates the Clause. Apply the CFPB v CFSA three-element test: is there a statute, is there a specified source, are there designated purposes? If yes, the challenge fails on the operative definition. Note any feature that distinguishes the hypothetical from CFPB itself: an uncapped draw is the open question, an unspecified purpose is the strongest challenger argument, and a non-Treasury source is now squarely settled in the agency's favor.

**Second, the impoundment fact pattern.** The President or an executive officer is refusing to spend, withholding, or delaying appropriated funds. Apply Train v City of New York: did the appropriating statute give the President discretion to spend less than the full amount? If no, refusal is unlawful. Check whether the President has invoked the Impoundment Control Act framework (deferral subject to override, rescission requiring affirmative congressional approval); if yes, the action is presumptively lawful within the ICA's procedures; if no, the action is unlawful regardless of the executive's policy reasons. The ministerial / discretionary distinction from Kendall v Stokes is the doctrinal hinge: courts can compel ministerial disbursements but not discretionary ones.

**Third, the line-item or post-enactment cancellation pattern.** A statute authorizes the President to cancel, suspend, or rescind individual provisions of an enacted appropriation. Apply Clinton v City of New York: any unilateral executive cancellation of an enacted statutory provision is a legislative act and requires bicameralism and presentment. The pattern survives only if the statutory authorization can be characterized as something other than cancellation (for example, a discretionary spending choice within a lump-sum appropriation, or a deferral under the ICA framework). The statute's label is not dispositive; the operative question is whether the President is in substance changing enacted law.

**Cross-doctrine flags.** Watch for Major Questions overlay (Biden v Nebraska): when an agency invokes ambiguous statutory authority to undertake a large new spending program, the Appropriations Clause supplies an independent reason for heightened scrutiny on top of the Major Questions framework's clear-statement requirement. Watch for Spending Clause overlap (South Dakota v Dole, NFIB v Sebelius): the Appropriations Clause governs Congress's authority to authorize federal expenditures; the Spending Clause governs Congress's authority to attach conditions to federal grants to states. Both can be present in the same fact pattern but ask different questions. Watch for Presentment Clause issues (INS v Chadha, Bowsher v Synar): when Congress has structured a budget-control mechanism that tries to give itself or one of its officers control over executive spending decisions, the Presentment / appointments / removal architecture rather than the Appropriations Clause is usually the dispositive frame.

## Critique

**Progressive critique (the formalist consolidation entrenches inequality).** A progressive critique of the modern Appropriations Clause arc focuses on the way CFPB v CFSA's textualist reading of the Clause hardens an institutional architecture that insulates regulatory agencies from democratic accountability. The CFPB was funded outside the annual appropriations cycle precisely because Dodd-Frank's drafters feared that industry-friendly Congresses would use the appropriations process to defund consumer protection. CFPB v CFSA preserves that insulation, which from one progressive standpoint is welcome. From another, the same ruling validates a model in which agencies the political branches favor (the Federal Reserve, the FDIC, in different forms the Postal Service, and increasingly the CFPB) operate beyond the reach of ordinary fiscal politics, while agencies the political branches disfavor remain subject to annual appropriations. The result is a structurally uneven regulatory state in which insulation correlates with policy preference rather than constitutional principle. The same critique extends to Train and Clinton: by formally protecting Congress's spending decisions against executive impoundment and cancellation, the doctrine entrenches whatever budgetary baseline a prior Congress enacted, even when the political coalition that enacted it has since lost the elections it would otherwise face. The democratic-accountability worry cuts both ways depending on whose ox is being gored, and progressives have been on both sides of it across different administrations.

**Originalist / textualist critique (the doctrine is too permissive).** The originalist position, articulated most forcefully in Justice Alito's CFPB dissent, argues that the majority's three-element test reads the Clause too thinly. The Clause refers to "the Treasury," and the original public meaning of that term in 1789 contemplated congressional control through a regular accounting cycle, not standing draws on extra-Treasury revenue streams. The CFPB's draw on Federal Reserve earnings is, on this view, an evasion of the structural commitment to congressional fiscal control that the Clause was designed to embody. Justice Gorsuch's separate dissent emphasized the absence of any meaningful appropriations-process check on the CFPB, suggesting that the Clause should be read to require some non-trivial periodic congressional engagement with agency funding. The originalist critique extends to the impoundment doctrine in a different direction: a Take Care Clause that compels the President to disburse every cent Congress has appropriated, regardless of the executive's policy judgment about whether the spending is wise, reads "executive power" as a purely ministerial function and elides the President's institutional stake in fiscal responsibility. On this view, Train v City of New York went too far, and a more textured impoundment doctrine should leave the President some discretion to delay or reprogram funds within the bounds Congress has set.

**Doctrinal-architecture critique (the rules underdetermine modern controversies).** Both progressives and conservatives have noted that the existing Appropriations Clause architecture leaves the most consequential modern controversies undertheorized. The 2025 funding-freeze episodes raised a question Train does not directly answer: when the President orders agency-wide pauses on grant disbursement pending review, is that a deferral (lawful within the ICA framework if properly notified) or an impoundment (unlawful)? The CFPB ruling answers the funding-architecture question but says little about whether agency-internal reallocation across program offices counts as impoundment when Congress has specified line-item amounts. Clinton's Presentment Clause holding leaves open whether some narrow class of statutory cancellation authorities (recissions under the ICA, expedited rescissions under newer proposed frameworks) survive its bar; lower courts have read Clinton both narrowly and broadly without Supreme Court correction. The doctrine, in short, has a clear core (CFPB on definitions, Train on direct impoundment, Clinton on line-item veto) and a fuzzy periphery (ICA-mediated discretion, lump-sum reprogramming, agency funding pauses) that the modern controversies are testing.

**The Major Questions amplifier.** Biden v Nebraska's invocation of the Appropriations Clause as a heightened-scrutiny rationale within Major Questions analysis generated a smaller but growing critique: that combining the two doctrines stacks separation-of-powers concerns in a way that systematically disadvantages executive-branch initiatives. Critics argue that the Appropriations Clause is a structural rule about who appropriates rather than a rule about how strictly courts should construe ambiguous delegations once Congress has appropriated. Defenders reply that the structural commitment is precisely the reason for tighter delegation review when the Executive is initiating large-scale spending under ambiguous authority. The dispute is mostly about doctrinal labeling, but it has practical bite because the Appropriations Clause amplifier may travel beyond Major Questions cases into broader nondelegation-style review of executive spending discretion.

## Connections

- [[Topics/Separation of Powers|Separation of Powers]]: The Appropriations Clause is the structural cornerstone of congressional control over the federal fisc and operates alongside the Vesting Clauses, the Take Care Clause, and the Presentment Clause to constrain executive revision of enacted spending decisions. Train and Clinton both rest on cross-clause arguments that the Appropriations Clause makes most sense when read in the context of the broader separation-of-powers architecture.
- [[Topics/Spending Clause|Spending Clause]]: The Spending Clause (Article I Section 8 Clause 1) governs Congress's substantive power to spend for the general welfare and to attach conditions to federal grants to states. The Appropriations Clause governs the procedural prerequisite for any expenditure: the requirement of an enacted appropriation. Both clauses can be implicated in the same fact pattern, but they ask different questions.
- [[Topics/Executive Power|Executive Power]]: The impoundment line of cases (Kendall, Train, Clinton in part) defines the limits of executive power over fiscal matters. The President's role is to execute appropriations, not to revise them.
- [[Topics/Major Questions Doctrine|Major Questions Doctrine]]: Biden v Nebraska treats the Appropriations Clause as a heightened-scrutiny rationale within Major Questions analysis, applying tighter delegation review when an agency invokes ambiguous statutory authority to initiate large-scale spending.
- [[Topics/Enumerated Powers|Enumerated Powers]]: The Appropriations Clause is one of the structural provisions of Article I Section 9 that limits the manner in which Congress's enumerated powers may be exercised, alongside the Bill of Attainder, Ex Post Facto, and Title of Nobility prohibitions.
- [[Cases/Consumer Financial Protection Bureau v Community Financial Services Association of America (2024)|CFPB v CFSA (2024)]]: The operative modern definition of "appropriation made by Law."
- [[Cases/Biden v Nebraska (2023)|Biden v Nebraska (2023)]]: The Major Questions / Appropriations Clause overlay in agency-initiated spending.
- [[Lectures/Impoundment and Presidential Spending Power|Impoundment and Presidential Spending Power]]: The lecture treatment of Kendall, Train, and Clinton; the Impoundment Control Act framework; and the 2025 funding-freeze controversies.
- [[Lectures/Congressional Vetoes and Line Item Vetoes - Chadha Bowsher Clinton|Congressional Vetoes and Line Item Vetoes]]: The Presentment Clause arc through Chadha, Bowsher, and Clinton in which the Line Item Veto Act decision is the Appropriations Clause endpoint.
