# Selective Incorporation

## Overview

Selective Incorporation is the method the Supreme Court has used since the late 1890s to apply Bill of Rights guarantees against the States one right at a time. It is a methodological commitment, not a substantive test: the Court looks at each Bill of Rights provision individually and asks whether that specific guarantee is fundamental enough to bind the States through the Fourteenth Amendment. The competitor theory, total incorporation, would have applied all Bill of Rights guarantees against the States in one move. Justice Black pressed total incorporation most forcefully in his *Adamson v California* (1947) dissent and his *Duncan v Louisiana* (1968) concurrence, but the Court has never adopted it. Selective Incorporation is therefore both the method used and the method that won.

This page focuses on the test mechanics: how the Court decides, one right at a time, whether a provision is fundamental. The broader doctrinal story (*Barron*, *Slaughter-House*, *Cruikshank*, the clause-shifting move from Privileges or Immunities to Due Process) is covered in the [[Topics/Incorporation Doctrine|Incorporation Doctrine]] Topic page. Here the unit of analysis is the test itself and its three successive formulations.

---

## Governing Rule

> **Rule:** A Bill of Rights provision is selectively incorporated against the States through the Fourteenth Amendment Due Process Clause when the right is fundamental. The Court has articulated fundamentality three different ways across the incorporation lineage. *Palko v Connecticut* (1937) asked whether the right is "implicit in the concept of ordered liberty," benchmarked against what a "fair and enlightened system of justice" would require. *Duncan v Louisiana* (1968) narrowed the inquiry to whether the right is "fundamental to the American scheme of justice," shifting the evidence base from abstract civilization to concrete Anglo-American tradition. *McDonald v City of Chicago* (2010) and *Timbs v Indiana* (2019) package the modern test as two components, asking whether the right is "fundamental to our scheme of ordered liberty" or "deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition." The three formulations are not interchangeable. Once incorporated under any of them, *Duncan*'s no-watered-down principle forecloses weaker state-level versions: the incorporated right applies to the States with the same scope it has against the federal government.

## Doctrinal Development

The doctrine moves in four steps: the foundational framework, the great debate, the modern refinement, and the unanimous capstone.

### Foundational Framework: Palko v Connecticut (1937)

Justice Cardozo supplied the first general test in *Palko*, framing the inquiry as which elements of the Bill of Rights are "absorbed" by the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause. His gradient asked whether a right is "implicit in the concept of ordered liberty" or a "principle of justice so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental." Under this standard, freedoms of speech, press, and religion passed (Cardozo called speech "the matrix, the indispensable condition, of nearly every other form of freedom"), but the procedural criminal-process rights of jury trial, grand jury indictment, and self-incrimination failed the 1937 analysis. Applying the framework to Connecticut's statute permitting State appeal of criminal judgments, Cardozo concluded that the Fifth Amendment's Double Jeopardy Clause was not fundamental in the relevant sense: Palka was "not being worn out by a multitude of prosecutions" but "given one trial free from error." The specific holding was overruled by *Benton v Maryland* (1969), but Cardozo's framework governed incorporation analysis for the next three decades. See [[Cases/Palko v Connecticut (1937)|full brief]].

### The Great Debate: Adamson v California (1947)

The methodological competition came to a head in *Adamson*. Justice Frankfurter for the majority defended selective incorporation, arguing that the overlap between incorporated rights and the Bill of Rights was largely coincidental and that the Fourteenth Amendment protected "specific methods for securing basic rights" without mechanically applying the first eight Amendments. Justice Black's dissent made the contrary case. Drawing on an extensive historical reconstruction of the Reconstruction-era drafting record, Black argued that the Fourteenth Amendment was designed to apply the whole Bill of Rights against the States through the Privileges or Immunities Clause, and that selective incorporation gave the Court "boundless power under natural law" to expand and contract constitutional standards at its own pace. Frankfurter's selective view won. Adamson is not separately briefed in this vault but is treated at length in [[Lectures/The Incorporation Doctrine|the Incorporation Doctrine lecture]].

### Warren Court Refinement: Duncan v Louisiana (1968)

*Duncan* reformulated *Palko*'s test. Justice White's majority opinion asked whether the right is "fundamental to the American scheme of justice," a shift from *Palko*'s abstract ordered-liberty framing toward a concrete examination of Anglo-American legal tradition. The evidence base narrowed. *Palko* had asked what any "fair and enlightened system of justice" would require; *Duncan* asked what our actual American and Anglo-American system does require, drawing on Magna Carta, the Declaration of Independence (which listed deprivation of jury trial among the colonists' grievances), and contemporary state practice (every State preserved criminal jury trial). Applied to the Sixth Amendment jury-trial right, the test produced incorporation for "serious" offenses, with a two-year maximum qualifying Duncan's case on the serious side of the line. *Duncan* also announced the no-watered-down principle: once a right is incorporated, it applies against the States with full federal strength. Justice Black's concurrence previewed the Thomas-McDonald position: the correct textual vehicle is Privileges or Immunities, and total incorporation remains his preferred approach. Justice Harlan's dissent is the fullest modern statement of the incorporation-skeptic position, defending *Palko*'s flexible fundamental-fairness inquiry against what he saw as *Duncan*'s mechanical-incorporation drift. See [[Cases/Duncan v Louisiana (1968)|full brief]].

### Modern Centerpiece: McDonald v City of Chicago (2010)

*McDonald* applied the modern test to the Second Amendment, packaging *Palko*'s ordered-liberty language and *Duncan*'s historical grounding as two conjoined components operating "together rather than as sequential hurdles": whether the right is fundamental to our scheme of ordered liberty and whether it is deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition. Justice Alito's plurality marshaled an extensive historical record (the Founders' Anglo-American inheritance, Reconstruction-era protection of freedmen's arms against Southern Black Codes, unbroken state-constitutional protection) and concluded both components were satisfied. The plurality declined to revisit *Slaughter-House*, preferring "the well-trodden path of selective incorporation under Due Process" to the academic debate over Privileges or Immunities. Justice Thomas concurred in the judgment but reached the same result through the Privileges or Immunities Clause, making the methodological alternative visible for the first time at the vote-casting level. *McDonald* is the modern case in which both routes are laid side by side, with the Due Process route commanding four votes and P or I supplying the fifth. See [[Cases/McDonald v City of Chicago (2010)|full brief]].

### Unanimous Capstone: Timbs v Indiana (2019)

*Timbs* applied the *McDonald* formulation mechanically to the Eighth Amendment's Excessive Fines Clause and reached the unanimous result. Justice Ginsburg's opinion restated the modern standard ("fundamental to our scheme of ordered liberty, or deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition") and marshaled an extensive history running from Chapter 39 of Magna Carta through the 1689 English Bill of Rights, state constitutions at the Founding, Reconstruction-era debates, and modern practice. The Court also applied *McDonald*'s no-daylight principle to hold that the Clause covers civil in rem forfeitures to the extent they are at least partially punitive. Justice Gorsuch's brief concurrence and Justice Thomas's longer concurrence in judgment both urged that Privileges or Immunities is the more appropriate vehicle, but the clause-selection question did not affect the outcome. *Timbs* is the clearest modern illustration that the *McDonald* formulation now functions as settled law, at least where the historical case for the right is strong. See [[Cases/Timbs v Indiana (2019)|full brief]].

### Rights Currently In (via Selective Incorporation)

Drawing from [[Lectures/The Incorporation Doctrine|the lecture's]] catalog and the case briefs in this vault: First Amendment (speech, press, religion, assembly, and petition, running from *Gitlow v New York* (1925) through *Cantwell v Connecticut* (1940) and later cases); Second Amendment ([[Cases/McDonald v City of Chicago (2010)|McDonald]]); Fourth Amendment (search, seizure, and the exclusionary rule); Fifth Amendment self-incrimination and just compensation (*Chicago Burlington and Quincy R.R. v Chicago* (1897) for just compensation; *Malloy v Hogan* (1964) for self-incrimination); Fifth Amendment double jeopardy (*Benton v Maryland* (1969) overruling *Palko* on this point); Sixth Amendment jury trial ([[Cases/Duncan v Louisiana (1968)|Duncan]]), confrontation, compulsory process, and counsel; Eighth Amendment cruel and unusual punishments; Eighth Amendment excessive fines ([[Cases/Timbs v Indiana (2019)|Timbs]]).

### Rights Currently Out

Three provisions remain unincorporated on current doctrine: the Fifth Amendment grand jury indictment requirement (*Hurtado v California* (1884), expressly preserved in [[Cases/Palko v Connecticut (1937)|Palko]]), the Seventh Amendment civil jury right in state court, and the Eighth Amendment Excessive Bail Clause (no Supreme Court case has squarely incorporated it, though a strong *Timbs*-analogue argument is available because the Excessive Bail and Excessive Fines Clauses share the same textual home and historical pedigree).

## Comparison: The Three Test Formulations

| Axis | Palko (1937) | Duncan (1968) | McDonald / Timbs (2010/2019) |
|------|-------------|---------------|------------------------------|
| **Test statement** | "Implicit in the concept of ordered liberty"; "principle of justice so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental" | "Fundamental to the American scheme of justice" | "Fundamental to our scheme of ordered liberty" OR "deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition" |
| **Evidence base** | Abstract -- what any "fair and enlightened system of justice" requires | Concrete -- Anglo-American tradition specifically (Magna Carta, colonial charters, Founding practice) | Extended historical record: English antecedents, Founding-era practice, Reconstruction-era debates, contemporary state practice |
| **Structure** | Single-component; philosophical | Single-component; historical-institutional | Two-component, either/or; conjoined rather than sequential |
| **Rights denied** | Double jeopardy (overruled 1969), self-incrimination (overruled 1964) | None denied under the new standard; applied to expand jury right | None denied; applied to expand Second Amendment (McDonald) and Excessive Fines (Timbs) |
| **P or I alternative?** | No; Cardozo routes through Due Process without discussion | No; Harlan dissent defends flexible fairness against mechanical incorporation | Yes -- Thomas (McDonald, Timbs) and Gorsuch (Timbs) press P or I concurrences at the vote level |
| **No-watered-down principle** | Not articulated | Announced -- incorporated right applies with full federal strength | Applied -- incorporated right carries full *Heller*/*Timbs* scope against States |
| **Key vulnerability** | Philosophical standard grants Court wide discretion; Black: "boundless power under natural law" | "American scheme" still requires historical judgment calls; Harlan dissent: undermines state procedural variation | The two components are alternative, not cumulative; a right can survive on either prong alone, which may expand incorporation reach |

Read horizontally: the test has moved from abstract-philosophical (Palko) to historical-institutional (Duncan) to a dual-pronged historical standard with P or I pressure building on the margins (McDonald/Timbs). The practical effect of each shift has been to make the test more favorable to incorporation, not less. No provision incorporated under Palko has been de-incorporated under Duncan or McDonald.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side

1. **Hypo:** A State enacts a statute empowering police to search any automobile without a warrant based on "reasonable suspicion of contraband." A driver challenges an evidentiary search that produced drug evidence. **Why:** The Fourth Amendment's warrant and probable cause requirements were incorporated against the States in *Mapp v Ohio* (1961), and under *Duncan*'s no-watered-down principle the state search is analyzed on the same terms as a federal search. The State cannot create a weaker probable-cause standard. Driver wins on the incorporation step; the merits of the search still require a standard Fourth Amendment analysis.

2. **Hypo:** A State prohibits individuals from possessing operable handguns in the home for self-defense. **Why:** Direct application of [[Cases/McDonald v City of Chicago (2010)|McDonald]]. The Second Amendment right recognized in [[Cases/District of Columbia v Heller (2008)|Heller]] was incorporated under the *McDonald* formulation (fundamental to our scheme of ordered liberty AND deeply rooted), and the Chicago ordinance is materially identical to the D.C. law struck down in *Heller*. Under *Duncan*'s no-watered-down principle the State may not enforce a regulation that would fail federal analysis. The ban fails.

### Opposite-Side

3. **Hypo:** A State charges a defendant with an aggravated felony by prosecutor's information rather than grand jury indictment. The defendant invokes the Fifth Amendment Grand Jury Clause. **Why:** The grand jury indictment guarantee has never been incorporated against the States. *Hurtado v California* (1884) held that due process does not require grand jury indictment, [[Cases/Palko v Connecticut (1937)|Palko]] expressly preserved that result by listing grand jury indictment among the Anglo-American procedural features that are not "implicit in the concept of ordered liberty," and nothing since has disturbed the holding. Under the *Duncan* formulation the practice is not deeply rooted as a uniform American tradition because most States have never used grand juries for felony charges. The defendant loses.

4. **Hypo:** A State civil small-claims statute eliminates jury trials for disputes below $500. The litigant invokes the Seventh Amendment civil-jury guarantee. **Why:** The Seventh Amendment has never been incorporated against the States. Civil jury practice varies too widely across state systems to find a deeply rooted national tradition under *Duncan* or *McDonald*. The same reasoning [[Cases/Palko v Connecticut (1937)|Palko]] applied to grand jury indictment (an Anglo-American feature that is valuable but not indispensable to a fair system of justice) disposes of the claim. The litigant loses.

### Fence-Sitter

5. **Hypo:** A State bail statute sets bail at ten times the maximum authorized fine for the underlying offense, without any individualized hearing. A detainee challenges the scheme under the Eighth Amendment Excessive Bail Clause, arguing [[Cases/Timbs v Indiana (2019)|Timbs]] controls by analogy. **Why:** This is the hard case because no Supreme Court decision has squarely incorporated the Excessive Bail Clause. The *Timbs* template looks directly applicable: the Excessive Bail Clause shares the same textual home in the Eighth Amendment as the Excessive Fines Clause, the historical pedigree runs through Magna Carta and the 1689 English Bill of Rights in parallel to the Excessive Fines lineage, and the *McDonald* formulation's deeply-rooted component would plausibly be satisfied. A court following [[Cases/Timbs v Indiana (2019)|Timbs]] closely would likely incorporate the Clause on a *Timbs*-by-analogy theory and apply *Duncan*'s no-watered-down principle. But a more cautious court would observe that incorporation has always been a right-by-right inquiry, and the absence of a direct holding makes the step formally unresolved. The doctrinal gap is exactly the kind of one-at-a-time issue the Selective Incorporation method produces and leaves pending.

## How to Spot on an Exam

Three exam triggers. First, a state or municipal government is the regulator and the challenger invokes a specific Bill of Rights guarantee, not a generic Fourteenth Amendment liberty theory. That is the incorporation pattern. Confirm whether the guarantee has been incorporated. If yes, apply the federal doctrine at full strength under *Duncan*'s no-watered-down principle. If no, frame the claim either as a candidate for incorporation under the *McDonald* formulation (fundamental to our scheme of ordered liberty AND deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition) or as a freestanding Due Process fundamental-fairness claim, which is a narrower channel.

Second, a prompt invites methodological engagement by naming *Palko* or *Duncan* or by emphasizing historical materials. Deploy the correct formulation for the Court term in question. *Palko*'s "implicit in the concept of ordered liberty" test controlled from 1937 to 1968. *Duncan*'s "fundamental to the American scheme of justice" test controlled from 1968 through at least the *McDonald* reformulation. *McDonald* and *Timbs* package both strands as two conjoined components, and that is the formulation the Court currently uses. Do not collapse the three into a single test. Professors watch for that mistake.

Third, the unincorporated provisions (Fifth Amendment grand jury, Seventh Amendment civil jury, Eighth Amendment excessive bail) are a reliable exam pattern. The examinee who names the specific unincorporated right, cites *Hurtado* or *Palko* for grand jury indictment, and flags that the Excessive Bail Clause is the closest live candidate for *Timbs*-by-analogy incorporation is separating points from the examinees who reflexively assume every Bill of Rights provision binds the States.

Analytical framework to deploy:

1. Identify the specific Bill of Rights guarantee.
2. State incorporation status from the vault's catalog above.
3. If incorporated: apply the federal doctrine at full strength (*Duncan* no-watered-down).
4. If unincorporated: argue for incorporation under the *McDonald* formulation with evidence drawn from Founding-era materials, state constitutional practice at the Fourteenth Amendment's ratification, and modern practice.
5. Flag the Privileges or Immunities alternative when the prompt invites it; see [[Topics/Privileges or Immunities|the P or I Topic page]] for the revival argument's architecture.

## Critique

**The subjectivity critique.** Justice Black's *Adamson* dissent and *Duncan* concurrence press the strongest progressive case against Selective Incorporation: the method gives the Court "boundless power under natural law" to control the pace of rights application one case at a time, with no textual criterion beyond judicial intuition about what is "fundamental." The three successive test formulations illustrate the worry. *Palko* asked what any "fair and enlightened" legal system would require. *Duncan* narrowed to what the American system actually requires. *McDonald* conjoined both components without explaining why the change matters. Each reformulation is plausible taken alone, but the absence of a principled criterion for choosing among them is exactly Black's point. Total incorporation grounded in the Fourteenth Amendment's original meaning would bypass the whole inquiry.

**The textual critique.** Justice Thomas's [[Cases/McDonald v City of Chicago (2010)|McDonald]] concurrence and [[Cases/Timbs v Indiana (2019)|Timbs]] concurrence in judgment argue that the Selective Incorporation method compounds *Slaughter-House*'s error by routing substantive rights through the Due Process Clause. The Court should have used the Privileges or Immunities Clause, which on an originalist reading plainly encompassed the enumerated Bill of Rights guarantees. The compounding point is that Due Process is a procedural clause; using it to deliver substantive rights against the States is textually incoherent. The Selective Incorporation method enshrines that incoherence as operative doctrine.

**The federalism critique.** Justice Harlan's [[Cases/Duncan v Louisiana (1968)|Duncan]] dissent makes the classical federalism objection: Selective Incorporation flattens state procedural variation in a way that forecloses the state experimentation the Tenth Amendment tradition was designed to preserve. Justice Breyer's [[Cases/McDonald v City of Chicago (2010)|McDonald]] dissent presses a modern version, worrying that incorporation of the Second Amendment forecloses state policy experimentation on gun regulation. The honest response is that the no-watered-down principle is doing work in the other direction too: once a right is incorporated, it carries full federal strength, and States cannot trim it down. The value of federalist experimentation must be weighed against the value of uniform national minima for individual rights.

**The pragmatic defense.** The Alito plurality in *McDonald* and the unanimous *Timbs* opinion implicitly defend Selective Incorporation from path dependence: the alternative (overruling *Slaughter-House* and rerouting everything through Privileges or Immunities) would require reopening 150 years of settled precedent. Taking "the road more traveled," as the plurality put it, avoids the need to define the full scope of a Clause that scholars cannot agree about. That is not a principled defense of the method; it is a defense from institutional conservatism, and its persuasiveness depends on whether the reader values stability over textual fidelity.
