# Thirteenth Amendment

## Overview

The Thirteenth Amendment is doctrinally unusual in two ways. First, Section 1 reaches private conduct directly: unlike the Fourteenth Amendment, it carries no state-action requirement, so the Amendment itself prohibits slavery and involuntary servitude regardless of who imposes them. Second, Section 2 grants Congress power to enforce the Amendment by appropriate legislation, and Jones v Alfred H Mayer Co reads that power broadly enough to reach private actors who perpetuate the "badges and incidents of slavery." Together, those two features make the Thirteenth Amendment the constitutional home of the Reconstruction-era civil-rights statutes (the Civil Rights Act of 1866, surviving today as 42 U.S.C. sections 1981 and 1982; and section 1985(3), the Ku Klux Klan Act conspiracy statute).

The doctrinal arc moves from broad legislative authority (Jones, 1968) through extension to private conspiracies (Griffin, 1971), to the principal limit (City of Memphis v Greene, 1981), where the Court held that not every disparate burden on Black citizens is a badge or incident of slavery within the Amendment's reach.

---

## Governing Rule

> **Rule:** Section 1 prohibits slavery and involuntary servitude (subject to a punishment-after-conviction exception) and operates against private as well as governmental conduct. Section 2 empowers Congress, by rational determination, to identify the badges and incidents of slavery and to legislate against private conduct that perpetuates them. Section 2 legislation does not require state action. The badges-and-incidents power is bounded by the requirement that the targeted conduct meaningfully impair an interest the Amendment was designed to secure (property, contract, physical liberty, freedom of movement); routine disparate impact on Black citizens, without more, is not enough.

---

## Doctrinal Development

The three vault briefs cover the foundational expansion, the conspiracy extension, and the principal limit. Earlier doctrinal context (the Civil Rights Cases of 1883, which had read the Thirteenth Amendment narrowly on the private-conduct question) is referenced through Harlan's dissent and is effectively eclipsed by the Jones reading.

### Jones v Alfred H Mayer Co (1968)

Foundational. Justice Stewart's majority held that 42 U.S.C. section 1982 bars all racial discrimination, private as well as public, in the sale or rental of property, and that Congress had power under Section 2 of the Thirteenth Amendment to reach the private actor (a developer who refused to sell to a Black buyer) as a badge and incident of slavery. The opinion makes two moves. On the statutory question, Stewart reads the text of section 1982 to draw no state-action limit. On the constitutional question, he holds that Section 2 reaches private conduct because the Thirteenth Amendment, unlike the Fourteenth, prohibits an institution that private actors maintained as well as states. Congress may "rationally determine" what counts as a badge or incident, and the right to buy and hold property was among the fundamental civil freedoms slavery denied. Stewart effectively adopts the framework of Justice Harlan's dissent in the Civil Rights Cases. See [[Cases/Jones v Alfred H Mayer Co (1968)|full brief]].

### Griffin v Breckenridge (1971)

Refinement. A unanimous Court (Stewart again writing) held that 42 U.S.C. section 1985(3) creates a civil cause of action against purely private, racially motivated conspiracies, requiring no state action. The Court overruled Collins v Hardyman (1951), whose state-action gloss on the statute had been atextual: section 1985(3) speaks of "two or more persons" and includes a "going in disguise" clause aimed at private marauders like the Klan. To avoid converting section 1985(3) into a general federal tort law, Stewart imposed a class-based animus element rather than a state-action element: the conspiracy must be motivated by some racial or otherwise invidiously discriminatory class-based hostility. Constitutional authority rests on two grounds: Section 2 of the Thirteenth Amendment (for racial conspiracies, applying Jones) and Congress's power to protect the right of interstate travel (assertable against private as well as governmental interference). See [[Cases/Griffin v Breckenridge (1971)|full brief]].

### City of Memphis v Greene (1981)

Limit. Justice Stevens, writing for a 6-3 Court, held that Memphis's closing of a street through a white neighborhood, which lengthened Black motorists' commute by two to five minutes, did not violate section 1982 or the Thirteenth Amendment. On the statute, the closure did not impair a property interest within section 1982: a marginal increase in travel time is not a deprivation of the right to inherit, purchase, lease, sell, hold, or convey property. On the Amendment, the Court declined to treat a disproportionate inconvenience as a badge or incident of slavery, calling the burden a "routine burden of citizenship" rather than a vestige of the Amendment's "odious practice." Marshall, joined by Brennan and Blackmun, dissented, treating the closure as the kind of symbolic walling-off that perpetuates slavery's caste structure. The case marks the principal doctrinal limit on the Jones-Griffin enforcement-power reading. See [[Cases/City of Memphis v Greene (1981)|full brief]].

## Comparison: Thirteenth Amendment Section 2 versus Fourteenth Amendment Section 5

| Axis | 13A Section 2 (Jones) | 14A Section 5 |
|------|-----------------------|---------------|
| State action required? | No. Reaches private conduct directly. | Yes. Must remedy state violations. |
| Standard of review | "Rational determination" of badges and incidents (Jones). | Congruence and proportionality (City of Boerne). |
| Substantive trigger | Private conduct that perpetuates slavery's vestiges. | State conduct that violates Section 1. |
| Scope on private actors | Broad, subject to the property-rights or liberty-impairment threshold from City of Memphis v Greene. | Cannot reach private actors directly (Civil Rights Cases). |

The contrast explains why modern civil-rights litigation against private actors that cannot route through the Commerce Clause typically routes through Section 1981 / 1982 / 1985(3), all anchored on Section 2.

## Comparison: The Three Reconstruction-Era Statutes Anchored on Section 2

| Statute | Origin | Conduct reached | State-action requirement | Anchoring case |
|---------|--------|-----------------|--------------------------|----------------|
| 42 U.S.C. § 1981 | Civil Rights Act of 1866; re-enacted 1870 | Private and public interference with the right to make and enforce contracts | None (Runyon v McCrary, 1976) | [[Cases/Jones v Alfred H Mayer Co (1968)\|Jones]] (companion-statute reading) |
| 42 U.S.C. § 1982 | Civil Rights Act of 1866 | Private and public racial discrimination in the sale or rental of real or personal property | None | [[Cases/Jones v Alfred H Mayer Co (1968)\|Jones v Mayer (1968)]] |
| 42 U.S.C. § 1985(3) | Ku Klux Klan Act of 1871 | Private conspiracies motivated by racial or class-based invidious animus to deprive of equal protection or privileges | None; replaced by class-based-animus element | [[Cases/Griffin v Breckenridge (1971)\|Griffin v Breckenridge (1971)]] |

The three statutes share a single constitutional anchor (Section 2) but cover distinct domains: contracts, property, and conspiracy. Litigators choose among them based on the form of the private interference, not on whether a state actor is involved. The class-based-animus requirement on § 1985(3) is what keeps it from collapsing into a federal tort statute; § 1981 and § 1982 lean on the racial-discrimination element instead.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side

1. **Hypo:** A private apartment complex refuses to rent to Black applicants. A Black applicant sues under 42 U.S.C. section 1982 and the Thirteenth Amendment. **Why:** Direct application of Jones. Private rental of property falls inside section 1982; refusal based on race is racial discrimination in the lease of property; Section 2 supplies congressional authority. Same outcome as Jones.
2. **Hypo:** A loose group of private individuals, motivated by racial animus, conspires to drive a Black family out of a previously all-white town by repeated harassment that injures person and property. A federal civil suit is brought under 42 U.S.C. section 1985(3). **Why:** Direct application of Griffin. Private conspiracy, class-based racial animus, acts in furtherance, injury to person and property. Section 2 plus the right to interstate travel both support the cause of action.

### Opposite-Side

3. **Hypo:** A homeowners association in a racially homogeneous neighborhood petitions the city to install speed bumps on its main road. The bumps marginally inconvenience commuters of all races, with somewhat greater impact on commuters from a poorer, predominantly Black neighborhood that abuts the road. **Why:** City of Memphis v Greene controls. The traffic measure does not impair a section 1982 property interest, and the disparate inconvenience is the kind of routine burden of citizenship the Court declined to treat as a badge of slavery.
4. **Hypo:** A private employer refuses to hire white applicants for a customer-facing role on the theory that the position is reserved for Black employees. A white applicant sues under 42 U.S.C. section 1981 and the Thirteenth Amendment. **Why:** The Thirteenth Amendment was designed to dismantle the slave system, and the badges-and-incidents framework is asymmetric in its historical justification. While modern section 1981 doctrine recognizes white plaintiffs (McDonald v Santa Fe Trail), the Thirteenth Amendment hook does not strengthen the claim, and the doctrine's slavery-anchored logic does not apply on these facts.

### Fence-Sitter

5. **Hypo:** A gig-economy platform's algorithm produces materially fewer, lower-paying matches for drivers it has identified as Black, even when other variables are held constant. A class of Black drivers sues under 42 U.S.C. section 1981 (right to make and enforce contracts), section 1982 (property and contract rights), and the Thirteenth Amendment, framing the algorithmic disparity as a digital-era badge of slavery. **Why:** This pulls toward Jones in that the conduct is purely private and directly impairs the right to contract. It pulls toward City of Memphis v Greene in that the impairment is statistical and indirect rather than a categorical refusal to deal. Whether the badges-and-incidents power reaches algorithmic disparate impact (without proof of intentional discrimination) is genuinely open and depends in part on whether the Court treats the platform's terms as a contract right within section 1981's "make and enforce contracts" language.

## How to Spot on an Exam

The trigger facts are private racial conduct (no state actor in sight), or a Reconstruction-era civil-rights statute (section 1981, 1982, or 1985(3)). When the conduct is private and racially motivated, the Thirteenth Amendment is the most plausible enforcement-power hook because the Fourteenth Amendment requires state action and the Commerce Clause requires a commerce nexus that private racial conduct often lacks.

A useful three-step approach:

1. Identify the right impaired (property, contract, physical liberty, freedom of movement). If the impairment is on a section 1981 / 1982 right, Jones is the entry point.
2. If the conduct is a private conspiracy with racial animus, route through section 1985(3) under Griffin.
3. Run the City of Memphis v Greene limit. Is there a real impairment of a property or liberty interest, or only a routine disparate impact? Note that 13A is rarely the lead theory in modern litigation; the Commerce Clause has crowded most of this space (Heart of Atlanta, Katzenbach, Ollie's Barbecue) for facially economic conduct, and the Fourteenth Amendment for state conduct. The Thirteenth Amendment is the right answer when the defendant is private and the conduct is not commercial.

## Critique

The principal scholarly debate centers on the scope of "badges and incidents." Defenders read Jones as faithful to the Reconstruction Congress's understanding (which gave us the 1866 Civil Rights Act before the Fourteenth Amendment was even ratified). Critics, following Harlan's dissent, argue that Jones reads Section 2 untethered from the institution of slavery as historically practiced, leaving the doctrine without a meaningful limit. City of Memphis v Greene supplied a partial answer by anchoring section 1982 to property-rights impairment, but the Amendment-level question (what counts as a vestige of slavery) remains underdeveloped.

A second critique concerns Jones's relationship to the Civil Rights Cases (1883). The Court did not formally overrule the Thirteenth Amendment portion of that decision, but Jones's reasoning leaves little doctrinal space for the older case to operate. Whether the Civil Rights Cases retain any Thirteenth Amendment force is, on the strongest reading, only a question of stare decisis politeness.

A third critique concerns the asymmetry between the Thirteenth and Fourteenth Amendment enforcement powers after City of Boerne v Flores tightened Section 5. Section 2 remains under the Jones rationality standard, while Section 5 is now under congruence-and-proportionality. Some commentators have urged the Court to import a Boerne-style limit into Section 2; the Court has not done so.
