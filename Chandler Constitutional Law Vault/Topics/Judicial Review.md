# Judicial Review

## Overview

Judicial review is the power of federal courts to declare acts of Congress, the executive, and the states unconstitutional and therefore void. It is the structural mechanism by which the Constitution operates as paramount law rather than as a hortatory recommendation. Although the text of Article III does not expressly grant this power, it has been treated as a settled feature of the American constitutional order since *Marbury v Madison* (1803), in which Chief Justice Marshall reasoned that a written constitution committed to be paramount law must be enforced by the courts whose duty it is "to say what the law is."

Judicial review carries two related but distinct ideas. The narrow idea is judicial *review*: courts will not enforce a statute or executive act they find inconsistent with the Constitution in a case properly before them. The broader idea is judicial *supremacy*: the Court's interpretation of the Constitution binds the coordinate branches as a final and authoritative settlement of the constitutional question, not merely as a judgment between the parties. *Marbury* announces the narrow idea openly and the broader idea by implication. The contest between these two readings runs throughout American constitutional history, from Jefferson's protests through Lincoln's response to *Dred Scott* through modern departmentalist critiques.

Marbury also planted, in passing, the seeds of two doctrines that limit the reach of judicial review from within: the political question doctrine (some questions are committed to the political branches and not for courts to second-guess) and what would later become executive privilege (some communications between the President and his department heads are not subject to judicial inquiry). Both reappear in [[Cases/The Prize Cases (1863)|The Prize Cases]] and [[Cases/Youngstown Sheet and Tube Co v Sawyer (1952)|Youngstown]] as natural counterpoints to the assertive judicial role *Marbury* announced.

---

## Governing Rule

> **Core Rule (Marbury v Madison, 1803):** "It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is." When an act of Congress conflicts with the Constitution, courts must apply the Constitution and treat the statute as void. "An act of the legislature, repugnant to the constitution, is void." This power inheres in the judicial function under any written constitution: if the Constitution is paramount law, then a court that closed its eyes to the Constitution and saw only the statute would give the legislature "a practical and real omnipotence" while professing to restrict its powers.

> **Original Jurisdiction Sub-Rule (Marbury v Madison, 1803):** Article III defines the Supreme Court's original jurisdiction exhaustively. Congress cannot expand the categories of cases the Court may hear in the first instance beyond those enumerated in Article III, Section 2 (Ambassadors, public Ministers and Consuls, and cases in which a State is a party). Section 13 of the Judiciary Act of 1789, insofar as it purported to grant original mandamus jurisdiction over federal officers, was unconstitutional and void.

> **Right-Remedy Alignment (Marbury v Madison, 1803):** "The very essence of civil liberty certainly consists in the right of every individual to claim the protection of the laws, whenever he receives an injury." Where a specific legal duty is assigned by statute and individual rights depend upon its performance, the injured party has a right to a judicial remedy. Government officers acting in violation of legal duty are not protected from suit by their office.

> **Internal Limit -- Political Questions (Marbury):** Acts that are "in their nature political" and "respect the nation, not individual rights" are not judicially examinable. When officers exercise discretion committed by law to their judgment, the courts provide no review. This passing distinction in *Marbury* is the doctrinal seed of the political question doctrine.

## Doctrinal Development

### Marbury v Madison (1803): The Foundational Holding

Marshall reached the rule of judicial review along an indirect path designed to confront Jefferson's administration without permitting Jefferson the political opportunity to defy a court order. Confronted with William Marbury's mandamus petition for delivery of a Justice of the Peace commission, Marshall organized the opinion around three sequential questions.

First, he held that Marbury possessed a vested legal right to the commission. The appointment was complete when President Adams signed and the Secretary of State sealed; delivery was a ministerial act that could not affect the vesting of the right. Second, he held that the law afforded a remedy: where there is a legal right, there is a legal remedy. Third, he held that mandamus from the Supreme Court was *not* the correct remedy, because the statute under which Marbury sought the writ had unconstitutionally expanded the Supreme Court's original jurisdiction.

This third question is where judicial review enters the doctrine. Marshall reasoned from the structure of a written constitution. The Constitution is "either a superior, paramount law, unchangeable by ordinary means, or it is on a level with ordinary legislative acts, and like other acts, is alterable when the legislature shall please to alter it." There is no middle ground. If the Constitution is paramount, an inconsistent statute must be void. Courts that apply the law in a particular case must decide which of two conflicting rules controls; when those rules are a statute and the Constitution, the Constitution must control. The judicial oath to support the Constitution and the Supremacy Clause's reference to laws "made in pursuance" of the Constitution reinforce this conclusion. See [[Cases/Marbury v Madison (1803)|full brief]].

The case also illustrates Marshall's tactical genius. He chose ground on which judicial review could be asserted at no political cost: the ruling went against the Federalist plaintiff, ordered nothing against the Jefferson administration, and struck down a provision (extension of Supreme Court original jurisdiction) that the Federalists themselves had supported. As the [[Lectures/Marbury v Madison - Judicial Review|Marbury lecture]] emphasizes, the doctrine was made permanent in part because Marshall manufactured a confrontation he was guaranteed to win.

### Internal Limits: The Political Question Doctrine in Operation

[[Cases/The Prize Cases (1863)|The Prize Cases]] applied *Marbury*'s political question distinction to presidential war-making. The Court held that the President's determination that armed hostilities have risen to the level of belligerency is committed to executive judgment and not reviewable by courts. The reasoning is structurally identical to Marshall's: where law commits a question to a political branch's discretion, the courts have no role in second-guessing it. Once again the Court announced its power while simultaneously narrowing the circumstances in which it would exercise it.

[[Cases/Youngstown Sheet and Tube Co v Sawyer (1952)|Youngstown]] sits on the opposite side of this distinction. President Truman's seizure of the steel mills was unmistakably a legal question concerning the lawmaking authority of the executive branch, not a discretionary exercise within an enumerated commitment to the President. The Court did not hesitate to enter the dispute and strike the seizure down. Jackson's framework -- maximum presidential authority when Congress authorizes, twilight when Congress is silent, lowest ebb when Congress prohibits -- presupposes that courts have institutional competence to police executive action against legislative will. That presupposition is *Marbury*'s rule in operation.

### Bounding Cases (Same Term as Marbury)

The [[Lectures/Marbury v Madison - Judicial Review|Marbury lecture]] flags *Stuart v Laird* (1803), decided the same week, as the immediate political bounding of *Marbury*. The Court upheld the Repeal Act of 1802 (which abolished the midnight-judge courts that the Federalists had created) and approved circuit riding by Supreme Court justices. The lecture suggests this can be read as a tactical capitulation to Jeffersonian pressure that immediately qualified the assertiveness of *Marbury*. The pattern -- announce the principle in a low-cost case, accept compromise on the high-cost question -- becomes a recurring move in the development of judicial review.

The lecture also notes pre-*Marbury* hints at judicial review: *Ware v Hylton* (1796) (federal supremacy over state law), *Calder v Bull* (1798) (Justice Iredell's concurrence anticipating review of federal statutes), and Hamilton's *Federalist No. 78*. *Marbury* did not invent the idea so much as supply the canonical statement that settled the institutional question.

## Judicial Review in Action: What Each Case Reviewed and What It Refused to Review

| Dimension | Marbury (1803) | McCulloch (1819) | Prize Cases (1863) | Youngstown (1952) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Object of review | Section 13 of the Judiciary Act (federal statute) and Madison's refusal to deliver a judicial commission (executive act) | Maryland's tax on the Bank of the United States (state statute) and the enabling Act chartering the Bank (federal statute) | Lincoln's proclamation of blockade against Confederate ports (executive act) | Truman's Executive Order 10340 seizing steel mills (executive act) |
| Rule enforced | Article III original-jurisdiction enumeration; no judicial remedy for discretionary executive acts | Article I implied powers; Supremacy Clause bars state interference with legitimate federal instrumentalities | Executive response to armed hostilities is a fact-bound political judgment | Congress, not the President, makes domestic law; legislative rejection counts as prohibition |
| Internal limit the case articulates | Political-question distinction (acts "in their nature political" are unreviewable) | Judicial modesty on the scope of "necessary and proper" ("we must never forget that it is a constitution we are expounding") | Political question applied to belligerency determination | Justiciability presumed; Jackson's tripartite framework supplies the metric |
| Relationship to *Marbury* | Source of the doctrine | Assumes judicial authority to strike state law interfering with federal power; extends Marbury to federalism | Cites Marbury's political-question distinction as an internal limit on review | Cites Marbury for the proposition that the judicial department "says what the law is," including as to the Executive |
| Outcome | Section 13 struck; no mandamus issued | Maryland tax struck; Bank upheld | President's action upheld as non-justiciable | President's action struck on the merits |

Marbury is the root; the other three are the branches. McCulloch extends the power to strike state law; Prize Cases recognizes an internal boundary in the political-question doctrine Marbury already announced in passing; and Youngstown operationalizes the review of executive conduct that Marbury held reviewable but prudently declined to order.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

1. **Hypo:** Congress enacts a federal Civil Procedure Reform Act that purports to give the Supreme Court original jurisdiction over any class action filed by 1,000 or more plaintiffs against the federal government. A class files directly in the Supreme Court invoking the statute. **Why:** Under *Marbury*, Article III's enumeration of the Supreme Court's original jurisdiction is exhaustive and cannot be expanded by Congress. The statute is unconstitutional as applied to original jurisdiction, regardless of how compelling the policy reasons for direct Supreme Court access might be. The plaintiffs must begin in a lower federal court.

2. **Hypo:** A federal statute requires lower federal courts to dismiss a category of constitutional claims unless the plaintiff first obtains executive branch certification that the case may proceed. A plaintiff sues without obtaining certification; the executive refuses to certify. **Why:** Under *Marbury*'s core principle, if the constitutional claim is meritorious then a court has the duty to apply the Constitution and disregard a statute that conflicts with it. A scheme that conditions judicial review of constitutional claims on executive permission gives the political branches "a practical and real omnipotence" over the Constitution -- the precise outcome *Marbury* refused to accept.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

3. **Hypo:** Congress enacts a statute expanding the Supreme Court's appellate jurisdiction to include a new category of state-court tax appeals. A litigant invokes the statute to bring an appeal that would not previously have been within Supreme Court appellate jurisdiction. **Why:** *Marbury*'s holding is specifically about *original* jurisdiction. Article III, Section 2 expressly provides that the Court's appellate jurisdiction is subject to "such Exceptions, and under such Regulations as the Congress shall make." Congress retains broad authority to expand or contract appellate jurisdiction by ordinary legislation. The *Marbury* rule does not apply.

4. **Hypo:** A state legislator who voted against a federal statute sues to enjoin its enforcement, arguing the statute is unconstitutional and that as a legislator who lost the vote he has standing to sue. **Why:** The judicial review power *Marbury* established operates only within the case-or-controversy framework of Article III. A legislator's institutional dissatisfaction is not the kind of concrete personal injury that gives rise to a justiciable case. Judicial review is not a free-standing power to issue advisory opinions on the constitutionality of legislation -- it is a power to decide cases that arise from real disputes, with parties properly before the court.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

5. **Hypo:** Congress enacts a statute providing that no court (state or federal) may entertain any constitutional challenge to a particular detention scheme during the pendency of an armed conflict, and providing that detentions become reviewable only after Congress declares the conflict ended. A detainee files a habeas action; the executive invokes the statute. **Why:** This pits *Marbury*'s logic of judicial review against the political question doctrine *Marbury* itself recognized and against Article I, Section 9's Suspension Clause framework. On the *Marbury* side: stripping all judicial review of detentions during open-ended conflict is functionally indistinguishable from giving the political branches unreviewable authority over individual liberty. On the political question / Suspension Clause side: the Constitution itself contemplates that ordinary judicial review may yield in extraordinary national security circumstances. The result depends on whether the suspension is authorized in form, whether the underlying detention scheme is consistent with constitutional rights, and how courts characterize a conflict without a clear endpoint. *Marbury*'s core principle suggests courts will find some path to review; the political question and Suspension Clause framework suggests they may defer significantly.

## How to Spot on an Exam

**Trigger facts:** A statute or executive order is challenged as unconstitutional; Congress purports to limit federal court jurisdiction; a litigant sues directly in the Supreme Court; an executive officer claims a duty is committed to political discretion; the constitutionality of an act has been left ambiguous by lower courts and the Supreme Court is being asked to settle it; a coordinate branch challenges the Court's authority to interpret the Constitution.

**Analytical framework:**

1. Is there a properly framed case or controversy with a plaintiff who has suffered a concrete injury and a defendant whose conduct can be remedied? If not, no judicial review (no advisory opinions).
2. Is the question one *Marbury* committed to political discretion? Apply the ministerial / discretionary distinction. Discretionary executive judgment over matters committed by law to the political branches is not for courts. Specific legal duties tied to individual rights are.
3. If the suit is in the Supreme Court directly, is it within Article III, Section 2's enumerated original jurisdiction? Congress cannot expand original jurisdiction, but can adjust appellate jurisdiction.
4. If a statute conflicts with the Constitution, the Constitution governs. Apply the substantive constitutional rule and treat the statute as void in that case. Watch for "saving constructions" that allow the court to read the statute narrowly to avoid the constitutional question (see the [[Lectures/Enumerated Powers - New Deal|New Deal lecture]] for the *NLRB v Jones & Laughlin* example).
5. Distinguish judicial *review* (the Court's power to decline to enforce an unconstitutional act in this case) from judicial *supremacy* (the Court's interpretation binds the coordinate branches as a final settlement). *Marbury* asserts both, but the broader claim is more contested. Departmentalist arguments (Jefferson, Lincoln, modern critics) accept review while denying supremacy.

**Common traps:**

- Article III's enumeration of original jurisdiction is exhaustive but applies *only to original jurisdiction*. Congress retains substantial authority over appellate jurisdiction.
- The political question doctrine sits inside *Marbury*, not against it. A holding that a question is non-justiciable is itself an exercise of judicial review (the Court is interpreting Article III to identify the limits of its own role).
- A "saving construction" is a device for avoiding the constitutional question -- if the statute can plausibly be read to avoid the conflict, the court may do so without exercising the *Marbury* power. This is a feature of judicial review, not a retreat from it.

## Critique

*Marbury*'s doctrinal foundation is more contested than its central place in the curriculum suggests. The argument from constitutional supremacy proves only that the Constitution is paramount law. It does not establish that *courts* in particular -- rather than Congress, the President, or some combination -- must be the final interpreters of what the Constitution means. The distinction between judicial *review* and judicial *supremacy* is the leverage point for departmentalist critique. Jefferson and Lincoln both held that coordinate branches had an independent obligation to interpret the Constitution and were bound by the Court's judgments only as to the parties before it, not as to the constitutional question itself.

A second, narrower critique attacks the opinion's craft. Section 13 of the Judiciary Act of 1789 may not have actually granted the Supreme Court original jurisdiction over mandamus against federal officers. A natural reading places the mandamus authority in the appellate context. If so, no constitutional conflict existed, and Marshall manufactured the issue he wanted to decide. A principle of constitutional law established through a deliberate doctrinal detour rests on shakier foundations than its rhetorical confidence suggests.

A third concern is institutional. *Marbury* concentrates final interpretive authority in a small, life-tenured body that is largely insulated from democratic accountability. Other constitutional democracies -- the United Kingdom, pre-Charter Canada, Australia in its mainstream judicial-review practice -- have operated with weaker forms of judicial review or with explicit legislative override mechanisms. The American model of strong judicial supremacy is one option among several available within constitutional democracy.

A fourth concern is internal: the doctrines that limit judicial review from within -- political question, justiciability, executive privilege, prudential standing -- have grown into substantial bodies of law that allow courts to decline review of politically inconvenient questions. Marshall's distinction between ministerial and discretionary executive acts, planted in *Marbury* as a one-paragraph aside, has become a discretionary tool for the Court to manage its political exposure. Critics argue that the same Court that asserts judicial supremacy when it serves its institutional interest withdraws into political-question abstinence when continuing engagement would be costly. *Marbury* contains both moves and has been used to justify each.

A final, present-tense concern flagged by the [[Lectures/Marbury v Madison - Judicial Review|Marbury lecture]]: *Marbury*'s norm depends on coordinate branches' willingness to comply with judicial judgments. A regime of strong judicial review survives because the political branches have, on the whole, treated the Court's judgments as binding. Where that compliance is contested -- through intimidation of judges, defiance of judgments, or sustained disinformation about the Court's authority -- the *Marbury* regime is structurally vulnerable in ways that the opinion itself cannot prevent.
