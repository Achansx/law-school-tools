# Presidential Removal Power

## Overview

The removal power is the constitutional authority of the President to dismiss officers who execute the laws. Article II does not expressly grant a removal power. The Supreme Court has derived one from the Vesting Clause, which commits "the executive Power" to the President, together with the Take Care Clause, which directs that he "shall take Care that the Laws be faithfully executed." Because the President cannot personally execute the laws, he must act through subordinates, and the power to remove those subordinates is what renders them accountable to him and, through him, to the electorate.

Removal-power doctrine oscillates between two frameworks. The formalist or unitary-executive approach treats Article II as committing all executive power to a single President, so any for-cause protection that insulates an officer wielding executive authority is constitutionally suspect. The functionalist approach treats the administrative state as a joint product of Article II and Congress's Necessary and Proper Clause authority, so Congress can structure agencies with for-cause removal protections as a matter of sound governance, subject only to the judicially managed limit that the restrictions not "unduly trammel" presidential authority.

The current doctrine, as restated in *Seila Law* (2020), treats unrestricted presidential removal as the general rule with two narrow exceptions: (1) for-cause protection for multimember bodies of experts not exercising purely executive power (*Humphreys Executor*), and (2) tenure protection for certain inferior officers with narrowly defined duties (*Morrison v Olson*). A single principal officer wielding significant executive power fits neither exception. A parallel line, anchored in *Bowsher v Synar* (1986), forbids Congress from reserving removal power over an officer charged with executing the laws.

---

## Governing Rule

> **General rule (Seila Law, 2020):** "The CFPB's leadership by a single individual removable only for inefficiency, neglect, or malfeasance violates the separation of powers." ([591 U.S. at 204-05](https://app.midpage.ai/document/seila-law-llc-v-consumer-4544949?utm_source=mcp&lines=245)) The President's removal power over officers who wield executive authority is the default; Congress may impose for-cause restrictions only within two recognized exceptions.

> **Exception 1 (Humphreys Executor, 1935):** Congress may protect a multimember body of experts balanced along partisan lines, appointed to staggered terms, performing quasi-legislative and quasi-judicial functions and said not to exercise purely executive power. Roberts's majority in Seila Law recast this as a narrow exception rather than a general permission.

> **Exception 2 (Morrison v Olson, 1988):** Congress may protect inferior officers with narrowly defined duties. Seila Law preserved this exception but did not expand it.

> **Congressional-removal limit (Bowsher v Synar, 1986):** Congress cannot reserve for itself the power of removal of an officer charged with the execution of the laws, except through impeachment. An officer subject to congressional removal cannot constitutionally be vested with the power to execute the laws, because allowing Congress to remove such an officer for "inefficiency" or "neglect" would amount to a prohibited legislative veto over the execution of statutes.

## Doctrinal Development

### Myers v United States (1926)

Myers establishes the baseline. President Wilson fired a postmaster without Senate consent, contrary to an 1876 statute that required Senate approval for removal. Chief Justice Taft, himself a former President, held that Congress cannot condition the President's removal power over purely executive officers. Taft's opinion rests the removal power on the Article II Vesting Clause and the Take Care Clause and treats the First Congress's Decision of 1789 as a contemporaneous liquidation of constitutional meaning. Read broadly, Myers commits an "illimitable" removal power to the President over all executive officers. No brief yet; see [[Lectures/Presidential Removal Power - Humphreys Executor to Trump v Slaughter|full lecture treatment]].

### Humphreys Executor v United States (1935)

Humphreys carved out the exception that defined the twentieth-century administrative state. FDR fired an FTC Commissioner for purely political reasons despite the FTC statute's for-cause requirement. A unanimous Court held that Congress may restrict removal of FTC Commissioners. Myers was confined to "purely executive officers" like postmasters. The FTC Commissioner, on the Court's account, exercised quasi-legislative and quasi-judicial functions and performed duties that were "neither political nor executive." The 1935 FTC had no fining power, no monetary penalties, and had to go to court to enforce its orders. Seila Law would later observe that the modern FTC, with its rulemaking power and enforcement actions, "would at the present time be considered executive." No brief yet; see [[Lectures/Presidential Removal Power - Humphreys Executor to Trump v Slaughter|full lecture treatment]].

### Bowsher v Synar (1986)

Bowsher addressed a different axis of the removal problem: not for-cause insulation from the President, but reservation of removal authority in Congress itself. The Gramm-Rudman-Hollings Act assigned final sequestration authority to the Comptroller General, an officer removable by Congress via joint resolution. Chief Justice Burger's majority held that Congress cannot reserve removal power over an officer charged with executing the laws. Allowing Congress to remove such an officer for "inefficiency" or "neglect" would permit a legislative veto over law-execution of the kind condemned in *INS v Chadha* (1983). See [[Cases/Bowsher v Synar (1986)|full brief]].

### Morrison v Olson (1988)

Morrison is the high-water mark of congressional power over removal under a functionalist framework. The Ethics in Government Act authorized for-cause removal of an independent counsel by the Attorney General. A 7-1 Court upheld the restriction. The majority replaced Humphreys's categorical "purely executive" line with a functional question: does the restriction impede the President's ability to perform his constitutional duties? Because the independent counsel was an inferior officer with limited jurisdiction and limited tenure, and because the Attorney General retained removal authority, the restriction was permissible. Justice Scalia's lone dissent argued that governmental investigation and prosecution of crimes is a quintessentially executive function and that any for-cause restriction on an officer exercising such power is unconstitutional. Seila Law's majority treats Scalia's dissent as now-vindicated, though the Court preserved Morrison as a surviving exception rather than overruling it. No brief yet; see [[Lectures/Executive Power over Agency Officials - Appointment and Removal|full lecture treatment]].

### Free Enterprise Fund v PCAOB (2010)

Free Enterprise Fund began the modern retreat from Morrison and Humphreys. The Sarbanes-Oxley Act created the Public Company Accounting Oversight Board with members removable by the SEC only for cause; SEC commissioners were themselves removable by the President only for cause. Chief Justice Roberts's 5-4 majority held that dual for-cause removal is unconstitutional. "Officers safely encased within a Matryoshka doll of tenure protections would be immune from Presidential oversight." The remedy was severance of the PCAOB for-cause restriction. Crucially, the opinion recast Humphreys and Morrison as narrow exceptions to a general rule of unrestricted removal rather than as independent principles, and articulated the "since 1789" framing that Seila Law would adopt wholesale. No brief yet; see [[Lectures/Presidential Removal Power - Humphreys Executor to Trump v Slaughter|full lecture treatment]].

### Seila Law LLC v CFPB (2020)

Seila Law is the controlling modern statement. The CFPB, created by Dodd-Frank in 2010, is led by a single Director removable only for "inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance in office" and wields rulemaking, enforcement, and adjudicative authority over nineteen federal consumer-protection statutes. Chief Justice Roberts's 5-4 majority held the single-director-for-cause structure unconstitutional. The majority declined amicus Paul Clement's invitation to expand Humphreys into a general permission slip for Congress to structure removal protections however it wishes, stating that the Court would not "elevate [Humphreys Executor] into a freestanding invitation for Congress to impose additional restrictions on the President's removal authority." The opinion preserved both Humphreys and Morrison as narrow exceptions but recharacterized them as two exceptions to a general rule rather than components of a functionalist balancing test. A separate 7-2 majority severed the removal restriction from the rest of Title X, leaving the CFPB operational with its Director now removable at will.

Justice Thomas, joined by Justice Gorsuch, concurred in part and would have overruled Humphreys outright on the ground that the "quasi-legislative/quasi-judicial" taxonomy is a judicial invention without constitutional foundation. Justice Kagan, joined by Justices Ginsburg, Breyer, and Sotomayor, dissented on three grounds: the Necessary and Proper Clause commits agency design to Congress; historical practice (the Second Bank of the United States, the Comptroller of the Currency, the ICC, the Federal Reserve, the FTC, and the New Deal administrative state) has liquidated constitutional meaning in favor of congressional latitude; and the single-versus-plural distinction has no Article II pedigree. "The Constitution does not distinguish between single-director and multimember independent agencies. It instructs Congress, not this Court, to decide on agency design." See [[Cases/Seila Law LLC v CFPB (2020)|full brief]].

### Collins v Yellen (2021)

Collins applied Seila Law to the Federal Housing Finance Agency, which Congress had structured identically (single director, for-cause removal). The unconstitutional removal restriction was severed. But Collins also addressed a remedial question Seila Law left open: whether actions taken by officers subject to unconstitutional removal protection are voidable. The Court answered no, provided the President had not attempted to remove the officer and been prevented from doing so. The structural flaw is therefore largely retrospective rather than ratification-undoing, which reduces the practical bite of the Seila Law rule for pre-severance agency actions. Justice Thomas's Collins concurrence went further than Seila Law, arguing that Humphreys Executor was "wrongly decided" and should be formally overruled. No brief yet; see [[Lectures/Presidential Removal Power - Humphreys Executor to Trump v Slaughter|full lecture treatment]].

### Trump v Slaughter (2025) and Trump v Cook (2026)

The Trump administration's 2025 dismissals of NLRB member Gwynne Wilcox, MSPB member Cathy Harris, FTC Commissioners Slaughter and Bedoya, and EEOC Democrats, all without citation of statutory cause, triggered a wave of separation-of-powers litigation. The Supreme Court granted three shadow docket stays in 2025 (Trump v Wilcox in May, Trump v Boyle in July, Trump v Slaughter in September), effectively permitting at-will removal while Humphreys Executor technically remained "good law." Justice Kagan dissented in each, warning that "our emergency docket should never be used to permit what our own precedent bars." At oral argument in Trump v Slaughter (December 8, 2025), the Solicitor General argued for "exclusive and illimitable" presidential removal power and conceded possible exceptions only for the Federal Reserve and non-Article III courts. A decision is expected June 2026. Trump v Cook (argued January 21, 2026), which challenges Trump's attempted removal of Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook, tests whether the Fed exception survives. No briefs yet; see [[Lectures/Presidential Removal Power - Humphreys Executor to Trump v Slaughter|full lecture treatment]].

## Comparison: The Four Removal-Power Axes

The doctrine sorts agency structures along four independent axes. A given statute is constitutional only when every axis clears the Seila Law framework.

| Axis | Permissive Position | Restrictive Position | Governing Case |
|------|--------------------|--------------------|----------------|
| Who controls removal | President controls removal, subject to possible for-cause restriction | Congress reserves removal power to itself | *Bowsher v Synar* (1986) forbids the restrictive position |
| Agency head structure | Multimember body balanced by partisan composition and staggered terms | Single principal officer | *Humphreys Executor* (1935) permits for-cause insulation of the permissive structure; *Seila Law* (2020) forbids it for the restrictive structure |
| Officer status | Inferior officer with narrowly defined duties | Principal officer wielding significant executive power | *Morrison v Olson* (1988) permits for-cause insulation of the permissive position; *Seila Law* confines Morrison to that category |
| Layers of protection | Single layer of for-cause protection | Two or more layers of for-cause protection | *Free Enterprise Fund* (2010) forbids the restrictive position |

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side

1. **The single-director Consumer Product Safety Commission rewrite.** Congress restructures the CPSC from a five-member commission into a single-director agency headed by an officer removable only for "inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance in office," wielding rulemaking, civil-penalty, and recall authority over a substantial sector of the consumer economy. **Why:** the structural features match Seila Law exactly, a single principal officer with for-cause removal and significant executive power. Roberts's rule applies directly without any doctrinal extension.

2. **A congressionally removable inspector-general with final enforcement authority.** Congress creates a new Inspector General for Critical Infrastructure with power to issue binding compliance orders against private operators, and makes the IG removable by Congress via joint resolution for inefficiency or neglect. **Why:** Bowsher controls. An officer who executes the laws cannot be placed under congressional removal short of impeachment, because the threat of legislative removal for disfavored enforcement decisions is functionally a legislative veto over law-execution.

### Opposite-Side

3. **Reconstituted CFPB as a multimember commission.** Congress amends Dodd-Frank to replace the single Director with a five-member bipartisan commission, staggered terms, partisan-balance requirement, removable only for cause. **Why:** the restructured agency fits squarely within Humphreys Executor's preserved exception. Roberts's Seila Law majority carefully preserved the multimember-body exception, and Kagan's dissent would reinforce the outcome on functionalist grounds. This is precisely the alternative structure Roberts's opinion contemplated.

4. **Inferior-officer special counsel with limited jurisdiction.** Congress creates a Special Counsel for Cybersecurity, appointed by the Attorney General, with limited jurisdiction over one agency's cyber-crime docket, for a two-year term, removable only for cause by the AG. **Why:** Morrison v Olson's exception for inferior officers with narrowly defined duties survives Seila Law. Roberts's majority treats Morrison as a preserved precedent for this category of officer, and the AG's retention of removal authority satisfies the unity-of-command concern that animates the doctrine. Thomas's concurrence would dissent; the majority's two-exceptions framework controls.

### Fence-Sitter

5. **Presidential removal of a Federal Reserve Governor.** The President dismisses a sitting Fed Governor without citing statutory cause, relying on the claim that the Federal Reserve Act's for-cause language is unconstitutional after Seila Law and Collins. **Why unclear:** (a) The Fed Governor is a principal officer wielding significant economic authority, which aligns the case with Seila Law's rule. (b) But the Fed's unique "quasi-private" structure, tracing to the First and Second Banks of the United States, was flagged by the Wilcox majority as potentially exceptional and was conceded at Trump v Slaughter oral argument by the Solicitor General. (c) The Fed is also a multimember body with staggered fourteen-year terms, which lines up with the Humphreys exception, but the Chair's enhanced power and the Fed's unambiguously executive monetary functions complicate that fit. The question is live in Trump v Cook (argued January 2026); the outcome will turn on whether the Court identifies a principled Article II basis for a Fed exception or treats the monetary-policy and market-stability rationales as mere pragmatism.

## How to Spot on an Exam

Trigger language on a Con Law exam typically involves: (1) a statutory for-cause removal provision governing a federal officer, (2) a President attempting to dismiss that officer without citing cause, or Congress attempting to reserve removal power for itself, and (3) a challenge framed in separation-of-powers terms. Watch for fact patterns that describe agency structure: how many heads, whether the head is a principal or inferior officer, whether the for-cause protection is single-layer or stacked, and whether the agency wields significant executive power or is confined to quasi-legislative and quasi-judicial functions.

The analytical framework tracks the Seila Law two-exception structure. First, identify who controls removal. If Congress reserves removal authority (Bowsher problem), the restriction is unconstitutional unless it proceeds via impeachment. Second, if the President controls removal and the question is whether a for-cause restriction is valid, identify which of the two Humphreys/Morrison exceptions is at issue. Multimember bipartisan board performing quasi-legislative and quasi-judicial functions: Humphreys applies. Inferior officer with narrowly defined duties: Morrison applies. Neither: Seila Law's general rule controls and the for-cause restriction falls. Third, look for the Free Enterprise Fund wrinkle of two or more layers of for-cause protection, which fails regardless of agency structure.

On remedy, Collins v Yellen confines relief: even an unconstitutional removal restriction will not void prior agency actions unless the challenger can show the President actually attempted removal and was prevented by the restriction. Severance is the standard remedy.

The live doctrinal question in any 2025 or 2026 exam is Trump v Slaughter's status. If the question asks whether a for-cause restriction on a multimember commission survives, flag that Humphreys remains formally controlling precedent but has been described by the Chief Justice as a "dried husk," that shadow docket stays have permitted at-will removal of FTC Commissioners pending merits review, and that a decision overruling or significantly narrowing Humphreys is expected in June 2026.

## Critique

**Formalist / unitary-executive critique (Calabresi, Prakash, Yoo; Thomas's Seila Law concurrence and Collins concurrence as internal critique).** From this vantage, the current doctrine is too cautious. The unitary executive thesis holds that Article II's Vesting Clause, read in light of the Decision of 1789 and Myers, commits "the executive Power" to the President in a way that forbids any for-cause insulation of officers wielding executive authority. Preserving Humphreys Executor as a narrow exception is a judicial compromise without constitutional foundation. The formalist critique predicts, and broadly welcomes, a future decision overruling Humphreys outright, the position Thomas and Gorsuch adopt in Collins.

**Functionalist / Kagan critique.** The counterweight reads Article II's Vesting Clause as a grant of authority to the President within a constitutional scheme that also authorizes Congress to structure the administrative state under the Necessary and Proper Clause. On this view, the Court has no principled basis for identifying single-director-plus-for-cause as the constitutional tripwire; the feature combination is a policy preference, not a textual command. Kagan's Seila Law dissent treats the single-versus-plural distinction as unprincipled: a multimember commission concentrates authority in a majority of commissioners who can block presidential priorities just as effectively as a single insulated director. The "congressional bargain" principle, central to Kagan's opinion, holds that Congress's willingness to delegate vast regulatory authority was conditioned on agency independence, and retroactively destroying that independence raises serious structural questions about whether delegations were legitimately made.

**Historical-practice critique.** Both the majority and dissent in Seila Law invoke historical practice, but they read the same record differently. The formalist camp treats the 1789 Decision and Myers as establishing a strong default, with Humphreys Executor as an outlier the Court should not extend. The functionalist camp treats the long unbroken line of independent agencies (Second Bank through the modern regulatory state) as liquidating constitutional meaning in favor of congressional latitude. Kagan's Seila Law dissent notes that in 1816, fifteen of twenty-five directors of the Second Bank could not be removed by the President, a more aggressive insulation than the CFPB Director enjoyed.

**Shadow docket critique.** Professor Chandler's lecture frames the 2025 stays as an "indecent burial" of Humphreys compared to the "decent funeral" Chevron received in Loper Bright. Kagan's dissents in Wilcox, Boyle, and Slaughter argue that using emergency applications to effectively kill a foundational precedent before full briefing and merits argument is a profound departure from judicial process and, by shifting structural power from Congress to the President, a reshaping of the separation of powers that ought to occur, if at all, on the merits docket.

**Administrative-state structural critique.** If Humphreys falls entirely, the immediate question is whether the Federal Reserve, the Federal Communications Commission, the Securities and Exchange Commission, the Federal Trade Commission, and other long-standing multimember independent agencies need restructuring or formal statutory authorization. The Executive Power over Agency Officials lecture raises the double-blow scenario in which agencies lose both independence (removal power) and broad rulemaking authority (through a tightened nondelegation doctrine Gorsuch has signaled on the merits). Whether Congress would have constructed the modern regulatory state under those constraints is, on the functionalist account, a serious counterfactual the Court's structural jurisprudence has not confronted.
