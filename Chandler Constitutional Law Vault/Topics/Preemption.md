# Preemption

## Overview

Preemption is the doctrine that federal law displaces state law when the two conflict or when Congress has signaled that state regulation is precluded. Its textual source is Article VI, Clause 2, the Supremacy Clause, which declares that the Constitution, federal statutes, and treaties are "the supreme Law of the Land" and binds state judges "anything in the Constitution or Laws of any State to the Contrary notwithstanding." The Supremacy Clause is self-executing in the limited sense that a conflicting state rule yields once a conflict is identified; it does not, however, tell a court when a state rule is actually inconsistent with federal law. That is the work of preemption doctrine.

Professor Chandler frames preemption as primarily a problem of statutory interpretation rather than constitutional adjudication. The constitutional hook is stable; the contested questions are linguistic and structural. How broadly should a phrase like "relating to" in ERISA or "with respect to" in the Federal Cigarette Labeling and Advertising Act sweep? Has Congress so occupied a regulatory field that no room remains for state law? Does state law stand as an obstacle to the accomplishment of federal objectives? These are interpretive inquiries that straddle statutory interpretation, administrative law, federal courts, and constitutional law. See [[Lectures/Federal Preemption and the Supremacy Clause|Preemption lecture]].

Preemption doctrine organizes into a three-branch tree. Express preemption occurs when a federal statute explicitly displaces state law, either comprehensively or as to specified conduct; the linguistic precision of the preemption clause drives the analysis. Field preemption occurs when Congress has legislated so thoroughly in a domain that it has implicitly occupied the entire field, leaving no room for state regulation even without a direct conflict. Conflict preemption has two subtypes: impossibility preemption, where simultaneous compliance with both federal and state law is physically impossible, and obstacle preemption, where state law stands as an obstacle to the accomplishment of federal objectives. Chandler's practical emphasis: physical impossibility is rare; most contested cases involve express-clause interpretation, field occupation, or obstacle preemption.

[[Cases/Arizona v United States (2012)|Arizona v United States]] (2012) is the modern showcase. Justice Kennedy's majority opinion runs the full preemption tree across four provisions of Arizona's SB 1070. Section 3 (making it a state misdemeanor for an alien to fail to carry federal alien-registration documents) is field-preempted by the comprehensive federal Registration Act scheme. Section 5(C) (criminalizing an unauthorized alien's seeking or performing work) is obstacle-preempted because Congress in the Immigration Reform and Control Act (IRCA) made a deliberate choice to sanction employers rather than impose criminal liability on unauthorized workers, and Arizona's contrary penalty frustrates that balance. Section 6 (authorizing warrantless arrests on probable cause of removability) is obstacle-preempted because it disrupts the centralized federal enforcement system by permitting unilateral state arrest decisions. Section 2(B), the status-check provision, survives facial challenge because nothing in the federal scheme categorically forbids state officers from checking immigration status during an otherwise lawful stop, though as-applied challenges remain available.

A presumption against preemption operates, according to Chandler, mainly as a tiebreaker when a federal statute explicitly preempts and courts must determine the scope of the clause. It is weaker or absent in fields where states have not traditionally regulated, where federal regulatory presence has historically been significant (maritime, national banking, foreign affairs, military), or where fraud on federal agencies is at issue. The Congressional Research Service characterizes the presumption as unsettled; Chandler's view is more direct, treating the presumption as a residual tool rather than a robust canon.

This page sits beside [[Topics/Dormant Commerce Clause|Dormant Commerce Clause]] in the federalism taxonomy. Preemption requires an actual federal statute displacing state law; the dormant Commerce Clause operates as a background default about what Congress would preempt if it had legislated on the subject. Congress can override a DCC bar (McCarran-Ferguson for insurance, Webb-Kenyon for alcohol), confirming the DCC's status as a judge-made default rather than a preemption rule. The preemption-commandeering axis is the other structural companion. Preemption tells States their law is displaced; anti-commandeering (developed in the Commandeering lecture) tells Congress it cannot conscript state legislative or executive machinery to enforce federal law. Both doctrines police the federal-state boundary, but from opposite directions.

## Governing Rule

> **The Supremacy Clause (Article VI, Clause 2):** The Constitution, laws of the United States made pursuant to it, and treaties are the supreme Law of the Land. State judges are bound by them notwithstanding any contrary state constitution or law. The clause is self-executing: once a conflict is identified, state law yields without any further congressional action.

> **Express Preemption:** Congress expressly displaces state law through a statutory preemption clause. The scope of displacement is a question of statutory interpretation driven by the language of the clause ("relating to" in ERISA, "with respect to" in the Federal Cigarette Labeling and Advertising Act). The presumption against preemption operates, where it operates at all, as a tiebreaker favoring narrow readings of ambiguous clauses.

> **Field Preemption (Arizona v United States, 2012):** Federal law preempts state law in an entire field when Congress has legislated so comprehensively that it has left no room for state regulation, even without a direct conflict. Field preemption requires a pervasive federal scheme, not merely the existence of federal regulation in the general area. The federal Registration Act scheme preempted Arizona's Section 3 duplicate criminal alien-registration provision because Congress had occupied the field of alien registration.

> **Conflict Preemption (Impossibility):** State law is preempted when simultaneous compliance with federal and state law is physically impossible. Chandler's practical assessment is that true impossibility is very rare; the doctrine's main domain is cases where federal and state rules set different maxima or minima for the same conduct.

> **Conflict Preemption (Obstacle):** State law is preempted when it stands as an obstacle to the accomplishment and execution of the full purposes and objectives of Congress. Sections 5(C) and 6 of SB 1070 were obstacle-preempted because Arizona's criminal penalty on unauthorized workers frustrated Congress's IRCA choice to sanction employers, and unilateral state arrest authority on removability disrupted the centralized federal enforcement scheme.

> **As-Applied Preservation (Arizona v United States, 2012):** Facial preemption challenges can fail while preserving as-applied challenges on a developed record. Section 2(B), the status-check provision, survived facial challenge because nothing in the federal scheme categorically forbade state officers from checking immigration status during a lawful stop. Kennedy's opinion expressly reserved as-applied challenges for cases in which Section 2(B)'s enforcement produced unreasonable detention or systematic constitutional violations.

> **Plenary Federal Authority (Arizona v United States, 2012):** "The Government of the United States has broad, undoubted power over the subject of immigration and the status of aliens" (Kennedy, J.) [Midpage pin-cite](https://app.midpage.ai/document/arizona-v-united-states-803270?utm_source=mcp). The plenary characterization frames the field-preemption analysis: where federal authority is comprehensive and historically federal, the preemption presumption tilts toward displacement.

> **Preemption Presumption Calibration:** A presumption against preemption operates primarily as a tiebreaker in express-clause interpretation. It is weaker or absent in fields where states have not traditionally regulated (foreign affairs, maritime, national banking), where federal regulatory presence has historically been significant, or where fraud on federal agencies is at issue. It is strongest when a federal statute enters an area of historical state regulation (family law, general tort, health and safety) and its preemption scope is ambiguous.

## Doctrinal Development

### Constitutional Foundation: The Supremacy Clause

The Supremacy Clause dates to 1787 and answers the most fundamental federalism question the framers faced: when federal and state law conflict, which controls? The Articles of Confederation had given federal law no comparable supremacy, and the resulting state-by-state evasion of federal obligations (including treaty commitments to British creditors) was among the principal structural motivations for the Constitutional Convention. The clause also binds state judges directly, a structural feature that avoids any need for an intermediate step through state law.

Two points about the clause limit its doctrinal usefulness. First, it does not tell a court when a state rule is inconsistent with federal law. That judgment is the preemption inquiry and is almost always an exercise in statutory interpretation rather than constitutional reasoning. Second, the clause is one-directional: it forbids state law from prevailing over federal law, but it does not authorize Congress to enact what it lacks the substantive power to enact. A statute must first fall within an enumerated Article I power before the Supremacy Clause is engaged. Preemption cases therefore typically presuppose a valid federal statute and focus on the scope-and-conflict question. See the Preemption lecture's treatment of the constitutional foundation.

### The Three-Branch Preemption Tree

**Express preemption.** When Congress writes an explicit preemption clause, the interpretive question becomes the scope of the clause. ERISA's "relating to" language is among the broadest preemption clauses in federal law and has been construed to sweep in state contract and bad-faith tort claims arising from employee benefit plans, supporting complete preemption and removal to federal court. The Federal Cigarette Labeling and Advertising Act preempts "any requirement or prohibition based on smoking and health" with respect to the advertising or promotion of properly labeled cigarettes. Whether a state health-warning sticker falls within that language requires careful parsing: is it a "requirement"? Is it "based on smoking and health"? Is it directed at "advertising or promotion"? Chandler's point is that the presumption against preemption, if it operates at all, operates as a tiebreaker here, favoring narrow readings that preserve state authority.

**Field preemption.** Field preemption asks whether Congress has so thoroughly occupied a regulatory domain that no room remains for state action. The federal Registration Act's comprehensive alien-registration scheme occupied the registration field and preempted Arizona's Section 3 state-law duplicate. The domain of foreign affairs is another classical field-preempted area: states generally cannot establish their own foreign policy even where Congress has not explicitly preempted. Field preemption requires a pervasive scheme, not merely federal regulation in the vicinity. Courts look for comprehensive federal legislation, a dominant federal interest, historical federal occupation, or a structural judgment that dual regulation would frustrate uniformity.

**Conflict preemption: impossibility.** Physical impossibility preemption arises when it is literally impossible to comply with both federal and state requirements. Chandler's practical assessment: very rare in the real world. Federal and state rules usually coexist as floors or ceilings, and the more common question is whether the state rule imposes an obligation in addition to the federal rule (usually permissible) or instead of it (preempted).

**Conflict preemption: obstacle.** Obstacle preemption is the workhorse of the conflict branch. State law is preempted when it stands as an obstacle to the accomplishment and execution of the full purposes and objectives of Congress. The inquiry is purposive and atextual: courts identify what Congress was trying to achieve and ask whether state law gets in the way. The indeterminacy of the standard is a standing critique (Justice Thomas's critique of obstacle preemption is that it is an unbounded invitation for judicial preferences). Kennedy's Arizona opinion deploys the branch twice, to Section 5(C) and Section 6, in textbook form.

### The Presumption Against Preemption

The Congressional Research Service treats the presumption as unsettled. Chandler's view is more direct: the presumption is primarily a tiebreaker in the express-clause context, resolving ambiguity against displacement of state law. It is weaker or absent in three settings. First, in fields where states have not traditionally regulated (foreign affairs, immigration, maritime, national banking, lighthouses, certain military matters), federal authority is the historical norm and the presumption carries little weight. Second, in fraud-on-federal-agencies cases, the Court has held that the state rule effectively attempts to supplement federal regulatory machinery in a way Congress did not invite. Third, in areas where federal regulatory presence has been pervasive for decades (medical devices, airline service, rail safety), the presumption operates more weakly because states are not enforcing a historically sovereign regulatory interest.

The practical payoff is that the presumption rarely determines outcomes on its own. What drives results is the text of the preemption clause (for express preemption), the pervasiveness of the federal scheme (for field preemption), and the identification of congressional objectives against which the state law is tested (for obstacle preemption). Chandler is skeptical that a robust presumption exists as a freestanding interpretive canon.

### Arizona v United States (2012): The Modern Preemption Showcase

Arizona enacted SB 1070 in 2010 in response to frustration with federal enforcement of immigration law within the State. Four provisions were challenged. Section 3 made it a state misdemeanor for an alien to fail to carry federal alien-registration documents. Section 5(C) criminalized an unauthorized alien's seeking or performing work in Arizona. Section 6 authorized warrantless arrests on probable cause of a removable public offense. Section 2(B) required state officers conducting a lawful stop to make a reasonable attempt to determine immigration status when reasonable suspicion existed. The federal government sued, arguing that the Supremacy Clause preempted all four provisions.

Justice Kennedy's majority opinion began with a framing: "The Government of the United States has broad, undoubted power over the subject of immigration and the status of aliens" [Midpage pin-cite](https://app.midpage.ai/document/arizona-v-united-states-803270?utm_source=mcp). The characterization is analytically consequential. Because immigration is a traditional federal domain with plenary federal authority, the presumption against preemption operated weakly (if at all), and the comprehensive federal scheme supported field-preemption reasoning.

On **Section 3**, Congress had enacted the comprehensive federal Registration Act scheme, which imposed its own criminal penalties on aliens for failure to register. Arizona's Section 3 duplicated the federal requirement and imposed state criminal penalties in parallel. The Court held the field of alien registration was fully occupied by the federal scheme, preempting even the facially parallel state provision. Field preemption applied.

On **Section 5(C)**, the analysis turned on IRCA. Congress in 1986 created a detailed employer-sanction scheme, imposing civil and criminal penalties on employers who knowingly hire unauthorized workers, but Congress made a deliberate choice not to impose criminal liability on unauthorized workers for seeking or performing work. The legislative record and statutory structure confirmed the choice. Arizona's Section 5(C) imposed state criminal penalties on the unauthorized worker, frustrating Congress's calibrated allocation of sanctions. Obstacle preemption applied because the state law stood as an obstacle to the accomplishment of the IRCA scheme.

On **Section 6**, Kennedy deployed obstacle preemption again. The federal immigration-enforcement system centralizes arrest decisions in federal officials with specific statutory authority and discretion. Section 6 authorized state officers to make unilateral arrests on probable cause of removability, disrupting the unitary federal enforcement scheme. The state rule was not merely duplicative; it permitted state agents to make enforcement decisions Congress assigned to federal officials.

On **Section 2(B)**, Kennedy reached the opposite conclusion. Nothing in federal law categorically forbids state officers from checking immigration status during an otherwise lawful stop. Federal statutes contemplate cooperative enforcement and information-sharing; state officers making status inquiries during valid stops are not usurping federal authority. The facial challenge failed, but Kennedy expressly reserved as-applied challenges for cases in which Section 2(B) produced unreasonable detention, systematic racial profiling, or other constitutional violations. The reservation converted Section 2(B) from a preemption question into a future civil-rights litigation question.

The concurring and dissenting opinions are doctrinally significant. Justice Scalia would have upheld all four provisions, arguing that States retain inherent sovereign authority over immigration enforcement within their borders as a matter of original constitutional design. Justice Thomas would have upheld all four on a narrow preemption theory that rejects obstacle preemption as indeterminate. Justice Alito agreed that Sections 3 and 6 were preempted but would have upheld Section 5(C), distinguishing employer sanctions from worker penalties as targeted at different actors. Justice Kagan did not participate. The Arizona case is therefore a five-justice majority on a fragmented Court, with doctrinally distinct theories of why (or whether) preemption applied. See [[Cases/Arizona v United States (2012)|Arizona v United States brief]].

### Arizona's Companions and Doctrinal Background

Arizona sits in a cluster with Hines v Davidowitz (1941), the field-preemption predecessor in the alien-registration context, and Chamber of Commerce v Whiting (2011), which upheld Arizona's earlier Legal Arizona Workers Act license-revocation statute as a permissible state sanction on employers notwithstanding IRCA's preemption of state "civil or criminal sanctions" on employers who knowingly employ unauthorized aliens. Whiting distinguished licensure from the generic civil-or-criminal preemption clause because IRCA specifically saved "licensing and similar laws." Whiting and Arizona together mark the line between permissible state cooperation with federal immigration enforcement (Whiting; Section 2(B) in Arizona) and impermissible state displacement of the federal enforcement scheme (Sections 3, 5(C), and 6 in Arizona). See the Preemption lecture's background discussion and the Arizona brief.

### Hypothetical-Driven Applications in the Lecture

The Preemption lecture builds intuition through a set of extended hypotheticals that work through the tree in practical settings. The Federal Rail Safety Act hypothetical asks whether a state negligence claim for failing to slow a freight train in fog is preempted when federal regulations address train speed but not weather-based speed restrictions. The answer turns on whether the federal scheme occupies the field of train-speed regulation or merely establishes a partial floor, leaving room for state tort law to supplement. ERISA's "relating to" clause presents the breadth question in its starkest form: Texas bad-faith claims for insurer underpayment of medical benefits are preempted because ERISA sweeps extra-contractual damages even into state-law tort framings, supporting complete-preemption removal to federal court.

The Ukraine Freedom Support Act hypothetical asks whether a state procurement ban on sanctioned Russian entities survives when Congress has authorized the federal executive to waive sanctions. The answer turns on foreign-affairs field preemption plus obstacle preemption: the state floor frustrates the federal scheme's calibrated discretion. The cigarette-labels hypothetical asks whether a state COVID-warning sticker on cigarette packaging falls within FCLAA's preemption of "any requirement or prohibition based on smoking and health" with respect to advertising or promotion of compliant cigarettes. The driver's-license-for-refugees hypothetical asks whether a state documentation rule interferes with federal refugee classification rights tied to specific document types. The foie gras hypothetical asks whether a state animal-welfare regulation imposes requirements "within the scope of" the Federal Poultry Products Inspection Act. The Burger Control Act hypothetical contrasts direct conflict (New York at 15 percent fat conflicting with a federal 25 percent ceiling) with non-conflict (Texas minimum fat content for burgers when federal law sets only a ceiling): the outcome depends on whether the federal rule is a floor, a ceiling, or both.

Each hypothetical runs the same three-part inquiry: is there an express preemption clause and, if so, how broadly does it sweep; has Congress occupied the field; and does the state rule stand as an obstacle to the accomplishment of federal objectives. ## Comparison: The Three Preemption Branches Applied to SB 1070

| SB 1070 Provision | What it does | Preemption branch | Why it applies | Outcome |
|-------------------|--------------|-------------------|----------------|---------|
| **Section 3** | State misdemeanor for alien's failure to carry federal registration documents | Field | Federal Registration Act is a comprehensive and occupied scheme covering the full domain of alien registration; no room remains for parallel state criminal duplication | Preempted |
| **Section 5(C)** | State criminal penalty on unauthorized alien's seeking or performing work | Obstacle (conflict) | IRCA made a deliberate choice to sanction employers, not workers; Arizona's contrary penalty frustrates that balance and the statutory allocation of sanctions | Preempted |
| **Section 6** | State warrantless arrest on probable cause of removability | Obstacle (conflict) | Centralized federal immigration-enforcement scheme assigns arrest decisions to federal officials with specific authority and discretion; unilateral state arrests disrupt the unitary scheme | Preempted |
| **Section 2(B)** | State officers required to check status during lawful stops when reasonable suspicion exists | None on facial challenge (as-applied reserved) | Federal law contemplates cooperative enforcement and information-sharing; status inquiries during otherwise lawful stops do not usurp federal authority; as-applied challenges remain available for unreasonable detention or racial profiling | Not preempted on facial challenge |

Read horizontally, Arizona demonstrates the analytic work of each branch. Field preemption requires a comprehensive scheme occupied by Congress (Section 3). Obstacle preemption requires a congressional objective and a state rule that frustrates it (Sections 5(C) and 6). Section 2(B) survives because no branch applies on the face of the statute, illustrating that preemption is not automatic even when a state acts in a heavily federalized domain. The as-applied reservation is doctrinally important because it signals that pre-enforcement preemption litigation does not exhaust constitutional review; post-enforcement challenges on a developed record remain available.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side

1. **Hypo:** A State enacts a criminal statute making it a misdemeanor for any alien to fail to register with the federal government and carry proof of registration, tracking the federal Registration Act provisions verbatim. **Why:** This is Section 3 of SB 1070 under a different statutory caption. The federal Registration Act occupies the field of alien registration comprehensively. State-law duplication is not saved by parallelism; field preemption bars the State from criminalizing conduct Congress has fully regulated. Outcome: preempted under Arizona v United States.

2. **Hypo:** Congress enacts a comprehensive federal consumer-disclosure regime for credit cards, specifying required disclosures, a preemption clause covering "any state requirement concerning disclosures on consumer credit cards," and a federal enforcement-only remedy structure. A State enacts a consumer-credit statute requiring additional disclosures not contained in the federal scheme and authorizing a private right of action for violations. **Why:** The federal preemption clause's scope is determinative. "Any state requirement concerning disclosures" plainly reaches both additional disclosure content and state enforcement mechanisms that impose disclosure-based obligations. Express preemption applies; the presumption against preemption, if it operates at all, is too weak to overcome the clear statutory text. Outcome: preempted.

### Opposite-Side

3. **Hypo:** A State law requires private employers to verify the employment eligibility of new hires through the federal E-Verify system. Congress has authorized but not required E-Verify use. An employer association sues, arguing that IRCA's preemption clause for state "civil or criminal sanctions" on employers who knowingly employ unauthorized aliens forbids the state mandate. **Why:** This is Chamber of Commerce v Whiting with a slight variation. IRCA's preemption clause expressly saves "licensing and similar laws." An E-Verify mandate tied to continuing employer licensure falls within that savings provision. The state rule cooperates with, rather than displaces, the federal scheme. Outcome: not preempted.

4. **Hypo:** A State enacts a tort statute allowing residents to sue private parties for personal injury caused by defective medical devices. The devices are regulated by the FDA under a federal statute with a preemption clause covering "any requirement different from, or in addition to, any requirement under this chapter" applicable to the devices. The state tort claim asserts that the manufacturer failed to warn physicians about a known risk, a warning that is not required by any FDA regulation. **Why:** The "different from or in addition to" clause and the relationship between state-law common law claims and federal regulatory requirements present a close scope question (and the Riegel v Medtronic line of cases is relevant). Whether a failure-to-warn theory survives depends on whether the state-law warning obligation is "parallel" to federal requirements (generally not preempted) or imposes a new or different requirement (generally preempted). On these facts, because no parallel federal warning requirement exists, the state-law theory imposes an "additional" requirement and is preempted. Outcome: preempted on narrower express-clause reasoning rather than field or obstacle preemption.

### Fence-Sitter

5. **Hypo:** A State enacts a sanctuary-jurisdiction statute directing state and local law enforcement not to expend resources on immigration detainers issued by federal Immigration and Customs Enforcement, not to share information about detainees' immigration status beyond what federal law requires, and not to grant federal officers access to state detention facilities absent a judicial warrant. A federal statute (8 U.S.C. 1373) bars state and local governments from restricting federal-state communication regarding immigration status. The federal government sues on preemption grounds; the State defends on anti-commandeering grounds. **Why:** The hypothetical deliberately pits preemption against anti-commandeering (developed in [[Lectures/Commandeering - Anti-Commandeering Doctrine|Commandeering lecture]]). The sanctuary statute's information-sharing restriction arguably conflicts with 8 U.S.C. 1373 on its face (possible express or obstacle preemption). But anti-commandeering doctrine (Printz v United States; Murphy v NCAA) forbids Congress from conscripting state legislative or executive machinery to enforce federal law. If 8 U.S.C. 1373 is read as forbidding the State from restricting its own agents from cooperating with federal enforcement, it may itself be unconstitutional commandeering. The detainer-non-compliance and facility-access provisions raise the same tension. The outcome depends on whether courts read 8 U.S.C. 1373 narrowly to preserve constitutional avoidance (no commandeering, no preemption conflict) or broadly (preemption applies but the federal statute is invalid under anti-commandeering, leaving no preemption). Genuinely contested; reasonable courts can come out either way, and the Supreme Court has not yet resolved the issue squarely.

## How to Spot on an Exam

Preemption issues appear when a state law regulates conduct that is also addressed by federal law or that operates in a federally regulated domain. Work through the preemption tree systematically:

First, identify the federal statute. The Supremacy Clause operates only on a valid federal statute. Verify that the federal law is within an enumerated Article I power (usually the Commerce Clause or, in immigration, the implicit plenary federal authority). If no federal statute is in play, the question is not preemption; it may be the dormant Commerce Clause or a direct constitutional challenge.

Second, check for an express preemption clause. If one exists, the question is scope. Parse the language carefully. "Relating to" sweeps very broadly (ERISA). "With respect to" is narrower (FCLAA). "Any requirement different from or in addition to" reaches parallel but distinct state obligations. Savings clauses ("licensing and similar laws" in IRCA) preserve state authority within named categories.

Third, analyze field preemption. Has Congress legislated so comprehensively that it has occupied the entire field? Look for a pervasive federal scheme, a dominant federal interest, historical federal occupation, or a structural need for national uniformity. Immigration, foreign affairs, maritime, and national banking are classical field-preempted domains. Product safety and drug regulation are partially field-preempted where FDA or similar regulatory schemes are pervasive.

Fourth, analyze conflict preemption. Start with impossibility (rare). Move to obstacle preemption: does the state law stand as an obstacle to the accomplishment of federal objectives? Identify the congressional objective with care and ask whether the state rule frustrates it or merely supplements it. Arizona Sections 5(C) and 6 are the template: IRCA's worker-versus-employer allocation and the centralized federal enforcement scheme are the identified objectives; Arizona's criminal penalty and unilateral arrest authority are the obstacles.

Fifth, consider the presumption against preemption. Is the state law operating in an area of traditional state regulation (family law, general tort, health and safety)? If yes, the presumption operates and favors narrow readings of ambiguous preemption clauses. If no (foreign affairs, immigration, maritime, national banking), the presumption is weak or absent and does not rescue the state law.

Sixth, consider as-applied preservation. Even if a facial preemption challenge fails, as-applied challenges may remain on a developed record (Arizona Section 2(B)). Flag this possibility in fence-sitter hypotheticals where the state law may be saved facially but could fail in practice.

Analytical trigger facts: a state regulates conduct also addressed by a federal statute; a state law enters a domain of pervasive federal regulation; a state enforcement scheme duplicates or conflicts with a federal enforcement scheme; a state tort claim relates to a federally regulated product or program.

## Critique

Preemption doctrine is the most frequently litigated application of the Supremacy Clause and attracts criticism from both structural and interpretive directions.

A textualist critique, associated most prominently with Justice Thomas, targets obstacle preemption. The doctrine requires courts to identify "the full purposes and objectives of Congress" and then ask whether state law stands as an obstacle. Both steps are inherently purposive and atextual. Different judges will read different objectives into the same statute and will assess obstruction at different levels of generality. The result is inter-judge variation that is doctrinally ungrounded. Thomas would confine preemption to express clauses and to impossibility, eliminating the field and obstacle branches as judicial inventions. The critique draws force from the asymmetry between the clear rule of the Supremacy Clause (state law yields to conflicting federal law) and the indeterminate rules by which courts decide what counts as a conflict.

A federalism critique runs the other direction. Preemption is an affirmative displacement of state authority whose textual warrant is thin beyond actual statutory conflict. The more state authority federal courts displace through field and obstacle preemption, the more the structural role of state sovereignty shrinks. The critique is doctrinally unified with anti-commandeering: both doctrines police the federal-state boundary, but preemption is sometimes aggressive in ways that anti-commandeering is not. A robust presumption against preemption would restore the balance by forcing Congress to speak clearly when it intends to displace state authority. Chandler's view, that the presumption operates only weakly as a tiebreaker, suggests the current doctrinal setting tilts toward federal displacement at the margins.

A third critique is institutional. Preemption doctrine has grown up around federal regulatory schemes in which the agency's interpretation of the preemption clause receives judicial deference. The administrative state's role in preemption is significant: agency views, preambles to regulations, and statements of policy often drive preemption outcomes even without textual support in the statute. Critics argue this converts preemption from a judicial interpretation of Congress's work into an agency expansion of executive-branch authority. The debate intersects with the post-Loper Bright contraction of agency deference: if courts no longer defer to agency interpretations of statutory preemption scope, the doctrine may drift toward narrower preemption readings over time.

A fourth critique, doctrinally specific, focuses on immigration. Arizona's "broad, undoubted power over the subject of immigration" framing has been questioned as insufficiently attentive to state interests in public safety and workforce integrity. Justice Scalia's dissent in Arizona rests on the view that states retain inherent sovereign authority over immigration enforcement within their borders as a matter of original constitutional design. The majority's federal-primacy framing has structural consequences beyond immigration: it suggests that where federal authority has historically been plenary, field preemption operates with little friction and the presumption against preemption has minimal force. Defenders respond that the framing is correct precisely because the federal interest in a uniform national immigration regime is structural and distinct from domains where states traditionally exercise regulatory authority.

A fifth critique is interpretive rather than structural. The breadth of ERISA's "relating to" clause and similar broad preemption clauses produces outcomes that look arbitrary from the plaintiff's perspective. A state bad-faith tort claim for insurer misconduct in processing an employee benefit claim is preempted and removed to federal court, where ERISA's limited remedies then bar the plaintiff's extra-contractual damages. The substantive contraction of remedies is real, and the critique is that Congress, in enacting broad preemption to support uniform federal administration of benefit plans, did not adequately consider the substantive remedial vacuum the preemption created. The same interpretive critique recurs in the medical-device context (Riegel v Medtronic) and in cigarette labeling (Cipollone v Liggett).

## Connections

- [[Topics/Federalism|Federalism]] is the structural umbrella. Preemption is the affirmative federal-over-state doctrine, exercising the Supremacy Clause's directive that federal law displaces conflicting state law. It sits alongside the dormant Commerce Clause, anti-commandeering, and state sovereign immunity as the principal structural federalism doctrines.
- [[Topics/Commerce Clause|Commerce Clause]] is the power source. Most preempting statutes are enacted under the Commerce Clause (the ERISA, FCLAA, rail-safety, food-regulation, and consumer-disclosure hypotheticals all rely on the commerce grant). Before a preemption analysis can proceed, the federal statute must fall within an enumerated Article I power. [[Cases/Gibbons v Ogden (1824)|Gibbons v Ogden]] (1824) is the original Supremacy Clause preemption case, preempting New York's steamboat monopoly as applied to a federally licensed interstate carrier.
- [[Topics/Dormant Commerce Clause|Dormant Commerce Clause]] is the doctrinal cousin. The DCC is a background default about what Congress would preempt absent legislation; preemption requires an actual federal statute. Chandler's meta-statute framing makes the distinction explicit: Congress can override the DCC default through affirmative authorization (McCarran-Ferguson, Webb-Kenyon), which confirms the DCC's status as a judge-made default rather than a preemption rule.
- [[Lectures/Commandeering - Anti-Commandeering Doctrine|Anti-Commandeering Doctrine]] is the structural complement. Preemption tells States their law is displaced; anti-commandeering tells Congress it cannot conscript state legislative or executive machinery to enforce federal law. Both doctrines police the federal-state boundary but from opposite directions. The sanctuary-jurisdiction fence-sitter hypothetical illustrates how the two doctrines can collide.
- [[Topics/Executive Power|Executive Power]] enters preemption doctrine indirectly. Preemption clauses are often drafted against a background of federal administrative enforcement (FDA, OSHA, FAA, ICE). Agency interpretations of preemption scope, though less deferred to after Loper Bright, shape doctrinal outcomes. The immigration-preemption cluster (Arizona, Whiting, Hines) reflects the plenary federal executive role in immigration enforcement.
- [[Cases/Arizona v United States (2012)|Arizona v United States (2012)]] is the anchor modern case. Four provisions of Arizona's SB 1070 run the full preemption tree: Section 3 (field), Sections 5(C) and 6 (obstacle), and Section 2(B) (facial challenge denied, as-applied preserved). Kennedy's majority is the definitive modern statement of all three branches applied in a single opinion.
- [[Cases/Gibbons v Ogden (1824)|Gibbons v Ogden (1824)]] is the historical predecessor. Marshall's preemption holding (federal coasting license preempts conflicting state monopoly grant under the Supremacy Clause) establishes the basic structural move that every modern preemption case extends.
- [[Lectures/Federal Preemption and the Supremacy Clause|Federal Preemption and the Supremacy Clause]] is the primary classroom development. It articulates Chandler's three-branch tree, the meta-statute framing, the calibration of the presumption against preemption, and the hypothetical-driven approach to practical preemption analysis.
