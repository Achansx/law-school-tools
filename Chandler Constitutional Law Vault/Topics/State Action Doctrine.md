# State Action Doctrine

## Overview

The state action doctrine is the threshold limitation on most constitutional claims: the Fourteenth Amendment, and by textual inference the Fifteenth, operate only against the states, not against private persons. Before a court can ask whether conduct violates the Equal Protection Clause, the Due Process Clause, or any other guarantee in those Amendments, it must first ask whether the challenged conduct is fairly attributable to the state. If not, the Constitution is silent, and the claimant must find a statutory or common-law source of regulation.

Three structural consequences follow. First, Congress's Section 5 enforcement power is coextensive with the amendment it enforces: because the Fourteenth Amendment reaches only state action, Section 5 reaches only state action. Second, the Thirteenth Amendment is the one Reconstruction Amendment without a state-action limit. Its Section 2 enforcement power has been read to reach private conduct that constitutes a "badge or incident of slavery," which is why Reconstruction-era civil rights statutes rest on the Thirteenth Amendment when the Fourteenth will not carry them. Third, when Section 5 will not reach private actors, Congress can and does reach them through the Commerce Clause and the Spending Clause. The Civil Rights Act of 1964 is the paradigm: what the 1875 Act could not accomplish under the Fourteenth Amendment, the 1964 Act accomplished under Article I.

---

## Governing Rule

> **Rule.** The Fourteenth Amendment's guarantees operate only against state action. Private conduct is fairly attributable to the state when (1) a state court or officer enforces the private arrangement (Shelley v Kraemer); (2) the private actor performs a function that has been traditionally and exclusively governmental (Marsh v Alabama); (3) the state and the private actor are so entwined or symbiotically bound that the private conduct is effectively the state's (Burton v Wilmington Parking Authority, Brentwood Academy); or (4) the state created the entity and maintains continuing control such that its formal private label cannot disguise its governmental character (Lebron v Amtrak). Generic state funding or regulation does not suffice; the plaintiff must connect the state to the specific challenged decision (Rendell-Baker v Kohn; Blum v Yaretsky). Governmental inaction in the face of private harm is not state action (DeShaney v Winnebago County). Congress may reach private conduct the Fourteenth Amendment cannot by invoking the Thirteenth Amendment (Jones v Alfred H. Mayer Co.; Griffin v Breckenridge), the Commerce Clause, or the Spending Clause.

## Doctrinal Development

### United States v Cruikshank (1876) — Setting the Narrow Baseline

Cruikshank grew out of the Colfax massacre, one of the most violent acts of political terror in American history. Federal prosecutors charged the perpetrators under the Enforcement Act of 1870 with conspiring to deprive the victims of federal rights. The Court held that the First Amendment right of assembly and the Second Amendment right to bear arms are not rights granted by the Constitution that the federal government may protect against private interference, and that the Fourteenth Amendment "adds nothing to the rights of one citizen against another." A federal indictment alleging a private conspiracy could not rest on generic statutory language tethered to rights the federal government had no authority to guarantee against private persons. See [[Cases/United States v Cruikshank (1876)|full brief]].

Cruikshank is the pre-1883 structural move that makes Civil Rights Cases possible. The Fourteenth Amendment is narrowed to state-directed guarantees; the Bill of Rights is still unincorporated; and private violence designed to nullify the outcome of the Civil War is left to state criminal law, which in the Reconstruction South was unwilling to prosecute.

### The Civil Rights Cases (1883) — The Classic Statement

The Civil Rights Act of 1875 prohibited racial discrimination in "inns, public conveyances on land or water, theatres, and other places of public amusement." Multiple prosecutions of private businesses consolidated into the Civil Rights Cases. Justice Bradley's majority held that the Fourteenth Amendment is "prohibitory upon the States" and that "individual invasion of individual rights" lies outside its scope. Section 5 empowers Congress only to enact "corrective" legislation directed at prohibited state action; it does not license a general federal police power over private conduct. On the Thirteenth Amendment, Bradley conceded Congress might reach "badges and incidents of slavery," but concluded that race-based exclusion from inns and theaters was a "social right" rather than a badge of slavery. See [[Cases/Civil Rights Cases (1883)|full brief]].

Two features of the opinion control the modern doctrine. First, Bradley's phrase — law must be "adapted to the mischief and wrong which the amendment was intended to provide against" — foreshadows the congruence-and-proportionality test the Court would formalize in [[Cases/City of Boerne v Flores (1997)|City of Boerne]]. Second, Harlan's solitary dissent — the Thirteenth Amendment reaches the "badges of servitude" inflicted by segregation — was revived, on the Thirteenth Amendment half of the analysis, eighty-five years later in Jones v Alfred H. Mayer Co.

### Jones v Alfred H. Mayer Co. (1968) — The Thirteenth Amendment Bypass

42 U.S.C. section 1982, a Reconstruction-era statute originating in the Civil Rights Act of 1866, bars racial discrimination in the sale or rental of property. The Joneses, a Black couple, were refused a home in a St. Louis subdivision; they sued the private developer. The Court held that section 1982 reaches private conduct and that Congress had authority to enact it under Section 2 of the Thirteenth Amendment. Congress "has the power under the Thirteenth Amendment rationally to determine what are the badges and incidents of slavery, and the authority to translate that determination into effective legislation." See [[Cases/Jones v Alfred H Mayer Co (1968)|full brief]].

Jones is the doctrinal escape valve the Civil Rights Cases left open. Where the Fourteenth Amendment's state-action limit blocks a federal remedy against private racial discrimination, the Thirteenth Amendment's Section 2 power to eliminate badges and incidents of slavery may supply one. The limit: the connection to slavery (or to the badges tradition has long associated with it) must be rational, not merely asserted.

### Griffin v Breckenridge (1971) — Section 1985(3) Against Private Conspiracies

Black Mississippi travelers were attacked by a group of white men who mistook them for civil rights workers. They sued under 42 U.S.C. section 1985(3), the civil conspiracy provision of the 1871 Ku Klux Klan Act. The Court held that section 1985(3) reaches purely private conspiracies and does not require state action, so long as the conspiracy is motivated by "some racial, or perhaps otherwise class-based, invidiously discriminatory animus." The statute was a valid exercise of the Thirteenth Amendment's Section 2 enforcement power (for racial conspiracies) and of Congress's implied power to protect the right of interstate travel. See [[Cases/Griffin v Breckenridge (1971)|full brief]].

Griffin generalizes Jones. Private conduct that nullifies civil rights or burdens travel can be reached by federal statute when Congress proceeds under a constitutional source that does not itself carry a state-action limit. The class-based-animus requirement is the doctrinal fence that keeps section 1985(3) from becoming a general federal tort.

### United States v Morrison (2000) — The Modern Reaffirmation

Section 13981 of the Violence Against Women Act created a civil remedy for victims of gender-motivated violence. Christy Brzonkala sued Virginia Tech football players who she alleged had raped her. The Court struck the provision on two independent grounds. Under the Commerce Clause, gender-motivated violence is noneconomic conduct whose aggregate effects cannot justify federal regulation after Lopez. Under Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment, the remedy fails because "the Fourteenth Amendment, by its very terms, prohibits only state action," and Section 5 cannot reach purely private actors. Rehnquist cited the Civil Rights Cases as the controlling authority. See [[Cases/United States v Morrison (2000)|full brief]].

Morrison is the proof that the Civil Rights Cases are still good law on the state-action point. More than a century after 1883 and in the face of sustained academic criticism, the Court reaffirmed that Congress's Section 5 power cannot create direct federal remedies against private actors. Any such remedy must travel through the Commerce Clause, the Spending Clause, or the Thirteenth Amendment — and Morrison shows that even the Commerce Clause has limits.

### The Five Pathways to State Action (Lecture)

The March 12, 2026 class recap organizes the private-conduct-as-state-action case law around five pathways: enforcement (Shelley v Kraemer), public function traditionally and exclusively governmental (Marsh v Alabama; narrowed in Lloyd Corp v Tanner and Manhattan Community Access Corp v Halleck), entwinement and symbiosis (Burton v Wilmington Parking Authority; Brentwood Academy), government-created entity with continuing control (Lebron v Amtrak), and the inaction non-pathway (DeShaney v Winnebago County). The lecture also organizes the Warren-Court-expansion-to-Burger/Rehnquist-contraction arc: the Warren Court pushed the doctrine outward through Shelley, Marsh, and Burton; the Burger and Rehnquist Courts pulled it back through Lloyd, Rendell-Baker, Blum, and Halleck. See [[Lectures/The State Action Doctrine - Class Recap|full lecture]].

The Warren-to-Rehnquist contraction is the structural story the exam wants. A fact pattern that looks like Burton in 1961 (a private restaurant in a municipally owned garage) would probably come out the other way in 2019 under Halleck's demand that the private actor perform a "traditional and exclusive" government function or be directly entwined with specific challenged conduct.

## Comparison: The Four Constitutional Routes Around the State Action Limit

| Route | Source | Reaches private actors? | Canonical statute | Example case | Limit |
|-------|--------|------------------------|-------------------|--------------|-------|
| Fourteenth Amendment Section 5 | 14th Am. § 5 | No | Civil Rights Act of 1875 (struck down) | Civil Rights Cases 1883; Morrison 2000 | State action required; congruent-and-proportional to state violations |
| Thirteenth Amendment Section 2 | 13th Am. § 2 | Yes | Civil Rights Act of 1866 (section 1982); 42 U.S.C. § 1985(3) | Jones 1968; Griffin 1971 | Rational connection to a "badge or incident of slavery" |
| Commerce Clause | Art. I § 8 cl. 3 | Yes | Civil Rights Act of 1964 Title II | Heart of Atlanta Motel 1964 | Economic activity or substantial effects; Lopez/Morrison limit |
| Spending Clause | Art. I § 8 cl. 1 | Yes (conditionally) | Title VI; Title IX | South Dakota v Dole framework | Germane conditions, not unduly coercive (see [[Cases/South Dakota v Dole (1987)\|Dole]]; [[Cases/NFIB v Sebelius (2012)\|NFIB]]) |

The table makes the structural point the lecture calls "No State Action ≠ No Law": the Section 5 route is blocked for private conduct, but Congress has three other constitutional vehicles to the same destination. The exam-trap move is to assume that failing the Fourteenth Amendment threshold ends the analysis.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side

1. **Hypo:** A state hires a private contractor to run the intake desk of a public unemployment office. A claimant alleges the contractor discriminated against her on the basis of race. **Why:** State action. The function — administering a government benefits program — is classically governmental, and the state's continuing authority to direct intake procedures makes the contractor's discrimination fairly attributable to the state under the entwinement/government-control pathway (Lebron-style). The Fourteenth Amendment claim proceeds.

2. **Hypo:** A state court issues an injunction ordering a white homeowner's neighbor to comply with a racially restrictive deed covenant by blocking a Black family's purchase. **Why:** State action under Shelley v Kraemer. The covenant is private, but the state's coercive machinery — courts and the recording system — performs the discriminatory exclusion. The Fourteenth Amendment Equal Protection claim proceeds against the state court's enforcement.

### Opposite-Side

3. **Hypo:** A privately owned and operated online platform, receiving no government funding and subject to no state regulation specific to its content moderation, bans users whose political views it dislikes. Banned users sue the platform under the First Amendment. **Why:** No state action. Content moderation on a private platform is neither a traditional and exclusive government function (Halleck narrows the "public forum" theory) nor the product of state entwinement or government creation. Plaintiffs must find a statutory vehicle — but none obviously exists on the Commerce or Spending Clause.

4. **Hypo:** A private hospital that receives 90 percent of its revenue from Medicare fires a nurse who blew the whistle on patient-safety lapses. The nurse sues under the Due Process Clause. **Why:** No state action. Under Rendell-Baker and Blum, extensive government funding does not transform the hospital into a state actor unless the state was connected to the specific termination decision. The nurse must find a state or federal whistleblower statute.

### Fence-Sitter

5. **Hypo:** A city creates a nonprofit "Innovation Partnership" to run a technology incubator. The nonprofit's board is appointed by the mayor, its facilities are leased from the city at a nominal rate, and its operating budget is substantially funded by a recurring city grant. The incubator excludes a startup because of its founder's political activity. **What makes this hard:** Lebron (government-created entity with continuing control) and Burton (symbiotic entwinement) both cut toward state action. Rendell-Baker and Blum cut the other way: city funding and facility use do not alone establish state action absent connection to the specific exclusion decision. The resolution turns on how tightly the city's appointment and grant mechanisms constrain the specific decision to exclude. If the mayor's office routinely reviews membership, state action is likely; if the board acts with genuine independence, Rendell-Baker/Blum controls.

## How to Spot on an Exam

State action is almost always a threshold question on a Con Law I essay fact pattern involving any kind of injury. The structural moves are:

1. **Identify who the defendant is.** If the defendant is a state, a municipality, or a government officer, state action is ordinarily conceded — skip to the merits. If the defendant is a private person or entity, state action is the gate.
2. **Run the five pathways.** Enforcement (Shelley), public function traditionally and exclusively governmental (Marsh, narrowed in Lloyd and Halleck), entwinement and symbiosis (Burton, Brentwood), government-created entity with continuing control (Lebron), and the inaction non-pathway (DeShaney). The "traditionally and exclusively" adverb is doing most of the work; functions a private party has historically performed do not pass.
3. **Connect to the specific challenged conduct.** Rendell-Baker and Blum are the trap cases for unwary students: generic funding and regulation are not enough. The state must be fairly responsible for the specific decision.
4. **If no state action, ask which non-Section-5 route reaches the conduct.** Thirteenth Amendment Section 2 (Jones, Griffin) for racially motivated private conduct with a rational connection to slavery's badges. Commerce Clause (Heart of Atlanta) for economic or substantial-effects activity. Spending Clause conditioned on federal funding. This is the "No State Action ≠ No Law" exam trap: do not stop analyzing when the Fourteenth Amendment fails.
5. **The Section 5 trap.** If Congress has invoked Section 5 to regulate private conduct directly, the statute is unconstitutional as applied to private actors (Morrison). Read the statute's constitutional hook carefully; Reconstruction-era civil rights statutes grounded in the Thirteenth Amendment (section 1982, section 1985(3) for racial conspiracies) survive where their Fourteenth Amendment cousins would not.

## Critique

The state action doctrine is one of the Court's oldest doctrinal choices and also one of its most critiqued. The progressive critique, going back at least to Charles Black's 1967 "Foreword: State Action, Equal Protection, and California's Proposition 14," argues that the doctrine is internally incoherent — every private act is performed against a background of property, contract, and enforcement rules ultimately supplied by the state, so the state is always "implicated" in private conduct in some sense. Erwin Chemerinsky and Laurence Tribe have pressed the same structural point: the "private/public" distinction the doctrine enforces is a legal construct, not a natural category, and its enforcement systematically under-protects marginalized groups against private actors whose power is unchecked by state constitutional limits.

The originalist/textualist defense answers that the Fourteenth Amendment's text is explicit: "No State shall . . ." The Amendment addresses the states because the Reconstruction Congress addressed the states, having just fought a war against state secession and confronting discriminatory state black codes. Private conduct is the domain of state tort, contract, and criminal law; the Amendment's drafters did not aim to federalize private wrongs. This is the Civil Rights Cases reading Morrison reaffirmed, and it has structural federalism payoffs: without the state action limit, every private tort becomes a potential federal constitutional claim.

The doctrinal-pragmatic critique accepts both of the above and focuses on the five-pathway architecture. The "traditional and exclusive" government-function test is so narrow (after Halleck) that it screens out almost everything. The Rendell-Baker/Blum demand for state involvement in the specific decision collapses the entwinement test into a Sixth-Circuit-hard-to-meet standard. Brentwood Academy's entwinement theory is almost never satisfied outside the unusual facts of an athletic association staffed by public-school officials. The result is that the doctrine's "pathways" promise access that the modern cases systematically withhold, producing a doctrine whose formal categories overpromise and whose practical operation underdelivers.

A fourth line, associated with Charles Black and Reva Siegel, emphasizes the Thirteenth Amendment workaround. The Reconstruction Congress in 1866 wrote a statute (section 1982) that reached private property transactions on a Thirteenth Amendment theory; Jones in 1968 revived it. That lineage suggests the Civil Rights Cases got the Thirteenth Amendment half wrong — segregation in public accommodations was a badge of slavery — even if the Fourteenth Amendment half was defensibly narrow. On an exam, this is the structural move that lets the doctrinal-pragmatic critic and the originalist share a floor: the state action doctrine as currently administered is workable if Congress is paying attention and invokes the right constitutional hook.
