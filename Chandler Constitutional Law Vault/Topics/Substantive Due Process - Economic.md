# Substantive Due Process - Economic

## Overview

Economic substantive due process is the doctrinal arc in which the Fourteenth Amendment Due Process Clause was used, between roughly 1897 and 1937, as a vehicle to invalidate state economic regulation on the ground that it interfered with an unenumerated liberty of contract. The arc has a pre-history in the Vested-Rights tradition (Calder v Bull (1798), Marshall-era takings cases), a peak in Lochner v New York (1905), a same-term counterpoint in Jacobson v Massachusetts (1905), an overruling in West Coast Hotel Co v Parrish (1937), a re-articulation of the post-1937 framework in United States v Carolene Products (1938), and an endpoint in Williamson v Lee Optical of Oklahoma (1955). After 1937, economic regulation gets rational-basis review at its most deferential, which in practice means the regulation always survives. The one remaining live pocket is the punitive-damages line, in which BMW of North America Inc v Gore (1996) treats grossly excessive punitive awards as an independent due-process violation; whether that line is best read as residual economic SDP or as procedural due process with a substantive overlay remains contested.

This Topic is one of three sister Topics covering substantive due process. [[Topics/Substantive Due Process|Substantive Due Process]] (the parent / incorporation Topic) treats the Bill-of-Rights-against-the-States lineage. [[Topics/Substantive Due Process - Privacy and Autonomy|Substantive Due Process - Privacy and Autonomy]] treats the Meyer / Griswold / Loving / Roe / Casey / Lawrence / Obergefell / Dobbs lineage. The three arcs share the same textual hook (the word "liberty" in the Due Process Clause) and the same historical objection (that "substantive due process is a contradiction in terms"), but they diverge sharply on the live-doctrine question: incorporation is a stable, near-unanimous business; economic SDP is dead letter except in the punitive-damages corner; privacy SDP is the most volatile of the three after Dobbs (2022).

---

## Governing Rule

> **Pre-1937 rule (Lochner era):** A state economic regulation is valid only when it bears a "real and substantial relation" to a legitimate police-power objective and is not "arbitrary." Liberty of contract is a presumed unenumerated right under the Fourteenth Amendment; the state bears the burden of justifying the intrusion. Lochner v New York (1905). The same-term counterpoint, Jacobson v Massachusetts (1905), accepts a police-power justification on its face absent a "plain, palpable invasion" — the gap between Lochner and Jacobson is a function of how searching the Court chose to be, not of any formal doctrinal difference.

> **Post-1937 rule (modern doctrine):** Economic regulation receives rational-basis review at its most deferential. A statute is constitutional if there is any "conceivable basis" for the legislative judgment, however far-fetched. West Coast Hotel Co v Parrish (1937); Williamson v Lee Optical of Oklahoma (1955). Heightened review under the Due Process Clause is reserved for the three categories named in Carolene Products' Footnote Four: enumerated rights, restrictions on the political process, and discrete and insular minorities. Economic liberty is none of these.

> **Punitive-damages exception:** A grossly excessive punitive damages award is itself a Fourteenth Amendment Due Process violation. The three guideposts from BMW of North America Inc v Gore (1996) — degree of reprehensibility, ratio between punitive and compensatory damages, and comparison to civil and criminal sanctions for comparable conduct — supply substantive constitutional limits on tort awards. This is the only live economic-liberty pocket post-1937.

## Doctrinal Development

The arc moves in four stations: pre-history, peak, repudiation, and endpoint.

### Pre-history: Vested rights and Calder v Bull (1798)

Before the Fourteenth Amendment existed, Calder v Bull (1798) supplied the Vested-Rights idiom that economic SDP would later inherit. Chase's seriatim opinion held that legislative acts "contrary to the great first principles of the social compact" exceed legislative authority even without a textual constitutional anchor. Iredell countered that judges should not strike legislation on natural-law grounds absent a constitutional text. The Chase-Iredell exchange becomes the doctrinal template for the entire SDP critique: the question of whether the Court can read substantive limits into open-textured constitutional language without a textual hook.

### Peak: Lochner and Jacobson (1905)

Lochner v New York (1905) struck down New York's Bakeshop Act, which capped bakery employees at 60 hours per week, as an unconstitutional interference with liberty of contract under the Fourteenth Amendment. Peckham's majority refused to defer to the state's stated public-health rationale and read the legislative record skeptically, treating the labor-law justification as a pretext for paternalism. The Lochner-era project was searching judicial review of economic regulation under a presumption favoring liberty of contract.

Jacobson v Massachusetts (1905), decided the same Term, upheld a compulsory smallpox vaccination statute against a Fourteenth Amendment challenge. Harlan's majority articulated a deferential police-power test: a regulation survives unless it is a "plain, palpable invasion" of rights, and the state need only show a "real or substantial relation" to public health. The two cases sit awkwardly together. The doctrinal gap between them is not formal; it is interpretive. Both opinions claim to apply the same Fourteenth Amendment liberty inquiry, but Lochner does so with searching scrutiny while Jacobson does so with rational-basis-in-substance deference.

### Repudiation: West Coast Hotel and Carolene Products (1937-1938)

West Coast Hotel Co v Parrish (1937) ended the Lochner era. Hughes's 5-4 majority upheld Washington State's minimum wage for women, explicitly overruling Adkins v Children's Hospital (1923). The opinion redefined "liberty" not as freedom from regulation but as "liberty within a social organization" subject to reasonable regulation in the public interest. Hughes credited Depression-era findings about unequal bargaining power as grounds for protective legislation, foreclosing the Lochner-style move of treating such findings as pretextual.

United States v Carolene Products (1938) supplied the post-1937 framework. Stone's majority upheld the federal Filled Milk Act on rational-basis grounds — any "rational basis" supplied by the legislative record suffices. Footnote Four then carved out three categories that get heightened review: enumerated rights, laws restricting the political process, and laws targeting "discrete and insular minorities." Economic liberty is none of the three. After Carolene Products, the operating rule is two-track: rational basis for economic regulation, strict scrutiny for the Footnote Four categories.

### Endpoint: Williamson and BMW v Gore (1955-1996)

Williamson v Lee Optical of Oklahoma (1955) marks the doctrinal endpoint. Douglas's unanimous opinion upheld an Oklahoma statute restricting lens-fitting to licensed optometrists under a deference standard so forgiving that "it is enough that there is an evil at hand for correction." Williamson's "conceivable basis" formulation makes economic SDP effectively dead letter: any imaginable legislative purpose, however thin or unsubstantiated, suffices. The opinion was not formally an overruling of anything; it just operated within the post-1937 settlement and showed how little work the Due Process Clause now does on economic regulation.

BMW of North America Inc v Gore (1996) is the surviving exception. Stevens's majority struck down a $2 million punitive damages award against BMW as an Alabama dealer's failure to disclose a $4,000 repaint, on the ground that grossly excessive punitive awards violate the Due Process Clause. The three guideposts (reprehensibility, ratio, and statutory-comparison) are themselves substantive, but the doctrinal hook is procedural-due-process notice — defendants are entitled to fair notice of the magnitude of penalty they face. Whether BMW v Gore is best classified as a residual economic-SDP pocket or as procedural due process is contested; the case is generally taught as the former because the substantive guideposts do real work that no notice-only theory could.

## Comparison: Lochner-Era Searching Review vs Post-1937 Rational-Basis Deference

| Axis | Lochner-era (1897-1937) | Post-1937 settlement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Standard of review | Searching review under "real and substantial relation"; presumption favoring liberty of contract | Rational basis at its most deferential ("conceivable basis"); presumption favoring legislation |
| Treatment of legislative findings | Refused: legislature's stated rationale read skeptically and often dismissed as pretext (Lochner) | Credited: any imaginable legislative purpose suffices, even without record support (Williamson) |
| Liberty baseline | Freedom of contract as a core unenumerated liberty against state interference | "Liberty within a social organization" subject to reasonable regulation in the public interest (West Coast Hotel) |
| Footnote Four carve-outs | None — all rights claims subject to the same Lochner template | Three categories get heightened review: enumerated rights, political-process restrictions, discrete-and-insular minorities (Carolene Products) |
| Live work today | Dead letter except as a negative symbol ("Lochnerizing" as judicial overreach) | Economic regulation virtually unreviewable under DP; Footnote Four does the real work for non-economic claims |
| Same-term counterpoint | Jacobson v Massachusetts (1905) — police-power deference applied to public health, foreshadowing the 1937 settlement | West Coast Hotel itself (1937) — the doctrinal hinge that ratifies the deference Jacobson had already practiced |

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side

**1. State licensing scheme for hair-braiders.** A state requires anyone wishing to braid hair commercially to obtain a 1,500-hour cosmetology license costing $15,000, even though no part of the cosmetology curriculum covers hair-braiding. A hair-braider sues, arguing the licensing scheme deprives her of liberty without due process. Under Williamson, the state needs only a "conceivable basis." Public-health justifications about sanitary salon practice will satisfy the test, however thin the connection to the actual conduct of braiding. The braider loses on substantive due process. (She might fare better under Article IV Privileges and Immunities or under federal antitrust if interstate commerce is involved, but those are different doctrines.)

**2. State minimum wage for restaurant workers.** A state raises its minimum wage from $7.25 to $15. A restaurant association sues, citing Lochner and arguing the minimum wage interferes with liberty of contract. Under West Coast Hotel and post-1937 doctrine, the claim is a non-starter: liberty of contract is no longer a constitutionally protected economic right against general regulation, and the state's interest in adequate wages plainly satisfies rational basis. Restaurant association loses.

### Opposite-Side

**3. State imposes punitive damages 1,000-times-compensatory.** A state court awards $1 million in compensatory damages and $1 billion in punitive damages against a defendant for a consumer-protection violation. The defendant raises a BMW v Gore due-process challenge. Under the three guideposts, a 1,000-to-1 ratio is presumptively excessive (the BMW Court flagged ratios above single-digit multipliers as constitutionally suspect), and unless the conduct is at the extreme of the reprehensibility spectrum and dwarfs comparable civil and criminal sanctions, the award is unconstitutional. Defendant wins on due process; the trial court must remit the award.

**4. State requires vaccination of all schoolchildren.** A parent challenges a state's mandatory-vaccination law for K-12 students under substantive due process, arguing it interferes with parental medical decision-making. Jacobson is directly on point: compulsory vaccination is the canonical police-power exercise, and the Court has never disturbed Jacobson's basic holding. The parent loses on the SDP claim. (A free-exercise claim, an EP claim, or a state-law religious-exemption claim are different questions.)

### Fence-Sitter

**5. State caps physician-charged surgical fees at 60% of Medicare reimbursement.** A state, attempting to control runaway medical costs, caps physician fees for elective surgery at 60% of the Medicare reimbursement rate. Surgeons sue, arguing the cap interferes with liberty of contract and with the practice of a learned profession in ways that Williamson's deference cannot save. The honest answer is that this is hard. Under post-1937 doctrine the cap probably survives — rational basis is forgiving, and cost-control is plainly legitimate — but the surgeons may invoke BMW v Gore by analogy (substantive due-process-style review of disproportionate state-imposed economic consequences) and may argue the cap, like the Lochner-era statutes, is paternalistic toward sophisticated bargainers. A court is more likely to apply Williamson and uphold than to revive Lochner, but the exam answer is the recognition that this is exactly the kind of fact pattern where the live-vs-dead-letter status of economic SDP becomes contestable.

## How to Spot on an Exam

Watch for three triggers. First, anything that smells like Lochner — a state economic regulation challenged on liberty-of-contract or freedom-of-occupation grounds. Apply the post-1937 settlement: rational basis at its most deferential; Williamson's "conceivable basis" formulation; West Coast Hotel and Carolene Products as the doctrinal hinge. The plaintiff almost always loses.

Second, a punitive damages award that looks grossly excessive — single-incident misconduct yielding seven-figure or eight-figure punitive damages on five-figure or six-figure compensatory damages. Apply the three BMW v Gore guideposts: reprehensibility, ratio, and statutory comparison. Note that BMW v Gore is the live pocket of economic SDP and that the three guideposts are themselves substantive even though the doctrinal hook is procedural-due-process notice.

Third, a fact pattern that bait-tests you with the phrase "substantive due process" or "liberty of contract" without obviously fitting either the Lochner template or the Privacy/Autonomy template. Flag that you recognize three different SDP arcs (incorporation, economic, privacy) and explain which one the prompt sits in. Distinguish Calder v Bull's vested-rights tradition (the pre-Fourteenth-Amendment doctrinal seed) from the post-1937 economic settlement (the working framework today).

The phrase to bury in the answer is "Lochnerizing" — a synonym for unprincipled judicial second-guessing of legislative economic policy. After 1937, accusations of Lochnerizing are how the Court tells itself it has stayed in its lane.

## Critique

The textual objection — that the Due Process Clause protects procedure, not substance — applies to the economic arc with the same force it applies to the privacy arc. Justice Thomas's claim that "substantive due process is a contradiction in terms" is most obviously available against Lochner, because Lochner is the case that names the era. The fact that the post-1937 Court has accepted the textual objection as to economic liberty (Williamson) but not as to incorporation (McDonald) or privacy (Obergefell) is the central asymmetry critics flag.

The economic-vs-non-economic asymmetry is also a Carolene Products legacy. Footnote Four's three carve-outs are themselves a normative choice about which liberty interests deserve heightened judicial protection. Critics point out that the Court's choice to demote economic liberty while elevating "discrete and insular minorities" is itself a substantive judgment about the proper allocation of judicial protection — one that the Lochner Court would have rejected. This is the Bruce Ackerman / Cass Sunstein point: 1937 is itself an unwritten constitutional moment that the modern Court enforces by stipulation rather than by textual derivation.

The historical objection is that the Lochner Court's conception of liberty of contract was not a fabrication. The Fourteenth Amendment Framers — many of them Reconstruction-era legislators with a strong pro-labor record — did treat the right to engage in lawful occupations as a core privilege of citizenship. Slaughter-House (1873) and the Lochner cases that followed read that conception narrowly toward employer interests, but the conception itself had textual and historical pedigree. The post-1937 settlement does not refute that pedigree; it disclaims judicial enforcement of it on prudential grounds.

The pragmatic defense of the post-1937 settlement is that Lochner-era judicial review of economic regulation generated bad outcomes: it struck down minimum-wage, maximum-hour, and child-labor laws while elevating untrammeled employer prerogative as a constitutional value. The 1937 Court's retreat is best understood as a recognition that the judiciary is a poor venue for distributive economic policy. Whether that is right or wrong, it is the framework the Court has operated under for nearly nine decades, and the BMW v Gore exception is the only modern departure.
