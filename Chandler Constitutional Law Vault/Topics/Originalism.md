# Originalism

## Overview

Originalism is an interpretive commitment rather than a doctrine of a single constitutional provision. In its mainstream modern form it holds that the meaning of a constitutional text was fixed at the time of ratification and that subsequent interpreters are bound by that original public meaning. The Con Law I vault's materials do not treat originalism as a stand-alone topic. They treat it as the interpretive mode that produced the modern Second Amendment jurisprudence (Heller, Bruen, Rahimi) and as the interpretive mode Justice Thomas invoked in McDonald to argue for a revival of the Privileges or Immunities Clause. The natural entry point for the topic is therefore the Heller-McDonald-Bruen-Rahimi arc, with originalism appearing in three distinct registers: scope of right (Heller), incorporation (McDonald), and methodology for adjudicating restrictions (Bruen and Rahimi). The topic also shows originalism's limits. Originalist judges reach opposite conclusions in Heller (Scalia majority versus Stevens dissent), and Bruen's own author splits from the eight-Justice Rahimi majority over how loose the historical analogy may be. The topic is thus as much a case study in originalism's contested application as it is a statement of an agreed method.

---

## Governing Rule

> **Rule:** Originalism holds that the meaning of a constitutional text was fixed at the time of ratification and that subsequent interpreters should recover and apply that original public meaning. In the vault's materials the method appears in three settings. First, as a method for interpreting the scope of a right (Heller's parsing of the operative and prefatory clauses). Second, as a method for identifying which Bill of Rights provisions bind the States (McDonald's deeply-rooted-in-history-and-tradition plurality, and Thomas's Privileges or Immunities concurrence). Third, as a method for adjudicating modern regulations (Bruen's text-history-and-tradition framework as clarified by Rahimi's historical-analogue requirement).

Originalism in these materials is descriptive, not prescriptive. Several opinions in the same case claim to apply an originalist methodology and still reach opposite results, which is a point the critique section below takes up directly.

## Doctrinal Development

### Heller (2008): Originalism Applied to Scope

Heller is the paradigm originalist opinion of the Roberts Court. Justice Scalia parses the Second Amendment into a prefatory clause and an operative clause and uses founding-era dictionaries (Samuel Johnson 1773), state constitutions, and post-ratification commentary (St. George Tucker, William Rawle, Joseph Story) to recover the original public meaning of the right to keep and bear arms. The operative clause's combined meaning, on Scalia's reading, is the individual right to possess and carry weapons in case of confrontation. The prefatory clause announces one purpose for codifying the right without exhausting the reasons Americans valued it. Justice Stevens's dissent is equally originalist in form. He marshals founding-era texts to argue that bear arms is a military idiom, that the Second Amendment's single-minded focus on militia service (and its silence on hunting and self-defense) shows the Framers intended to protect only military-related arms bearing, and that the people in the Second Amendment refers to the people collectively in their capacity as militia members. Both opinions claim to recover the original public meaning and reach opposite results. The Heller split is the vault's clearest example of originalism's methodological indeterminacy in practice. See [[Cases/District of Columbia v Heller (2008)|full brief]].

### McDonald (2010): Originalism Applied to Incorporation

McDonald shows originalism operating at the doctrinal level. The plurality, written by Justice Alito, applies the standard selective-incorporation test under Due Process, finding the Heller right fundamental to our scheme of ordered liberty and deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition. Justice Thomas concurs in the judgment but reaches the same result through the Privileges or Immunities Clause, deploying an extensive originalist reappraisal of the Fourteenth Amendment. Thomas argues that the Slaughter-House Cases were grievously wrong because the Clause, as originally understood, protected the rights of national citizenship, including the rights enumerated in the Bill of Rights, against state abridgment. He cites the debates of the Thirty-Ninth Congress, Senator Howard's introduction speech specifically listing the right to keep and bear arms among the protected privileges, and the historical treatment of the Colfax Massacre defendants in Cruikshank as evidence that the original public meaning of the Clause was betrayed in 1873 and remains betrayed today. Thomas also rejects substantive due process as a contradiction in terms, arguing that a clause guaranteeing process cannot define the substance of protected rights. The McDonald split between Alito's plurality and Thomas's concurrence is a methodologically important moment: both Justices invoke history and both claim to recover original meaning, but Alito does so within an inherited doctrinal framework (selective incorporation) while Thomas does so to challenge that framework at its textual root. See [[Cases/McDonald v City of Chicago (2010)|full brief]].

### Bruen (2022): Originalism Systematized as Methodology

Bruen converts the originalist commitments of Heller and McDonald into an affirmative framework for adjudicating Second Amendment challenges. Justice Thomas's majority rejects the two-step framework the lower courts had developed, which paired text-and-history scope analysis with means-end scrutiny. Thomas holds that Heller's methodology centered on constitutional text and history without invoking any means-end test, and that Heller's approach requires a similar fidelity to text and history in future cases. The framework Thomas announces is two steps: when the plain text covers the conduct, the Constitution presumptively protects it, and then the government must demonstrate consistency with the Nation's historical tradition of firearm regulation. The methodology privileges founding-era (1791) and Reconstruction-era (1868) evidence because those are the moments at which the relevant constitutional text was adopted. Bruen is originalism operationalized as a doctrinal test rather than as a mode of opinion writing. See [[Cases/New York State Rifle and Pistol Association v Bruen (2022)|full brief]].

### Rahimi (2024): Originalism's Administrability Problem

Rahimi is the originalist methodology's first major stress test. The Fifth Circuit, applying Bruen, had read the historical-analogue requirement to demand a founding-era twin for § 922(g)(8) and struck it down. The Supreme Court, also applying Bruen, reversed 8-1 on the ground that the methodology requires an analogue, not a twin, and that the analogue need only be relevantly similar in burden and justification. Chief Justice Roberts's majority opinion speaks in originalist terms but loosens the methodology in a way that Justice Thomas (the author of Bruen) argues is inconsistent with its original public meaning as Bruen announced it. Thomas's solo dissent is a striking intramural critique: the author of the originalist framework accuses his colleagues of diluting the framework by permitting piecemeal analogies that mix the burden of one historical law with the justification of another. The Rahimi split thus illustrates a recurring feature of originalism in practice. Originalist methodologies do not eliminate judicial discretion so much as they relocate it to the level-of-generality choice and to the selection of which historical evidence counts. See [[Cases/United States v Rahimi (2024)|full brief]].

## Methodological Evolution Across the 2A Arc

The four cases use originalism in different registers and at different levels of operational specificity. The table below reads across five dimensions that the exam tends to press, and together they show the arc moving from interpretive mode (Heller) to doctrinal test (Bruen) to application stabilizer (Rahimi), with McDonald operating one step laterally on incorporation.

| Dimension | [[Cases/District of Columbia v Heller (2008)\|Heller (2008)]] | [[Cases/McDonald v City of Chicago (2010)\|McDonald (2010)]] | [[Cases/New York State Rifle and Pistol Association v Bruen (2022)\|Bruen (2022)]] | [[Cases/United States v Rahimi (2024)\|Rahimi (2024)]] |
|-----------|----------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------|
| **Originalist register** | Scope of right, clause-parsing of operative and prefatory clauses. | Incorporation, Alito plurality via substantive due process; Thomas concurrence via Privileges or Immunities. | Methodology, originalism systematized into a two-step doctrinal framework. | Application, analogical reasoning discipline (analogue not twin). |
| **Primary historical sources** | Founding-era dictionaries (Samuel Johnson 1773), state constitutions, post-ratification commentary (Tucker, Rawle, Story), English antecedents (1689 Bill of Rights). | Thirty-Ninth Congress debates, Howard's introduction speech, Reconstruction-era enforcement history (Colfax/Cruikshank). | Founding-era (1791) and Reconstruction-era (1868) regulations; later outliers discounted. | Surety tradition; common-law going-armed prohibition; colonial-through-antebellum firearm adjudication mechanisms. |
| **Level-of-generality move** | High, "the right of the people" read broadly to cover individual civilian self-defense; Stevens's dissent reads the same sources narrowly. | Divided, Alito reads "deeply rooted" at the right-selection level; Thomas reads the clause at the text level to reopen Slaughter-House. | Tightened, historical analogue must match in burden and justification; courts of appeals warned off generic public-safety invocations. | Loosened, analogue requirement explicitly rejects historical-twin reading; Thomas dissent accuses the majority of mixing-and-matching at too high a level. |
| **Doctrinal stabilizer** | Presumptively-lawful-regulations list (felons, mentally ill, sensitive places, commercial sale conditions); common-use test for protected arms. | Fundamental-and-deeply-rooted incorporation test; selective-incorporation framework preserved over Thomas's P-or-I revival. | How (burden) and why (justification) metrics; shall-issue permissible, may-issue suspect (Kavanaugh concurrence). | Temporariness (disarmament lasts only while the order is in effect) and judicial process (credible-threat finding after notice and hearing). |
| **Standard of review posture** | Rejects rational basis and Breyer's interest-balancing; declines to specify strict or intermediate scrutiny. | Applies Heller federally-then-incorporates; no new standard-of-review holding. | Rejects means-end scrutiny as "one step too many"; text-history-and-tradition is exclusive. | Preserves Bruen; defuses Breyer's administrability critique by showing the test tolerates principled analogy. |

The arc moves in two directions at once. Horizontally, the methodology gets more operationally specific, from Scalia's opinion-writing mode in Heller to Thomas's affirmative doctrinal test in Bruen to Roberts's application rules in Rahimi. Vertically, the Court's tolerance for analogical looseness expands from Bruen's tightening to Rahimi's loosening, which is precisely the disagreement between the author of Bruen (Thomas, dissenting alone in Rahimi) and the eight-Justice Rahimi majority. McDonald sits off-axis: it uses history to pick which right gets incorporated rather than to adjudicate modern regulations of an incorporated right. For cross-reference with the methodology-specific Topic, see [[Topics/Text History and Tradition|Text History and Tradition]].

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side

1. **Hypo:** A litigant challenges a state-level firearm regulation under the Second Amendment and the government defends by pointing to a founding-era statute that imposed a financial bond on persons found to threaten the peace. A court that adopts Rahimi's analogue-not-twin reading, applies the how and why metrics, and finds the modern law relevantly similar in burden and justification has applied originalism in its currently governing Second Amendment form. **Why:** This is the core Bruen-as-clarified-by-Rahimi application. The methodology requires the court to identify a historical regulation and assess comparability at a workable level of generality; the result is originalist in form even if the outcome depends on interpretive judgment at the margins.

2. **Hypo:** A Justice urges reconsideration of a long-standing substantive due process decision and cites original public meaning evidence from the Fourteenth Amendment's drafting history, arguing that Privileges or Immunities rather than Due Process is the textually natural home for the underlying right. **Why:** This is Thomas's McDonald-Timbs move. Originalism as practiced in these concurrences is a methodology for reopening settled doctrine, not merely for interpreting a specific provision.

### Opposite-Side

3. **Hypo:** A court adjudicates a challenge to a modern firearm regulation by applying intermediate scrutiny to the government's public-safety interest, without identifying any historical regulation. **Why:** Bruen rejected exactly this approach. The one-step-too-many holding forecloses means-end scrutiny in Second Amendment cases. An opinion that proceeds this way is not merely wrong on the result; it is methodologically incompatible with the governing originalist framework.

4. **Hypo:** A Justice argues that the original public meaning of the Constitution is irrelevant because the Court's duty is to interpret the document as a living instrument that adapts to contemporary values, and that any reliance on founding-era evidence is antiquarianism. **Why:** This is the direct rejection of the originalist premise. Whether or not the position has intellectual merit, it is incompatible with the vault's key cases, all of which take original public meaning as a binding interpretive constraint.

### Fence-Sitter

5. **Hypo:** Two originalist judges reach opposite conclusions about whether a founding-era surety law for persons threatening the peace is a relevantly similar analogue to a modern red-flag statute authorizing temporary confiscation of firearms from persons found to be a danger to themselves. Both use founding-era sources, both apply the how and why metrics, and both claim to recover the original public meaning. **Why:** This is the level-of-generality problem. A judge who describes the historical regulation narrowly (a bond-based remedy for interpersonal threats) will find no analogue to a confiscation-based remedy for self-directed harm. A judge who describes it broadly (a tradition of preemptive restraint of persons adjudicated to pose a threat) will find an analogue. Heller (Scalia versus Stevens), Bruen (majority versus Breyer dissent), and Rahimi (majority versus Thomas dissent) all exhibit this pattern. The exam answer is that originalism's administrability problem is most visible at precisely this frontier.

## How to Spot on an Exam

Originalism appears in Con Law I exam prompts in three registers, and the right move depends on which register the prompt implicates.

1. **As an interpretive method for a specific provision.** If the question asks about the scope of a constitutional right, a disciplined originalist answer parses the text, consults founding-era evidence, and defaults to original public meaning. Heller is the template. Identify the text, identify the historical sources, and apply the meaning those sources support.

2. **As a doctrinal methodology for adjudicating modern regulations.** If the question implicates the Second Amendment directly, apply Bruen as clarified by Rahimi. Do not revert to means-end scrutiny. Identify the text-covers-the-conduct step and the historical-analogue step, and work the how and why metrics explicitly. Be sensitive to the level-of-generality choice.

3. **As an argument for reopening settled doctrine.** If the question implicates incorporation, substantive due process, or unenumerated rights, identify the Thomas-McDonald-Timbs move and its limits. Originalist arguments for revival of Privileges or Immunities have at least two votes; the plurality's path-dependence on substantive due process is a load-bearing concession the incorporation lecture names as the central modern pressure point.

4. **Originalist critique as a doctrinal weapon.** Recognize when an originalist-sounding argument is being deployed to destabilize a modern doctrinal framework (Bremerton's turn away from Lemon, Dobbs's deeply-rooted inquiry). Flag the methodological move even when it appears in a non-Second-Amendment context.

5. **The indeterminacy pressure point.** Every Heller-Stevens, Bruen-Breyer, and Rahimi-Thomas split illustrates that originalism does not by itself determine outcomes. The level of generality at which historical sources are described, the selection of which sources count, and the extent to which mixing and matching of burdens and justifications is permitted all leave interpretive room. Name the pressure point explicitly on an exam; do not pretend originalism is self-executing.

## Critique

The vault's materials supply two internally generated critiques of originalism and suggest a third from the surrounding doctrinal structure.

The first critique is methodological indeterminacy. The Heller split is the clearest example. Scalia and Stevens both claim fidelity to original meaning and reach opposite conclusions about whether the Second Amendment protects an individual right to civilian self-defense. Critics observe that Scalia's reading of bear arms as encompassing individual civilian carry requires dismissing the overwhelmingly military usage of the phrase in founding-era documents that Stevens catalogued at length. The Rahimi split reinforces the point. The author of Bruen and the Court that applied Bruen two Terms later disagree not about whether originalism is the right method but about how the method should be operationalized. When the same method can be applied in good faith to the same materials and produce opposite results, the method's discipline is at least partly illusory.

The second critique is selectivity about source-of-tradition. Bruen privileges Founding-era (1791) and Reconstruction-era (1868) evidence, treating later historical materials as progressively less weighty. The choice is defensible as a matter of original public meaning, but it is still a choice, and it has the effect of excluding evidence that might complicate the majority's preferred reading (late-nineteenth-century Western territorial restrictions, for example, or the antebellum Southern regulation of slave and free Black populations that shaped the Reconstruction-era record). Heller likewise treats dictionary definitions and commentary as authoritative while giving less weight to the usage statistics Stevens marshaled from contemporary state papers and newspapers. The selection of which historical sources to read, and how to weigh them against each other, is itself a methodological choice that originalism does not determine from outside.

The third critique, drawn from the incorporation lecture, is structural. If substantive due process is illegitimate, as Thomas and many originalists argue, then the entire twentieth-century incorporation edifice is built on tenuous textual footing. McDonald's plurality declined to reconsider Slaughter-House in part because opening the Privileges or Immunities question would unsettle adjacent doctrines (equal protection, unenumerated-rights substantive due process, and by extension Griswold, Roe, and Obergefell). An originalism rigorous enough to require revisiting Slaughter-House would destabilize far more than the Second Amendment. An originalism permissive enough to coexist with selective incorporation under Due Process is hard to distinguish from the clause-shifting the incorporation lecture identifies as the Court's doctrinal sleight of hand. Either horn is a critique of originalism as a complete interpretive methodology.

A counter-argument on behalf of originalism is worth stating. Originalism's defenders respond that every interpretive methodology leaves room for judgment, and that the right question is not whether originalism produces mechanical outcomes but whether it constrains judicial discretion more than the alternatives. On that comparative question the Bruen-Rahimi record is at least mixed. The methodology did not prevent the Fifth Circuit and the Supreme Court from reaching opposite results on the same statute, but it did generate a shared framework that both courts worked within, and the dispute was about application rather than about whether historical evidence matters at all. Whether that constraint is sufficient is the unresolved pressure point.
