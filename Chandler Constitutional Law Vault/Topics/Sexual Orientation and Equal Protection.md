# Sexual Orientation and Equal Protection

## Overview

Sexual-orientation doctrine is the branch of Equal Protection and Substantive Due Process law that governs governmental classifications and criminal regulation directed at gay, lesbian, and bisexual persons. Over roughly four decades, the Court moved from denying that private consensual same-sex intimacy was protected conduct ([[Cases/Bowers v Hardwick (1986)|Bowers]]) to recognizing a fundamental right to marry for same-sex couples ([[Cases/Obergefell v Hodges (2015)|Obergefell]]), and then to a partial First Amendment retrenchment in the public-accommodations arena ([[Cases/303 Creative LLC v Elenis (2023)|303 Creative]]). The Court has never declared sexual orientation a suspect class for heightened scrutiny purposes; instead, three distinct Kennedy-era frameworks did the doctrinal work: (1) Romer's "rational basis with bite" via the animus doctrine, (2) Lawrence's liberty-based Due Process framing that deliberately left the scrutiny tier unspecified, and (3) Obergefell's dual-clause "synergy" of Due Process and Equal Protection. These frameworks reach results associated with heightened scrutiny without ever announcing that sexual orientation is a suspect class, leaving the doctrine analytically underspecified.

Three anchor principles organize the area after Obergefell: (1) a "bare desire to harm a politically unpopular group" is not a legitimate governmental interest and cannot sustain even rational-basis review; (2) private, consensual adult sexual conduct is protected liberty that the State may not criminalize; (3) the fundamental right to marry extends to same-sex couples, binding the States under both the Due Process and Equal Protection Clauses. 303 Creative cuts a First Amendment channel back: public-accommodations statutes cannot compel a creator of custom expressive content to communicate a message with which she disagrees, even where the regulation responds to sexual-orientation discrimination.

---

## Governing Rule

> **Rule:** Governmental classifications directed at gay, lesbian, or bisexual persons are reviewed under a fluctuating Equal Protection / Due Process framework that reaches heightened-scrutiny results without formally declaring sexual orientation a suspect class. A law that reflects "a bare desire to harm a politically unpopular group" fails even rational-basis review ([[Cases/Romer v Evans (1996)|Romer]]). The State cannot criminalize private, consensual adult sexual conduct ([[Cases/Lawrence v Texas (2003)|Lawrence]]). The fundamental right to marry extends to same-sex couples under the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process and Equal Protection Clauses operating in "synergy" ([[Cases/Obergefell v Hodges (2015)|Obergefell]]). Nothing in this line overrides the First Amendment's bar on compelled expressive speech ([[Cases/303 Creative LLC v Elenis (2023)|303 Creative]]).

> "[A] bare desire to harm a politically unpopular group cannot constitute a legitimate governmental interest."
>
> — Kennedy, J., [[Cases/Romer v Evans (1996)|Romer]], quoting Department of Agriculture v Moreno ([pin-cite](https://app.midpage.ai/document/romer-v-evans-118027?utm_source=mcp&lines=168-172))

> "The petitioners are entitled to respect for their private lives. The State cannot demean their existence or control their destiny by making their private sexual conduct a crime."
>
> — Kennedy, J., [[Cases/Lawrence v Texas (2003)|Lawrence]], 539 U.S. at 578 ([pin-cite](https://app.midpage.ai/document/lawrence-v-texas-130160?utm_source=mcp&lines=294-297))

> "[T]he right to marry is a fundamental right inherent in the liberty of the person, and under the Due Process and Equal Protection Clauses of the Fourteenth Amendment couples of the same-sex may not be deprived of that right and that liberty."
>
> — Kennedy, J., [[Cases/Obergefell v Hodges (2015)|Obergefell]] ([pin-cite](https://app.midpage.ai/document/obergefell-v-hodges-2812209?utm_source=mcp&lines=443-446))

## Doctrinal Development

### Bowers v Hardwick (1986): The Baseline That Was Overruled

Michael Hardwick challenged Georgia's criminal sodomy statute after an officer, serving an unrelated warrant, observed him engaged in consensual same-sex intimacy in his bedroom. A 5-4 majority authored by Justice White refused to recognize a fundamental right to engage in "homosexual sodomy." The Court's methodology was historical-tradition in the mode later formalized in Washington v Glucksberg: it identified the asserted right narrowly and asked whether that narrowly described right was deeply rooted in history. Finding it was not, the majority sustained the Georgia statute under rational-basis review, citing the State's interest in public morality. Burger's concurrence grounded the rule in natural law and Judeo-Christian tradition. Blackmun's dissent framed the case as a privacy-as-right-to-be-let-alone question; Stevens's dissent argued the statute was arbitrary because Georgia did not prosecute heterosexual sodomy. Bowers governed the field for seventeen years before Lawrence overruled it. See [[Cases/Bowers v Hardwick (1986)|full brief]].

### Romer v Evans (1996): Rational Basis with Bite

Colorado's Amendment 2, adopted by statewide referendum in 1992, repealed all existing state and local laws protecting gay, lesbian, or bisexual persons from discrimination and barred enactment of any future such protections. Kennedy's 6-3 majority struck it down under rational basis review. The analytic move was to bypass the suspect-class framework entirely: Amendment 2's "sheer breadth" was "so discontinuous with the reasons offered for it that the amendment seems inexplicable by anything but animus toward the class it affects" ([pin-cite](https://app.midpage.ai/document/romer-v-evans-118027?utm_source=mcp&lines=180)). The opinion announced the core doctrinal rule that "a bare desire to harm a politically unpopular group cannot constitute a legitimate governmental interest," drawing on Department of Agriculture v Moreno. Scalia's dissent (joined by Rehnquist and Thomas) framed Amendment 2 as "a modest attempt by seemingly tolerant Coloradans to preserve traditional sexual mores" and accused the majority of mistaking "a Kulturkampf for a fit of spite" ([pin-cite](https://app.midpage.ai/document/romer-v-evans-118027?utm_source=mcp&lines=196)). The Romer framework operates below heightened scrutiny yet reliably strikes down legislation; scholars call this "rational basis with bite." The move left the doctrine analytically underspecified: Kennedy never said whether sexual orientation was a suspect class, yet the result matched what heightened scrutiny would produce. See [[Cases/Romer v Evans (1996)|full brief]].

### Lawrence v Texas (2003): Liberty Without a Scrutiny Tier

Texas Penal Code §21.06 criminalized "deviate sexual intercourse with another individual of the same sex" while leaving identical heterosexual conduct lawful. Kennedy's majority overruled Bowers and framed the case primarily under substantive Due Process rather than Equal Protection, even though the sex-based targeting would have supported an Equal Protection rationale. The opinion held that "Bowers was not correct when it was decided, and it is not correct today" ([pin-cite](https://app.midpage.ai/document/lawrence-v-texas-130160?utm_source=mcp&lines=289-291)); the Texas statute "furthers no legitimate state interest which can justify its intrusion into the personal and private life of the individual" ([pin-cite](https://app.midpage.ai/document/lawrence-v-texas-130160?utm_source=mcp&lines=302)). O'Connor's concurrence in judgment would have reached the same result under Equal Protection without overruling Bowers, drawing out the same Moreno-derived animus principle Romer invoked: "Moral disapproval of this group, like a bare desire to harm the group, is an interest that is insufficient to satisfy rational basis review under the Equal Protection Clause" ([pin-cite](https://app.midpage.ai/document/lawrence-v-texas-130160?utm_source=mcp&lines=381)). Scalia's dissent warned the majority's reasoning would sweep beyond same-sex intimacy into marriage and foreshadowed Obergefell by more than a decade. The Lawrence majority deliberately left the scrutiny tier unspecified, applying only "legitimate state interest" language without identifying the level of review. That ambiguity both enabled and handicapped the doctrine going forward. See [[Cases/Lawrence v Texas (2003)|full brief]].

### Obergefell v Hodges (2015): The Synergy Framework and the Fundamental Right to Marry

Obergefell consolidated challenges to state bans on same-sex marriage and same-sex marriage recognition. Kennedy's 5-4 majority held that the Fourteenth Amendment requires every State to license same-sex marriages and to recognize same-sex marriages lawfully performed elsewhere. The analytic architecture was unusual: the opinion rested on both the Due Process Clause and the Equal Protection Clause acting in what Kennedy called "synergy," without identifying which clause did the decisive work. The fundamental right to marry was said to be "inherent in the liberty of the person," and its extension to same-sex couples followed from four features of marriage that apply equally across orientations (individual autonomy, the two-person union, safeguarding children and families, and the institution's centrality to social order). The four dissents (Roberts, Scalia, Thomas, Alito) attacked the majority on separate but overlapping grounds: usurpation of democratic self-governance (Roberts), absence of historical tradition supporting the right (Scalia), reliance on "liberty" disconnected from any originalist meaning (Thomas), and harm to religious liberty (Alito). Scalia's "judicial Putsch" passage ([pin-cite](https://app.midpage.ai/document/obergefell-v-hodges-2812209?utm_source=mcp&lines=1190-1191)) became a shorthand for the legitimacy critique that Dobbs (2022) would later mobilize against Roe and Casey. The synergy framework has been criticized as analytically opaque: by declining to identify the governing clause, Obergefell provided no clear guidance for lower courts facing new sexual-orientation claims. See [[Cases/Obergefell v Hodges (2015)|full brief]].

### 303 Creative LLC v Elenis (2023): The First Amendment Carve-Out

A Colorado website designer brought a pre-enforcement challenge to the State's public-accommodations law, which she feared would require her to create custom wedding websites for same-sex ceremonies even though she objected on religious grounds to doing so. Gorsuch's 6-3 majority held that the Free Speech Clause barred Colorado from applying the public-accommodations statute to compel her to create custom expressive content communicating a message she did not wish to convey. The opinion disclaimed any retreat from Obergefell on the right to marry or from the validity of public-accommodations laws generally; the narrow holding was that where a state public-accommodations statute would operate to compel a custom-expressive-content creator to speak a message she rejects, the First Amendment prevails. Sotomayor's dissent (joined by Kagan and Jackson) argued the majority announced a constitutional right for a class of commercial actors to discriminate against protected classes of customers, reopening "separate-but-equal"-style questions in the public-accommodations domain. 303 Creative is a narrow First Amendment decision that does not alter the Obergefell / Lawrence / Romer structure of substantive Equal Protection doctrine, but it marks the first Roberts Court decision creating a constitutional limit on sexual-orientation antidiscrimination enforcement. See [[Cases/303 Creative LLC v Elenis (2023)|full brief]].

## Comparison: The Bowers-to-Lawrence Framing Pivot

Same factual posture (a state criminal-sodomy statute applied to private consensual adult conduct), opposite outcomes seventeen years apart. The reversal turns almost entirely on how the asserted liberty interest is framed, illustrating Scalia's Michael H footnote-6 concern that level-of-generality choices can determine results before the merits are reached.

| Element | Bowers v Hardwick (1986) | Lawrence v Texas (2003) |
|---------|--------------------------|--------------------------|
| **Framing of asserted right** | "Fundamental right to engage in homosexual sodomy" (most-specific level of generality) | "Liberty in their private lives" / autonomy in intimate conduct (broader, autonomy-dignity register) |
| **Operative test** | Glucksberg-style historical-tradition test (deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition) | Casey-style autonomy-dignity inquiry (intimate decisions central to personal dignity) plus rational-basis-with-bite |
| **Role of moral disapproval** | Majoritarian moral disapproval is a sufficient rational basis | Moral disapproval cannot by itself be a legitimate state interest |
| **Treatment of Griswold-Eisenstadt-Roe line** | Confined to procreative or marital privacy; refused to extend to non-procreative same-sex intimacy | Treated as the "most pertinent beginning point"; Griswold and the privacy line are read to protect intimate autonomy more broadly |
| **Vote / coalition** | 5-4 (White, Burger, Powell, Rehnquist, O'Connor; Powell later regretted his vote) | 6-3 (Kennedy majority; O'Connor concurrence on Equal Protection grounds; Scalia, Thomas, Rehnquist dissent) |
| **Methodological lineage continued by** | [[Cases/Washington v Glucksberg (1997)\|Washington v Glucksberg]] (1997); [[Cases/Dobbs v Jackson Women's Health Organization (2022)\|Dobbs]] (2022) | [[Cases/Obergefell v Hodges (2015)\|Obergefell]] (2015); [[Cases/Lawrence v Texas (2003)\|Lawrence]]'s autonomy thread carries forward through Kennedy's later opinions |
| **What the pivot reveals** | Specific-level-of-generality framing locks in tradition-based outcomes before the merits are reached | Broad-level-of-generality framing routes the case through autonomy and dignity; Bowers is overruled not because facts changed but because the question changed |

The Bowers-Lawrence pair is the canonical exhibit for the proposition that, in Substantive Due Process, *how* the right is named often *is* the holding.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side

1. **Hypo:** A state legislature repeals its statute extending domestic-partnership benefits to same-sex couples and, by a narrow referendum, amends its constitution to bar any future enactment of such benefits by any state or local body. **Why:** This is Romer on close facts. The amendment imposes a special disability on a targeted group, barring them from seeking specific legal protections through ordinary democratic processes while leaving all others free to do so. Without an independent legitimate interest disconnected from group animus, it fails rational basis review.

2. **Hypo:** A state criminalizes "same-sex cohabitation in private dwellings" as a misdemeanor punishable by fine. **Why:** This is Lawrence on modern facts. Private, consensual adult sexual conduct (which cohabitation necessarily implicates) is protected liberty under Substantive Due Process, and the criminal penalty "demean[s] their existence." The statute furthers no legitimate state interest that can justify the intrusion into personal and private life.

### Opposite-Side

3. **Hypo:** A state civil-rights commission orders a corporate event-planning company to provide its standard, off-the-shelf wedding services to a same-sex couple on the same terms the company offers any other paying customer. The services are non-expressive (venue booking, catering coordination, guest management) and do not require the company's owner to speak any message about the ceremony's meaning. **Why:** 303 Creative's holding is narrow. It turns on compulsion of custom expressive content communicating a message the creator rejects. Off-the-shelf non-expressive services are precisely what public-accommodations laws legitimately reach; the First Amendment carve-out does not cover them. The order is a permissible application of antidiscrimination law.

4. **Hypo:** A state passes a statute imposing a higher tax rate on "entities organized to celebrate nonconforming family arrangements" and the legislative record is replete with floor speeches describing the statute as a response to Obergefell and a reaffirmation of "traditional morality." **Why:** This is Romer doctrinally. The classification singles out the Obergefell-protected group and the legislative record establishes that the statute's purpose is to impose a disability on that group because of moral disapproval. Both the Romer "bare desire to harm" principle and Lawrence's rejection of moral disapproval as a sufficient interest defeat rational basis. Struck down.

### Fence-Sitter

5. **Hypo:** A state prohibits all public elementary and middle schools from any curricular "instruction on sexual orientation or gender identity" through Grade 8. The statute is facially neutral (it bars instruction, not a viewpoint), applies to schools not individuals, and the legislative record emphasizes child-development concerns and parental-rights rhetoric but contains scattered statements that could be read as reflecting animus toward LGBTQ persons. **Why:** This sits at the intersection of Romer, First Amendment viewpoint-discrimination doctrine, and Pierce v Society of Sisters parental-rights tradition. A plaintiff would argue Romer: the curricular ban imposes a special disability on an identifiable group of students, families, and teachers by singling out their identities for omission while all other topics are treated on the merits. The State would respond that instructional content choices are a traditional area of state authority and that the statute classifies by subject matter, not by protected class. The outcome depends on how a court reads the legislative record and whether it treats curricular design as closer to Romer's broad disability-imposition or to ordinary state pedagogical choices. The statute's exposure is highest if the record supports an animus inference; lowest if the State can establish a coherent child-development rationale unconnected to the Romer pattern.

## How to Spot on an Exam

Trigger facts: a government regulation, criminal statute, or benefits scheme that classifies by sexual orientation, targets same-sex conduct or relationships, or withdraws protections previously extended to gay, lesbian, or bisexual persons. The analytical framework:

**Step 1: Identify the classification or regulation.** Is the state criminalizing conduct (Lawrence lane)? Classifying persons and imposing disabilities (Romer lane)? Regulating marriage eligibility or recognition (Obergefell lane)? Compelling expressive speech in a public-accommodations context (303 Creative lane)?

**Step 2: Choose the doctrinal framework.** For criminal regulation of private consensual adult sexual conduct, Lawrence Substantive Due Process governs. For classifications imposing legal disabilities, Romer rational-basis-with-bite animus analysis is the first move. For marriage and intimate-relationship recognition, the Obergefell Due Process / Equal Protection synergy framework controls. For compelled custom expressive content in public accommodations, 303 Creative First Amendment analysis governs.

**Step 3: Apply the relevant test.** Under Lawrence, ask whether the statute furthers a legitimate state interest that justifies the intrusion into personal and private life. Under Romer, ask whether the classification is explicable by anything other than animus toward the affected group; the "sheer breadth" and discontinuity with reasons offered are the key markers. Under Obergefell, ask whether the four features of marriage (individual autonomy, two-person union, safeguarding children and families, institutional centrality) apply equally. Under 303 Creative, ask whether the regulation compels custom expressive content and whether the content communicates a message the speaker rejects.

**Step 4: Watch for the scrutiny-tier ambiguity.** None of these cases holds that sexual orientation is a suspect class. A careful answer notes that the Court has reached heightened-scrutiny-equivalent results through three distinct frameworks that avoid the suspect-class question directly.

**Step 5: Flag the unresolved edges.** Transgender-status classifications (Skrmetti post-2025 unresolved beyond the healthcare context); religious-exemption challenges to sexual-orientation antidiscrimination laws outside expressive-content contexts; the reach of Obergefell's recognition holding to parental and family-law benefits in the wake of Dobbs's reliance-interest dicta.

## Critique

**Progressive critique: the concealed classification problem.** The Araiza and Pollvogt line of scholarship (referenced in Professor Chandler's lecture) argues that the animus doctrine conceals an implicit classification hierarchy. Kennedy never decided in Romer whether sexual orientation is a suspect class, yet the result is indistinguishable from what heightened scrutiny would produce. The problem is that laws against, say, bank robbery also reflect "animus" toward bank robbers, yet no one treats such laws as suspect. The animus doctrine works only if we believe, sub silentio, that gay, lesbian, and bisexual persons deserve more protection than bank robbers; but that conclusion requires exactly the suspect-class analysis Kennedy avoided. The result: the doctrine produces heightened-scrutiny outcomes while leaving lower courts without a workable standard to extend or limit them.

**Originalist and textualist critique.** Scalia's dissents in Romer, Lawrence, and Obergefell form the core of the originalist critique. The Fourteenth Amendment's Reconstruction Framers legislated against the backdrop of universal State prohibition of same-sex intimacy and marriage; no plausible historical-tradition reading of "liberty" or "equal protection" yielded the Lawrence or Obergefell results in 1868 or in 1886 (when every State still criminalized sodomy). The Lawrence and Obergefell decisions therefore rest on judicial will substituted for democratic self-governance. Thomas's separate Dobbs concurrence carries this critique forward by flagging Lawrence and Obergefell as candidates for the same reconsideration Dobbs applied to Roe. The originalist critique accepts Romer's animus principle as an intelligible rule against invidious targeting but rejects the substantive-liberty and fundamental-rights extensions as illegitimate judicial lawmaking.

**Doctrinal critique: the analytic opacity of the synergy framework.** Obergefell's decision to rest on both Due Process and Equal Protection acting "in synergy" without identifying which clause did the decisive work leaves lower courts without guidance for new sexual-orientation claims. A same-sex-couple parental-rights question (presumption of parentage, second-parent adoption, birth certificate entries) is hard to analyze under Obergefell because the opinion does not isolate the operative rule. The result is a doctrine that performs strongly on the core case (marriage licensing and recognition) but communicates poorly on the periphery. Professor Chandler's lecture treats this opacity as the signature feature of the Kennedy-era sexual-orientation jurisprudence: the Court reached correct results through frameworks that resist easy replication.

**Internal tension with 303 Creative and the post-Dobbs environment.** The post-Dobbs environment (substantive-due-process rights under reconsideration pressure; originalist majority entrenching in Thomas, Alito, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh, and Barrett) creates a structural tension with the Kennedy-era framework. 303 Creative is one visible expression: it constructs a First Amendment channel for refusing service tied to sexual orientation without disturbing Obergefell. Whether that channel becomes narrow (custom-expressive-content cases only) or broad (any sincere religious-liberty objection to serving same-sex customers) is the central doctrinal question of the next decade. Skrmetti (2025) suggests the Court is willing to classify transgender-status regulation as targeting diagnosis rather than sex, moving the doctrine away from heightened-scrutiny frameworks.
