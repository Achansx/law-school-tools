# Fundamental Rights

## Overview

Fundamental Rights is the branch of substantive due process that protects unenumerated personal liberties from state infringement under the Fourteenth Amendment, and from federal infringement under the Fifth. It is distinct from the incorporation strand of substantive due process, which uses the Fourteenth Amendment as a conduit to apply specific provisions of the Bill of Rights against the States. This page collects the cases in which the Court has recognized, limited, or repudiated unenumerated rights rooted directly in the word "liberty" rather than routed through a specific Bill of Rights guarantee.

Professor Chandler treats the doctrine as primarily a debate about method rather than a stable catalogue of protected rights. The black-letter test, crystallized in Washington v Glucksberg (1997), asks whether an asserted liberty interest is (1) deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition and (2) implicit in the concept of ordered liberty, with a "careful description" of the right at the appropriate level of specificity. What looks like a single test is actually a collection of methodological choices that are individually outcome-determinative. How narrowly the claimed right is described, how far back the historical inquiry runs, whether contemporary consensus counts as evidence, and whether Due Process and Equal Protection may be allowed to reinforce each other are each contested moves on which entire bodies of doctrine have risen and fallen.

The lecture frames the arc as four methodological turns layered on top of the same textual hook. Meyer and Pierce supply the founding premise, reading the Fourteenth Amendment's "liberty" to include the right to marry, to establish a home, to raise children, and to direct their education. Griswold, Loving, and Moore extend that premise into privacy, marriage, and family structure, but each does so on unstable methodological footing (penumbras, dual holdings, plurality opinions). Michael H introduces Scalia's Footnote 6 most-specific-tradition rule as the tool that would sharply restrict the doctrine, but that footnote never commands a majority. Glucksberg packages the test in its modern form. Kennedy's opinions in Lawrence and Obergefell then displace Glucksberg by describing rights at higher levels of generality and inviting Due Process and Equal Protection to reinforce each other. Dobbs reverses course, restoring Glucksberg as the governing standard and applying it narrowly to conclude that abortion is not a fundamental right. Lawrence and Obergefell were expressly disclaimed from the overruling, but the methodological turn leaves them on contested footing.

Two structural features recur across the doctrine. First, the level-of-generality problem is not a side debate; it is the engine that drives outcomes, because the same claim can be framed as "the right to abortion" or "the right to bodily autonomy" and the tradition-and-history inquiry will reach opposite results depending on which framing prevails. Second, the doctrine's history includes a catastrophic failure in Buck v Bell (1927), which Holmes decided without ever asking whether bodily autonomy was a liberty interest Meyer had recognized four years earlier. The case has never been explicitly overruled, and its continuing presence on the books is the single most powerful reminder that methodology is never neutral.

This Topic sits beside, and partly inside, [[Topics/Substantive Due Process|Substantive Due Process]]. The SDP Topic page treats substantive due process mainly as an incorporation vehicle and deliberately omits the privacy, marriage, and family lineage covered here. Related doctrinal pages include [[Topics/Incorporation Doctrine|Incorporation Doctrine]] (the parallel substantive-due-process branch that carries enumerated Bill of Rights guarantees against the States) and [[Topics/Privileges or Immunities|Privileges or Immunities]] (the Thomas-concurrence alternative that would relocate unenumerated rights to a different textual home).

## Governing Rule

> **The Glucksberg Test (Washington v Glucksberg, 1997):** An asserted liberty interest is a fundamental right protected by the Due Process Clause only if (1) it is deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition, and (2) it is implicit in the concept of ordered liberty, such that neither liberty nor justice would exist if it were sacrificed. The Court must produce a "careful description" of the asserted right. A right that survives both prongs triggers strict scrutiny: the state action must be narrowly tailored to a compelling government interest. A right that fails either prong is reviewed for rational basis, and a legitimate state interest (including traditional state interests in preserving human life, protecting vulnerable populations, and regulating medical practice) will usually sustain the restriction.

> **Rule (Meyer-Pierce liberty definition):** The word "liberty" in the Fourteenth Amendment Due Process Clause is not limited to freedom from bodily restraint. It includes, at least, the right to contract, to engage in the common occupations of life, to acquire useful knowledge, to marry, to establish a home and bring up children, to worship God according to the dictates of one's conscience, and generally to enjoy those privileges long recognized at common law as essential to the orderly pursuit of happiness by free men.

> **Rule (level-of-generality / careful description):** The tradition inquiry is keyed to the specificity at which the asserted right is described. A right framed broadly (personal autonomy, intimate association, bodily integrity) will usually find some tradition of protection. The same right framed narrowly (the right to physician-assisted suicide, the right to abortion, the right of a biological father to parental rights when the child is born during another man's marriage) will rarely find one. Scalia's Michael H Footnote 6 would fix this problem by requiring courts to use the most specific level at which a tradition protecting or denying the right can be identified, but Footnote 6 has never commanded a majority and is therefore non-binding precedent under Marks v United States.

> **Rule (emerging-awareness alternative, Lawrence and Obergefell):** History is a floor, not a ceiling. Tradition may be a starting point but cannot be the complete answer, because "times can blind us to certain truths and later generations can see that laws once thought necessary and proper in fact serve only to oppress." A right may be fundamental if the reasons underlying its recognition for some persons apply with equal force to others, even in the absence of a specific historical tradition of protection. This methodology is not the governing rule after Dobbs; its continued doctrinal force is one of the open questions the Topic tracks.

> **Rule (dual-holding insulation, Loving v Virginia):** When a statute classifies on race and also burdens a protected liberty, the Equal Protection Clause and the Due Process Clause may each independently invalidate the statute. Either doctrinal foundation is sufficient, and a narrowing of one does not automatically narrow the other. Loving therefore remains doctrinally stable even if its due-process branch were to be reread under a narrower test.

## Doctrinal Development

### Meyer v Nebraska (1923) and Pierce v Society of Sisters (1925)

The founding premise. [[Cases/Meyer v Nebraska (1923)|Meyer]] held that a Nebraska statute banning foreign-language instruction to pre-ninth-grade students violated the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause. Justice McReynolds produced the first broad judicial definition of "liberty," extending it well beyond freedom from bodily restraint. [[Cases/Pierce v Society of Sisters (1925)|Pierce]] applied that definition a year later to strike down an Oregon statute requiring all children to attend public schools, on the ground that it unreasonably interfered with the liberty of parents to direct the upbringing of their children. Together, Meyer and Pierce established that the Due Process Clause protects a zone of family autonomy against state interference, without routing the analysis through any specific Bill of Rights guarantee. See [[Cases/Meyer v Nebraska (1923)|full brief]] and [[Cases/Pierce v Society of Sisters (1925)|full brief]].

### Buck v Bell (1927)

The doctrine's catastrophic failure. [[Cases/Buck v Bell (1927)|Buck v Bell]] upheld Virginia's forced-sterilization statute 8-1, with Justice Holmes analogizing surgical destruction of reproductive capacity to compulsory vaccination under Jacobson v Massachusetts and declaring "three generations of imbeciles are enough." The opinion is a methodological failure on its own terms: Holmes never asked whether bodily autonomy was within the "liberty" that Meyer had defined four years earlier to include the right to "establish a home and bring up children." Over 60,000 Americans were sterilized under state eugenics laws modeled on or authorized by Buck. The case has never been explicitly overruled. See [[Cases/Buck v Bell (1927)|full brief]].

### Griswold v Connecticut (1965)

Marital privacy. [[Cases/Griswold v Connecticut (1965)|Griswold]] struck down a Connecticut statute criminalizing the use of contraceptives even by married couples. Justice Douglas's majority grounded a right of marital privacy in "penumbras, formed by emanations" from the specific guarantees of the First, Third, Fourth, Fifth, and Ninth Amendments, deliberately avoiding a direct substantive-due-process theory to sidestep Lochner's shadow. Justice Goldberg's concurrence, joined by Chief Justice Warren and Justice Brennan, would have invoked the Ninth Amendment more directly. The penumbras methodology proved rhetorically memorable but doctrinally unstable, and later cases (Eisenstadt v Baird, Carey v Population Services, Roe v Wade) re-grounded the privacy line directly in Fourteenth Amendment liberty. See [[Cases/Griswold v Connecticut (1965)|full brief]].

### Loving v Virginia (1967)

The freedom to marry. [[Cases/Loving v Virginia (1967)|Loving]] unanimously struck down Virginia's Racial Integrity Act on two independently sufficient grounds: the Equal Protection Clause (racial classifications in criminal statutes require the most rigid scrutiny, and "white supremacy" is not a permissible state objective) and the Due Process Clause (marriage is "one of the basic civil rights of man, fundamental to our very existence and survival"). The dual-holding architecture insulates the case from any narrowing of either doctrine. The Loving paradox (Chandler's phrase): anti-miscegenation laws were themselves deeply rooted in American tradition, so a literal Glucksberg test would condemn Loving as wrong. Chandler treats this paradox as the strongest single argument that purely backward-looking tradition cannot be the whole story of constitutional rights. See [[Cases/Loving v Virginia (1967)|full brief]].

### Moore v City of East Cleveland (1977)

Extended family. [[Cases/Moore v City of East Cleveland (1977)|Moore]] invalidated an East Cleveland housing ordinance whose narrow definition of "family" excluded a grandmother who lived with her son and two grandsons (cousins, not siblings). Justice Powell's plurality extended the family-autonomy line from Meyer and Pierce to reach extended family living arrangements, grounding the extension in a tradition "equally venerable" to the nuclear family norm. Justice Brennan's concurrence, joined by Justice Marshall, added the racial dimension: facially neutral family-definition ordinances can encode a white suburban norm while disparately burdening Black family structures shaped by the Great Migration and by economic necessity. See [[Cases/Moore v City of East Cleveland (1977)|full brief]].

### Michael H v Gerald D (1989)

Level of generality, made explicit. [[Cases/Michael H v Gerald D (1989)|Michael H]] denied parental rights to a biological father whose child had been born during the mother's marriage to another man. Justice Scalia's plurality, in the famous Footnote 6, argued that courts "must refer to the most specific level at which a relevant tradition protecting, or denying protection to, the asserted right can be identified." Only Chief Justice Rehnquist joined the footnote; Justices O'Connor and Kennedy joined the rest of Scalia's opinion but explicitly refused to endorse footnote 6, while Justice Stevens concurred in the judgment alone. The outcome (Michael loses) is binding; the methodology (the specificity rule) is not, under Marks v United States. Justice Brennan's dissent identified the circularity: if a tradition of denying a right is treated as evidence that the right does not exist, the more successfully a state has repressed a right, the less constitutional protection it receives. Griswold, Eisenstadt, and Stanley v Illinois would all fail a consistently applied Footnote 6 test. Michael H is therefore the engine of the methodology wars: the level-of-generality debate it exposed remained open, and Glucksberg, Lawrence, Obergefell, and Dobbs are each staking positions along that axis. See [[Cases/Michael H v Gerald D (1989)|full brief]].

### Cruzan v Director Missouri Department of Health (1990)

Bodily integrity and the right to refuse. [[Cases/Cruzan v Director Missouri Department of Health (1990)|Cruzan]] recognized, through a combination of majority, concurring, and dissenting opinions, that a competent person has a constitutionally protected liberty interest in refusing unwanted medical treatment, including artificial nutrition and hydration. The Court nevertheless upheld Missouri's clear-and-convincing-evidence procedural requirement for surrogate decisions on behalf of a non-competent patient. Cruzan is doctrinally important both as the Court's most explicit recognition of a bodily-integrity liberty interest and as the launching point for Glucksberg seven years later, where the same methodology is used to deny a further step: a right to physician-assisted suicide. See [[Cases/Cruzan v Director Missouri Department of Health (1990)|full brief]].

### Washington v Glucksberg (1997)

The test crystallized. [[Cases/Washington v Glucksberg (1997)|Glucksberg]] upheld Washington's ban on assisted suicide 9-0 in the judgment, but the modern two-part test was formalized by a 5-Justice methodology majority (Rehnquist plus O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas), with Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer concurring in the judgment without joining the rigid history-and-tradition framing. The test as articulated by the majority: the asserted right must be (1) deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition and (2) implicit in the concept of ordered liberty, framed with a "careful description." Chief Justice Rehnquist's opinion placed historical evidence first, emphasized narrow description, and treated Meyer, Pierce, Griswold, Loving, Moore, and Cruzan as recognized markers on a short list of historically grounded rights rather than instances of a continually expanding methodology. Glucksberg is the governing articulation when read strictly; Kennedy's later opinions depart from it; Dobbs returns to it. See [[Cases/Washington v Glucksberg (1997)|full brief]].

### Troxel v Granville (2000)

Parental rights, re-anchored. [[Cases/Troxel v Granville (2000)|Troxel]] struck down a Washington State third-party visitation statute as applied, on the ground that it permitted courts to override the decisions of a fit custodial parent regarding grandparent visitation without affording any special weight to the parent's judgment. Justice O'Connor's plurality treated the Meyer-Pierce parental-rights line as a settled fundamental right and crystallized the Troxel presumption: so long as a parent adequately cares for the child, there will normally be no reason for the State to inject itself into the private realm of the family. See [[Cases/Troxel v Granville (2000)|full brief]].

### Lawrence v Texas (2003)

Emerging awareness. [[Cases/Lawrence v Texas (2003)|Lawrence]] overruled Bowers v Hardwick and invalidated Texas's sodomy statute. Justice Kennedy's majority reframed the asserted right away from Bowers's narrow "right to engage in homosexual sodomy" to a broader right to engage in private consensual adult intimate conduct, observed that laws criminalizing such conduct had been widely repealed, and announced that "times can blind us" to certain truths. The opinion declined to label the right "fundamental," declined to name a tier of scrutiny, and strategically avoided a clean Due Process versus Equal Protection election. Justice Scalia's dissent identified the methodological stakes: under a consistent Glucksberg reading, the narrow framing controls and the claim fails; under Kennedy's framing, tradition is a floor rather than a ceiling and the claim succeeds. See [[Cases/Lawrence v Texas (2003)|full brief]].

### Obergefell v Hodges (2015)

Four principles and DP-EP synergy. [[Cases/Obergefell v Hodges (2015)|Obergefell]] held that the Fourteenth Amendment requires states to license and recognize same-sex marriages. Justice Kennedy's majority declined to apply Glucksberg's careful-description step directly. Instead, he identified four principles that explain why marriage is a fundamental right (individual autonomy, two-person commitment, safeguarding of children and families, social-order keystone) and concluded that each principle applies to same-sex couples with equal force. He then allowed Due Process and Equal Protection to reinforce each other: "The Due Process Clause and the Equal Protection Clause are connected in a profound way." Chief Justice Roberts's dissent called this "sentiment dressed up as constitutional analysis" and warned that without Glucksberg's limiting principle, judicial recognition of unenumerated rights has no constraint. Obergefell is the clearest modern example of Glucksberg displacement by methodological reformulation. See [[Cases/Obergefell v Hodges (2015)|full brief]].

### Dobbs v Jackson Women's Health Organization (2022)

Return to Glucksberg. [[Cases/Dobbs v Jackson Women's Health Organization (2022)|Dobbs]] overruled Roe v Wade and Planned Parenthood v Casey and held that the Constitution does not confer a right to abortion. Justice Alito's majority applied Glucksberg at its narrow framing, surveyed English common law and nineteenth-century state statutes, and concluded that abortion was criminalized throughout the relevant historical period. The opinion expressly disclaimed any effect on Griswold, Lawrence, or Obergefell, reasoning that abortion is unique because it involves potential human life. Justice Thomas's concurrence urged the Court to go further and reconsider Griswold, Lawrence, and Obergefell, and to relocate any surviving unenumerated-rights protection to the Privileges or Immunities Clause. The joint dissent warned that the majority's methodology, applied consistently, cannot be cabined. Dobbs is the immediate doctrinal pivot this Topic tracks: the governing test is Glucksberg again, the methodology Kennedy had displaced has no clear successor, and the durability of Lawrence and Obergefell is a live open question. See [[Cases/Dobbs v Jackson Women's Health Organization (2022)|full brief]].

## Comparison: Level of Generality Across the Methodology Wars

| Case | Asserted Right, Broadly Framed | Asserted Right, Narrowly Framed | Which Framing Prevailed | Outcome |
|------|-------------------------------|--------------------------------|------------------------|---------|
| Michael H (1989) | Right of a natural father to a relationship with his biological child | Right of a biological father to parental rights when the child was born during another man's marriage | Narrow (Scalia plurality) | No right recognized |
| Glucksberg (1997) | Right to bodily integrity and self-determination at the end of life | Right to commit suicide with another's assistance | Narrow | No right recognized |
| Lawrence (2003) | Right to engage in private consensual adult intimate conduct | Right to engage in homosexual sodomy | Broad (Kennedy) | Bowers overruled; statute invalid |
| Obergefell (2015) | Why we protect marriage (autonomy, commitment, children, social order) applies to same-sex couples | Right to same-sex marriage rooted in historical tradition | Broad (Kennedy) | Same-sex marriage recognized |
| Dobbs (2022) | Right to bodily autonomy, reproductive self-determination | Right to obtain an abortion | Narrow (Alito) | Roe and Casey overruled |

Reading across the rows: the same methodological choice (how narrowly to describe the claimed right) predicts each outcome more reliably than any other single variable. Chandler's point is not that one framing is objectively correct, but that the choice is visible, consequential, and contested.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side

1. **Hypo:** A state statute requires every child ages 6 through 14 to attend public school, with no exception for religious or private schools. A religious private school and the parents of children enrolled in it sue, claiming the statute violates their Fourteenth Amendment liberty. **Why:** This is Pierce on nearly identical facts. Parents have a fundamental liberty interest in directing the upbringing of their children, and the state may not "standardize" its children by compelling a single form of instruction. The statute is not narrowly tailored: the state's legitimate interest in an educated citizenry can be served by regulated private schooling. Strict scrutiny, statute fails.

2. **Hypo:** A state statute authorizes state courts to order the sterilization of intellectually disabled women in state custody upon findings of parental "unfitness." No elevated procedural protections apply. A woman in custody challenges the statute. **Why:** Buck v Bell has never been explicitly overruled, but Skinner v Oklahoma and the Meyer-Pierce-Griswold line recognize procreation as a fundamental liberty interest. On modern doctrine, the asserted liberty interest (bodily integrity with respect to reproduction) is both deeply rooted and implicit in ordered liberty. Glucksberg step two easily satisfied; step one satisfied if the Court treats Skinner and Cruzan as the controlling tradition rather than Buck. Strict scrutiny, statute fails.

### Opposite-Side

3. **Hypo:** A state requires physicians who prescribe opioids to file quarterly reports with the state Department of Health cataloguing dosage, duration, and patient ID. A physician and several patients sue, claiming the reporting scheme violates a fundamental right to medical privacy. **Why:** Under Glucksberg's careful-description instruction, the claimed right is "the right to avoid state record-keeping of prescription information" at its narrowest. That framing finds no deep-rooted tradition of protection; pharmacy records have been regulated since the Progressive Era. Even framed more broadly as "medical privacy," the tradition is one of regulation for public-health purposes. Rational basis review, statute sustained.

4. **Hypo:** A state statute criminalizes the sale of lottery tickets to anyone under 21. A 19-year-old sues, claiming a fundamental right to participate in the state's economy on equal terms with adults. **Why:** The claim is not analytically different from asserting a fundamental right to economic liberty, a lineage repudiated since West Coast Hotel v Parrish. Glucksberg does not reach commercial autonomy, and the state's interest in protecting minors from gambling is the paradigm case for rational basis. Statute sustained.

### Fence-Sitter

5. **Hypo:** A state enacts a "Mature Minor Medical Decisions Act" permitting courts to override parental medical decisions upon a clear-and-convincing-evidence finding that the minor is mature and fully appreciates the consequences. An 18-year-old minor with aggressive leukemia wishes to refuse standard chemotherapy in favor of palliative care; her same-sex adoptive parents want the standard treatment; the court finds the minor mature and overrides the parents. Both the parents and the minor sue the state, each asserting a fundamental-rights theory: the parents under Meyer, Pierce, and Troxel; the minor under Cruzan and Lawrence. **Why:** Both claims have solid doctrinal footing, and the case genuinely turns on the level-of-generality move. Under a Glucksberg-narrow framing, the minor's claim is "right of a minor to refuse life-saving treatment over parental objection" (no tradition, claim fails) and the parents' claim is "right of adoptive parents to override a mature minor's medical choice" (weak tradition, claim fails). Under a Kennedy-Obergefell-broad framing, the minor's claim is "right to bodily integrity and to refuse unwanted medical intervention" (robust historical pedigree, claim succeeds) and the parents' claim is "right of fit parents to direct the rearing of their child" (unquestioned core of Meyer-Pierce-Troxel, claim succeeds). The Obergefell irony Chandler emphasizes: the same broad methodology that constituted the adoptive family now supports the child's claim against the parents. No stable doctrinal answer exists on current law; outcome depends on which Justice's opinion the court reads as controlling.

## How to Spot on an Exam

Fundamental-rights issues arrive in three recurring patterns. First, a state statute burdens a personal decision that sits near the Meyer-Pierce-Griswold-Loving-Moore line (family, marriage, contraception, bodily integrity, end-of-life decisions, intimate association, parental rights over children). Second, the claim is a novel extension of that line (a right to physician-assisted suicide, a right to medical-marijuana access, a right to polygamous marriage, a right to refuse vaccination). Third, a majority-rights opinion the student thinks is settled (Lawrence, Obergefell) is implicitly challenged by a later narrow-framing opinion (Dobbs), and the question is whether the earlier case still controls.

The analytical sequence on exam: (1) name the asserted right at both a broad and a narrow level of description, following Glucksberg's careful-description instruction; (2) run the two-part Glucksberg test at each level of description, citing the traditions that cut for and against; (3) note whether Scalia's Michael H Footnote 6 specificity rule would be dispositive, and observe that it is non-binding under Marks; (4) consider Kennedy's emerging-awareness and DP-EP synergy alternatives from Lawrence and Obergefell, citing Dobbs for the point that their continued force is contested; (5) apply the appropriate tier of scrutiny (strict if fundamental; rational basis if not); (6) for racially classified burdens, add a Loving-style Equal Protection track that insulates the claim if either foundation succeeds; (7) identify counter-doctrines that might swallow the claim (Buck v Bell remaining on the books, West Coast Hotel's repudiation of economic-liberty fundamental rights, Glucksberg's insistence on historical grounding).

Trigger phrases that should cue this Topic: "liberty interest," "deeply rooted," "ordered liberty," "careful description," "penumbras," "emerging awareness," "personal autonomy," "intimate association," "bodily integrity," "parental rights," "the right to choose." A statute that "codifies traditional morality" or "preserves historical family structures" without more will often flag a Glucksberg-narrow-framing argument for the state.

## Critique

Progressive critics (Tribe, Sunstein, Dworkin) have consistently argued that Glucksberg's narrow-framing methodology is politically selective rather than neutral. The cases that survive (marriage, procreation, parental rights) happen to be the rights the majority in power prefers; the cases that fail (assisted suicide, abortion) happen to be contested at the political margin. Brennan's circularity objection in Michael H is the same critique in technical form: if a tradition of repression counts as evidence that a right does not exist, then oppression becomes self-justifying and the Fourteenth Amendment is reduced to ratifying past majorities' preferences. Kennedy's emerging-awareness move in Lawrence and Obergefell is, on this view, a defensible alternative that treats history as a starting point for deliberation rather than a veto over current constitutional meaning.

Originalist and textualist critics (Scalia, Thomas, and more recently Alito in Dobbs) argue the opposite: without Glucksberg's narrow framing, fundamental-rights doctrine has no limiting principle and necessarily collapses into judicial imposition of majority sentiment. Thomas's Dobbs concurrence would relocate the entire enterprise to the Privileges or Immunities Clause, treating substantive due process as an "oxymoron" with no historical basis and urging reconsideration of Griswold, Lawrence, and Obergefell. Scalia's "ask the nearest hippie" jab in the Obergefell dissent is the same critique in rhetorical form: rights that "rise from a better informed understanding" are rights the Court invented, not rights the Constitution protected.

Institutional critics raise a third line of concern: the doctrine's methodological instability produces recurring litigation in substantive areas that Americans could otherwise settle politically. Each methodological turn (Footnote 6, Glucksberg, Lawrence, Obergefell, Dobbs) re-opens every adjacent area of doctrine, because the case-by-case scope of protected liberties depends on which framing the present majority prefers. On this view, the doctrine's defect is not a particular outcome but the absence of a stable decision rule, and either a consistently narrow approach (Glucksberg applied honestly) or a consistently broad approach (Kennedy's framework embraced) would be preferable to the current cycle of methodological whiplash.

Feminist and critical race scholars (MacKinnon, Harris, Roberts) have separately emphasized that the doctrine's focus on abstract liberty masks distributional realities. The Loving paradox Chandler flags is one example; Moore's racial dimension, in Brennan's concurrence, is another. Rights recognized formally at the constitutional level may remain functionally unavailable to populations that lack access to the private institutions (physicians, schools, housing markets) through which those rights are exercised. Whether substantive due process can be re-theorized to account for these distributional facts is itself an open question.

Chandler's own view, delivered across several lectures, is that methodology is outcome-determinative, that no methodology has ever achieved stable majority status, and that the Loving paradox is the strongest single reason to treat history as a floor rather than a ceiling. He nevertheless presents Dobbs as the current governing framework and expects students to be able to run the Glucksberg analysis at both levels of description.
