# Rational Basis Review

## Overview

Rational basis review is the default standard of constitutional scrutiny. Whenever a law is challenged under the Due Process or Equal Protection Clauses and the challenge does not trigger strict scrutiny (race, national origin, fundamental rights) or intermediate scrutiny (sex, legitimacy), the Court asks only whether the classification or restriction is rationally related to a legitimate government interest. The burden rests on the challenger, the state is entitled to any conceivable justification, and almost all laws survive.

The doctrine has two entry points that share one standard. In the Due Process line, rational basis is what is left of economic substantive due process after [[Cases/West Coast Hotel Co v Parrish (1937)|West Coast Hotel (1937)]] retired freedom of contract and [[Cases/United States v Carolene Products (1938)|Carolene Products (1938)]] announced the post-Lochner framework: regulation of ordinary commercial transactions gets the lowest tier of scrutiny, with heightened review reserved for specific textual guarantees, political-process restrictions, and discrete and insular minorities. In the Equal Protection line, rational basis governs every classification that the Court has not marked suspect or quasi-suspect. The two entry points converge on the same two-step test.

The structural import of the doctrine is quiet but enormous. By setting the default this low, [[Cases/United States v Carolene Products (1938)|Footnote Four]] shifted all the judicial work to the threshold question of whether heightened scrutiny applies. Once the rational basis tier is assigned, the outcome is nearly predetermined for the state; once strict scrutiny is assigned, the outcome is nearly predetermined against the state. The Court's real value judgments live in the classification of the classification, not in the application of the tier.

Three modern equal protection cases -- [[Cases/United States Department of Agriculture v Moreno (1973)|Moreno (1973)]], [[Cases/City of Cleburne v Cleburne Living Center (1985)|Cleburne (1985)]], and [[Cases/Romer v Evans (1996)|Romer (1996)]] -- have produced rare invalidations at the lowest tier. Academic and lower-court shorthand calls this "rational basis with bite." The Court has never formally conceded that a sharper standard operates, insisting the same test is being applied. But the pattern is clear: when the legislative record reveals animus or bare desire to harm a politically unpopular group, rational basis stops being the rubber-stamp Williamson set up.

---

## Governing Rule

> **Rule:** A law survives rational basis review if (i) the legislature is pursuing a legitimate government interest and (ii) the classification or restriction is rationally related to that interest. The challenger bears the burden. The state may rely on any conceivable legitimate purpose, whether or not that purpose motivated the enacting legislature. The classification need not be perfectly tailored; the legislature may address a problem one step at a time, and reform need not be all-or-nothing. A law fails rational basis only when no legitimate purpose can be identified, when the asserted purpose is pretextual, or when the legislative record discloses that the true motivation was animus toward a politically unpopular group or a bare desire to harm them.

## Doctrinal Development

### The Pre-1937 Baseline: Lochner as the Counter-Example

Before 1937, the Due Process Clause was not a deferential framework at all. [[Cases/Lochner v New York (1905)|Lochner v New York (1905)]] struck down New York's Bakeshop Act on the theory that liberty of contract was a substantive component of Fourteenth Amendment "liberty" and that courts would independently assess whether a regulation was genuinely health-and-safety legislation or a protectionist measure dressed up as such. Justice Peckham's majority treated the asserted health justification as pretextual and invalidated the sixty-hour cap as a "meddlesome interference" with the bargaining freedom of "grown and intelligent men." See [[Cases/Lochner v New York (1905)|full brief]].

Justice Holmes's dissent supplied the kernel of what rational basis would later become: "The Fourteenth Amendment does not enact Mr. Herbert Spencer's Social Statics." A constitution "is not intended to embody a particular economic theory," and a law should be upheld unless no rational person could think it consistent with the nation's legal traditions. That was a deferential standard, but it was a dissent. For the next three decades, Lochner-era doctrine remained the retail judicial evaluation of legislative wisdom that the Holmes dissent resisted.

### The 1937 Break: West Coast Hotel

[[Cases/West Coast Hotel Co v Parrish (1937)|West Coast Hotel Co v Parrish (1937)]] expressly overruled *Adkins v Children's Hospital* and retired freedom of contract as a judicially enforceable right. Chief Justice Hughes wrote that "liberty under the Constitution is not absolute and uncontrollable" but "liberty in a social organization" that requires regulation for the protection of public health, safety, morals, and welfare. Reasonable regulation, adopted in the community's interest, satisfies due process. That formulation is recognizably the modern rational basis standard in skeletal form: the state has a legitimate interest and the regulation is reasonably related to it. See [[Cases/West Coast Hotel Co v Parrish (1937)|full brief]].

### Carolene Products: The Footnote Four Architecture

The text of [[Cases/United States v Carolene Products (1938)|United States v Carolene Products (1938)]] is unremarkable. It upheld the federal Filled Milk Act on the familiar deferential framework: "Regulatory legislation affecting ordinary commercial transactions is not unconstitutional unless it is of such a character as to preclude the assumption that it rests on some rational basis within the knowledge and experience of legislators. The existence of supporting facts is to be presumed." That sentence is the positive content of rational basis review: presumption of constitutionality, conceivable supporting facts, and a character-of-the-legislation threshold that is almost impossible to fail.

The constitutional significance of the case lies in Footnote Four. Justice Stone in three sentences laid out the exceptions that would come to define the entire scrutiny architecture. Legislation "within a specific prohibition of the Constitution, such as those of the first ten Amendments," may be subject to "a narrower presumption of constitutionality." Legislation restricting "those political processes which can ordinarily be expected to bring about repeal of undesirable legislation" warrants "more exacting judicial scrutiny." Legislation directed at "particular religious, or national, or racial minorities" -- or, more broadly, at "discrete and insular minorities" -- warrants heightened review because "prejudice against discrete and insular minorities may be a special condition which tends seriously to curtail the operation of those political processes ordinarily to be relied upon to protect minorities." See [[Cases/United States v Carolene Products (1938)|full brief]].

Footnote Four thus defined rational basis by defining what it is not. Economic regulation of ordinary commercial transactions is the default. Everything outside that default is the site of heightened scrutiny. The rest of equal protection and incorporation doctrine is built on this single footnote.

### Williamson v Lee Optical: Rational Basis as Near-Impossibility

[[Cases/Williamson v Lee Optical of Oklahoma (1955)|Williamson v Lee Optical of Oklahoma (1955)]] cemented the modern deferential approach. Oklahoma required a prescription from an optometrist or ophthalmologist before an optician could fit lenses to new frames, a classic piece of protectionist legislation favoring optometrists over opticians. The Court upheld the statute because "the law need not be in every respect logically consistent with its aims to be constitutional. It is enough that there is an evil at hand for correction, and that it might be thought that the particular legislative measure was a rational way to correct it." On the equal protection challenge, the Court added the one-step-at-a-time principle: "Evils in the same field may be of different dimensions and proportions, requiring different remedies. Or so the legislature may think. The legislature may select one phase of one field and apply a remedy there, neglecting the others." See [[Cases/Williamson v Lee Optical of Oklahoma (1955)|full brief]].

Williamson sets the ceiling of the doctrine's deference. Any articulable legitimate purpose, however speculative, will support the law. The legislature need not be consistent; it need not address all related evils; it need not even have identified the justification later offered by the state's lawyers. Williamson is the case that makes rational basis, as the class recap lecture puts it, "the 'unless it is completely bonkers' standard."

### Washington v Davis: Purpose over Impact

[[Cases/Washington v Davis (1976)|Washington v Davis (1976)]] imports the deference structure into the equal protection analysis of facially neutral laws. A facially neutral statute with disproportionate racial impact does not trigger strict scrutiny absent proof of discriminatory purpose. In the absence of that proof, the statute is tested under rational basis. The D.C. police verbal-skills examination cleared that low bar because it was "neutral on its face and rationally may be said to serve a purpose the Government is constitutionally empowered to pursue." Washington v Davis thus restricts the doctrinal openings through which disparate-impact plaintiffs could escape rational basis, reinforcing the structural point that the tier assignment is where the action is. See [[Cases/Washington v Davis (1976)|full brief]].

### Rational Basis With Bite: Cleburne and Romer

The Court has occasionally struck laws at the rational basis tier. The pattern of those rare invalidations is consistent: each involves a legislative record that the Court reads as disclosing animus or a bare desire to harm a politically unpopular group.

[[Cases/City of Cleburne v Cleburne Living Center (1985)|City of Cleburne v Cleburne Living Center (1985)]] refused to treat disability as a quasi-suspect classification and expressly applied rational basis. The Court then invalidated Cleburne's denial of a special-use permit for a group home for the mentally retarded because the city's stated justifications (neighbors' fears, distance to a 500-year floodplain, concerns about junior high students nearby) "appear to us to rest on an irrational prejudice against the mentally retarded." Bare negative attitudes, even if widely shared, are not a legitimate government interest. See [[Cases/City of Cleburne v Cleburne Living Center (1985)|full brief]].

[[Cases/Romer v Evans (1996)|Romer v Evans (1996)]] struck Colorado's Amendment 2, a state constitutional provision barring any level of state government from protecting gays and lesbians against discrimination. The Court applied rational basis and held that a law that withdraws from one identifiable group the right to seek legal protections that remain available to everyone else "bears no rational relationship to any legitimate governmental interest" and is "born of animosity toward the class of persons affected." Justice Kennedy's opinion made the doctrinal move explicit: a law enacted solely to harm a politically unpopular group cannot survive even rational basis. See [[Cases/Romer v Evans (1996)|full brief]].

The lectures and the class recap treat Moreno (food stamps exclusion of households containing unrelated persons), Cleburne, and Romer as the canonical trio of rational-basis-with-bite cases. Each turned on a legislative record that the Court read as animus. The Court has never named this a separate tier. The Lochnerism-critique tradition reads the cluster as proof that the Court is still doing retail review when it is motivated enough to.

## Comparison: Ordinary Rational Basis vs Rational Basis With Bite

The Court formally applies a single standard. In practice, the pattern of outcomes suggests two operational modes. The table makes the operational distinction explicit without endorsing the claim that a separate tier exists.

| Axis | Ordinary Rational Basis | Rational Basis With Bite |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Canonical exemplars | [[Cases/Williamson v Lee Optical of Oklahoma (1955)\|Williamson v Lee Optical (1955)]]; [[Cases/United States v Carolene Products (1938)\|Carolene Products (1938)]]; Railway Express Agency v New York (1949); FCC v Beach Communications (1993); Heller v Doe (1993) | [[Cases/United States Department of Agriculture v Moreno (1973)\|USDA v Moreno (1973)]]; [[Cases/City of Cleburne v Cleburne Living Center (1985)\|City of Cleburne (1985)]]; [[Cases/Romer v Evans (1996)\|Romer v Evans (1996)]] |
| Operational burden | Nearly unbearable for challenger; state need not even identify the actual legislative purpose | Real, if unacknowledged; legislative record can disqualify a purpose the state wants to rely on |
| Treatment of conceivable purposes | Any conceivable legitimate purpose will do; post-hoc rationalizations permitted | The Court examines the record for signs of animus; purposes that contradict the record are rejected |
| Treatment of underinclusiveness and overinclusiveness | Tolerated freely; legislature may address a problem one step at a time | Still formally tolerated, but the pattern of under- or overinclusion becomes evidence of animus |
| Typical subject matter | Economic regulation; professional licensing; ordinary commercial legislation | Classifications that disadvantage politically unpopular groups outside suspect and quasi-suspect classes |
| Announced standard | Rational relationship to a legitimate interest | Same language, same formal test |
| Actual decision rule | If any rational basis is conceivable, the law stands | A bare desire to harm a politically unpopular group is not a legitimate interest |
| Frequency | Near-universal upholding | Rare; perhaps three or four cases in fifty years |
| Doctrinal honesty claim | Court: applying deference consistently | Scholars: the Court is applying Lochnerism in disguise when it cares about the group |

The framework becomes important on exam hypotheticals involving novel classifications (age, income, disability outside Cleburne facts, non-marital family structures). The default should be that rational basis applies and the law survives; the Romer-Cleburne-Moreno line is available only where the record discloses animus or bare desire to harm. Conclusory assertions of animus unsupported by the record will not move the case out of ordinary rational basis.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side

1. **Hypo:** A state enacts a statute requiring every licensed cosmetologist to complete 1,500 hours of supervised training in a school approved by the state board before sitting for the licensing exam. A challenger operating an African hair-braiding salon sues, alleging that 95 percent of the mandated curriculum is irrelevant to hair braiding and that the requirement exists only to protect incumbent cosmetology schools. **Why:** The law is economic regulation subject to rational basis after West Coast Hotel and Williamson. Protectionist motives do not defeat rational basis; the legislature is entitled to any conceivable legitimate purpose, and consumer-protection rationales (hygiene, infection control, basic chemistry of hair products) are available. The disconnect between the curriculum and braiding is exactly the kind of underinclusiveness the Court tolerated in Williamson's optician statute. Upheld.

2. **Hypo:** Congress enacts a tax credit for small businesses that purchase domestically manufactured software, phased out at higher revenue levels. A large multinational challenges the distinction on equal protection grounds, arguing that there is no rational reason to exclude large businesses from the credit. **Why:** Commercial regulation of ordinary economic transactions; rational basis applies. The "one step at a time" rule from Williamson permits Congress to address perceived gaps in small-business access to domestic software markets without simultaneously addressing large-business needs. Carolene Products tells the Court to presume the existence of supporting facts. Upheld.

### Opposite-Side

3. **Hypo:** A city council, responding to constituent complaints about a proposed halfway house for formerly incarcerated persons, amends its zoning ordinance to require a special-use permit for residential facilities housing "persons convicted of any felony" but not for other group living arrangements. The record of the council meeting shows members repeatedly describing the future residents as "criminals we do not want in our neighborhood." A plaintiff challenges under the Equal Protection Clause. **Why:** The facts track Cleburne closely. Formerly incarcerated persons are not a suspect class, so rational basis applies. But the legislative record resembles the Cleburne record: bare negative attitudes toward a politically unpopular group, not any legitimate government interest. Under Cleburne and Romer, a law "born of animosity" toward a disfavored group fails even rational basis. Struck.

4. **Hypo:** A state adopts a constitutional amendment barring any political subdivision from enacting non-discrimination protections on the basis of immigration status. Legitimate local measures to protect undocumented residents from discrimination in housing, employment, and public accommodation are swept away. **Why:** This is Romer's structure applied to a new group. The amendment withdraws from an identifiable group the right to seek ordinary legal protections that remain available to everyone else. Romer's holding is not limited to sexual orientation by its reasoning; it turns on the structural disability the measure imposes and on the inference of animus. Even under rational basis, a law aimed at disabling a politically unpopular group from ordinary political participation is not rationally related to a legitimate interest. Struck.

### Fence-Sitter

5. **Hypo:** A state revokes its previous recognition of homeopathic practitioners as licensed health providers and requires all new applicants to complete an accredited MD or DO program. Existing homeopathic practitioners are grandfathered for ten years, after which they must retrain or cease practice. A homeopathic-practitioner trade association sues under the Due Process and Equal Protection Clauses, alleging that the new rule is driven entirely by the medical lobby. **Why:** The law is economic regulation, and under Williamson, protectionist motivation does not defeat rational basis. Consumer-protection rationales are available. But the ten-year phase-out plus the total exclusion thereafter creates a stronger case of legislative animus than Williamson faced, and the trade association may introduce legislative-record evidence that members repeatedly dismissed homeopathy as "quackery" rather than as unsafe. The case sits between ordinary Williamson-style deference and Cleburne-style rational basis with bite. The likely outcome is upholding under Williamson's conceivable-basis rule, because the Court has been reluctant to extend rational basis with bite beyond Moreno, Cleburne, and Romer, and because consumer-protection rationales for medical licensing are plainly available. But a sufficiently ugly legislative record could flip the result if the Court reads it as Romer rather than Williamson.

## How to Spot on an Exam

The scrutiny question is the first question, and rational basis is the answer to it much more often than students expect.

1. **Identify the constitutional clause.** The doctrine operates under both the Due Process Clause (economic regulation, non-fundamental rights) and the Equal Protection Clause (non-suspect and non-quasi-suspect classifications). The operating standard is the same, but the domains are different.

2. **Rule out heightened scrutiny.** Before applying rational basis, run the heightened-scrutiny checks. Is the law a racial, national-origin, or alienage classification (strict scrutiny)? A sex or legitimacy classification (intermediate scrutiny)? Does it burden a fundamental right (strict scrutiny on the substantive-due-process side)? If none of those applies, rational basis governs.

3. **Be ready to say so.** The most common exam error is to run a heightened-scrutiny analysis because the plaintiffs sound sympathetic. A disability, age, or wealth classification is rational-basis territory even if the plaintiffs are vulnerable. The tier question is a doctrinal question, not a normative one.

4. **Apply the two-step test.** Legitimate government interest and rational relationship. Remember that the state may rely on any conceivable purpose and need not establish the actual legislative motive. Remember that underinclusive and overinclusive laws survive. Remember that the challenger bears the burden.

5. **Know when rational basis bites.** If the legislative record discloses animus or bare desire to harm a politically unpopular group, the Moreno-Cleburne-Romer line is available. Look for: a classification that singles out an identifiable group, a record of disparaging statements about that group, a lack of any plausible legitimate purpose, or a structural disability that blocks the group from ordinary political participation. These cases are rare; conclusory assertions of animus will not do.

**Exam trap: the conceivable-basis rule.** The state is not confined to the justifications actually offered in the legislative record. Writing "the legislature did not consider that interest" does not defeat rational basis. Frame the analysis around what rationales are available, not what rationales were offered.

**Exam trap: Footnote Four as outcome-determinative.** The tier-assignment question is where the Court's real work happens. Do not bury the Footnote Four analysis in a footnote. State the tier, justify the tier, and then apply the tier.

## Critique

The progressive critique, most closely associated with process-theory descendants of Ely, reads Carolene Products and Williamson as correct in retiring retail judicial review of economic legislation and correct in insisting that tiers of scrutiny track political-process pathology rather than judicial preference. On this view, the Moreno-Cleburne-Romer line of rational basis with bite is either (a) a legitimate application of the animus exception that the ordinary rational basis formulation already contained, or (b) a tacit admission that political-process theory cannot do the work of protecting groups that the Court believes are being subordinated. The Equal Protection Theory lecture presses this point hard: anti-subordination theorists (Fiss, Balkin, Siegel) argue that Cleburne and Romer show the doctrine's real machinery, which is substantive judgment about group status, not formal application of a tier.

The originalist and textualist critique, associated with Thomas, Scalia, and the anti-Footnote-Four tradition, reads modern rational basis review as a well-intentioned mistake. On this view, the Fourteenth Amendment was ratified to protect newly freed slaves against specific forms of state-imposed subordination, and the tiered-scrutiny architecture is a judicial invention that has no grounding in the Clause's text or original meaning. The critique has two operational branches. One branch urges retiring tiers altogether in favor of history-and-tradition analysis; Bruen's Second Amendment methodology is an early instance. The other branch urges a narrower return to the Clause's original meaning, which would excise both the displaced Lochnerism at the tier-assignment stage and the rational-basis-with-bite line at the application stage.

The doctrinal-pragmatic critique, drawn out most clearly in the Equal Protection Theory lecture's four-framework presentation, argues that rational basis review is structurally under-theorized. The Court's official position is that a single standard applies uniformly. The actual pattern of outcomes shows that the Court applies a different standard when it disapproves of the classification. The costs of that mismatch are steep: legislatures cannot predict which classifications will bite back, litigants cannot calibrate their legislative-record strategies, and lower courts receive inconsistent signals about what evidence of animus suffices. The proposed solutions are either to formalize rational basis with bite as a distinct tier or to replace the tiered architecture with a single proportionality framework.

The displaced-Lochnerism critique, advanced in both the Lochner and Lochnerism lecture and the class recap, pushes the hardest. The argument is that Lochner was wrong because unelected judges should not second-guess economic policy, and rational basis review was designed to retire that form of judicial value judgment. But the tier-assignment stage relocates the value judgment from retail (this particular law) to wholesale (this type of law). Each tier is a Bayesian prior on unconstitutionality, and the assignment of the prior is itself a judicial bet that a certain kind of law is probably a bad idea. That is Lochnerism at one level of abstraction up. The BMW v Gore punitive-damages line and the rational-basis-with-bite line are, on this reading, Lochnerism at the application stage seeping back through. The hard question is whether Footnote Four provides a principled distinction between displaced Lochnerism (heightened review for textually guaranteed rights and process-excluded minorities) and illegitimate Lochnerism (heightened review for what judges happen to prefer). The class recap lecture treats that question as genuinely open.
