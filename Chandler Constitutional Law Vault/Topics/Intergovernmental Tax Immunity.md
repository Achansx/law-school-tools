# Intergovernmental Tax Immunity

## Overview

Intergovernmental tax immunity is the constitutional doctrine, grounded in the Supremacy Clause (Article VI), that limits the power of one sovereign within our federal system to tax or financially burden the instrumentalities or operations of another. The doctrine originated in [[Cases/McCulloch v Maryland (1819)|McCulloch v Maryland]] (1819) and has evolved from a broadly reciprocal immunity into a modern rule that is largely asymmetric: states are still barred from discriminatorily taxing federal instrumentalities, but the federal government may tax state activities and employees on a non-discriminatory basis.

The foundational logic is Marshall's axiomatic proposition that "the power to tax involves the power to destroy." If a state could impose a tax on a federal instrumentality, it could impose a tax sufficient to eliminate that instrumentality, thereby controlling -- and potentially defeating -- a power that Congress had constitutionally exercised. Because the federal government is "supreme within its sphere of action" under the Supremacy Clause, state taxation that operates on that sphere is unconstitutional.

The doctrine belongs within the broader [[Topics/Federalism|Federalism]] structure. It answers one of the specific state-federal friction points: not whether the federal government can act (the [[Topics/Necessary and Proper Clause|Necessary and Proper Clause]] question) but whether a state can financially hamper what the federal government has already constitutionally decided to do. Its modern form reflects a significant narrowing from the nineteenth-century high point: the Court has rejected the idea of symmetrical immunity and has confined the state-side bar to discriminatory or specifically targeted taxation.

---

## Governing Rule

> **McCulloch Tax Immunity Rule (1819):** States may not impose a tax that singles out a federal instrumentality for a discriminatory burden. A state tax whose legal incidence falls on the operations of a federal entity, imposed precisely because the entity is a federal creation, is unconstitutional under the Supremacy Clause. The power to tax is the power to destroy, and a state that could tax a federal instrumentality to extinction could nullify a power Congress constitutionally exercised.

> **McCulloch Carve-Out:** Non-discriminatory, generally applicable taxation remains permissible even when it reaches federal entities. A state property tax applied to all real property within its borders at a uniform rate -- including a parcel owned by a federal instrumentality -- does not violate the doctrine. The vice of Maryland's law in McCulloch was the discriminatory singling out of a federal entity, not taxation as such.

> **Modern Asymmetry:** As the doctrine evolved through the twentieth century, the Court abandoned the proposition that intergovernmental tax immunity is perfectly reciprocal. The federal government may tax state employees and state bond interest on the same terms it taxes equivalent private activities. The state-side prohibition on discriminatory taxation of federal instrumentalities survives; the federal-side immunity of state entities from federal taxation does not.

## Doctrinal Development

### McCulloch v Maryland (1819): The Foundation

The doctrine was born in the second holding of [[Cases/McCulloch v Maryland (1819)|McCulloch v Maryland]]. Maryland imposed a tax on all banks not chartered by the state -- a measure targeting the Baltimore branch of the Second Bank of the United States. McCulloch, the cashier, refused to comply. After upholding Congress's power to charter the Bank under the Necessary and Proper Clause, Marshall turned to Maryland's taxation.

The structural argument ran: if the states of the Union could tax a constitutional federal instrumentality, the power that Congress had exercised would be subject to destruction at the hands of any state. The people of one state, acting through their legislature, would effectively be taxing the people of all other states, who had no voice in that legislature. More fundamentally, allowing states to tax federal operations would invert the federal supremacy the Framers established. The Supremacy Clause makes federal law "the supreme Law of the Land," and a state tax designed to impede federal operations is incompatible with that supremacy.

Marshall was careful to draw the line at discriminatory targeting. The tax immune from state power is the one that singles out the federal entity: "the states have no power, by taxation or otherwise, to retard, impede, burden, or in any manner control, the operations of the constitutional laws enacted by Congress." Ordinary property taxes applied to the Bank's real estate in common with all other real property within Maryland, and taxes on citizens' interests in the Bank, were expressly preserved. See [[Cases/McCulloch v Maryland (1819)|full brief]].

### The Nineteenth-Century Expansion: Reciprocal Immunity

The Court initially read McCulloch as establishing a symmetrical principle. The [[Lectures/McCulloch v Maryland - Federal Powers|McCulloch lecture]] notes that the opinion's tax-immunity holding was linked in later doctrine to "modern intergovernmental immunities doctrine," including a period of reciprocal immunity in which the protection ran both directions. Collector v Day (1871) is the leading case for that reciprocal phase, holding that McCulloch's logic applied equally against federal taxation of state governmental functions. This created a zone of mutual immunity that would prove administratively unworkable in the modern tax state.

### The New Deal Contraction: Graves v New York (1939) and Asymmetric Immunity

The New Deal era brought a sharp contraction of reciprocal immunity. The cases from this period, anchored by Graves v New York ex rel O'Keefe (1939), abandoned the symmetrical immunity theory. The key move was to pivot away from formal-sovereignty reasoning and toward the practical question of burden: a generally applicable tax applied evenhandedly to state employees does not "retard, impede, burden, or in any manner control" state governmental operations in the constitutionally relevant sense, because the control concern is about sovereignty discrimination, not the incidence of a neutral revenue law.

After Graves, the doctrine became asymmetric. The state-side bar on discriminatory taxation of federal instrumentalities remained intact, because it rests on the Supremacy Clause text and on Marshall's structural argument that a state cannot financially incapacitate an entity Congress constitutionally created. But the reciprocal federal-side immunity -- the idea that state entities enjoy blanket protection from generally applicable federal taxes -- was abandoned.

### South Carolina v Baker (1988): The Modern Statement

South Carolina v Baker restated the modern asymmetric doctrine and confirmed that the federal government may tax state activities on a non-discriminatory basis. The case is not yet briefed in this vault (see the key_cases placeholder above). Its significance is that it marks the endpoint of the doctrinal arc away from broad reciprocal immunity: states retain protection against discriminatorily targeted federal taxation, but the general principle of federal-side immunity over state bond interest and similar state revenue instruments did not survive.

## The Doctrinal Arc: From Symmetrical to Asymmetric Immunity

| Era | State-Side Rule | Federal-Side Rule | Key Case |
|-----|----------------|-------------------|----------|
| McCulloch (1819) | States may not discriminatorily tax federal instrumentalities | Ambiguous (McCulloch decided only the state-side) | McCulloch v Maryland |
| Post-Collector v Day (1871) | States may not discriminatorily tax federal instrumentalities | Federal government may not tax state governmental functions (reciprocal immunity; later abandoned) | Collector v Day (no vault brief) |
| Post-Graves (1939) | States may not discriminatorily tax federal instrumentalities | Federal government may tax state entities under generally applicable laws; Collector v Day abandoned | Graves v New York (no vault brief) |
| Modern (post-1988) | States may not discriminatorily tax federal instrumentalities | Federal government may tax state revenue instruments on non-discriminatory basis; federal-side immunity dismantled | South Carolina v Baker (no vault brief) |

The arc runs from broad reciprocal immunity (mid-nineteenth century) to the modern asymmetric rule: the state-side bar is strong because it rests on Supremacy Clause text and on Marshall's structural argument about federal self-preservation; the federal-side bar has been progressively dismantled because it lacks comparable textual grounding and because Congress, representing all the people, cannot "oppress" states through a generally applicable tax in the same structural sense.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side

1. **Hypo:** Congress establishes a federal student-loan corporation to administer federal education lending, and a state imposes an annual licensing fee of $500,000 on banks not chartered by the state -- a category that includes the federal corporation but not any state-chartered bank. **Why:** The fee is identical in structure to Maryland's tax in McCulloch: a facially neutral law whose practical incidence falls exclusively on a federal instrumentality. Under the McCulloch rule, the discriminatory targeting of a federal entity violates the Supremacy Clause regardless of whether the state labels it a "tax" or a "fee." The power to impose such a burden is the power to destroy the entity.

2. **Hypo:** A state legislature enacts a statute imposing a 10% annual surcharge on the operating revenues of any federally chartered financial institution doing business in the state. No equivalent surcharge applies to state-chartered banks. **Why:** The surcharge discriminatorily burdens federal instrumentalities as such. It does not apply to similarly situated state-chartered institutions, so it cannot be defended as a generally applicable tax on business revenues. Under McCulloch, states may not single out federal entities for burdens that do not fall on comparable private or state entities.

### Opposite-Side

3. **Hypo:** A state assesses a general real-property tax at the same millage rate applied to all commercial real estate within the jurisdiction, and the assessment reaches a parcel of land owned by the federal Post Office. **Why:** Marshall expressly carved out this scenario in McCulloch. States retain the power to tax real property of federal entities "in common with the other real property within the State." The tax is not discriminatory; it does not single out the federal entity. The federal Post Office's land is taxed exactly as any other commercial parcel would be.

4. **Hypo:** Congress enacts a generally applicable federal income tax that applies to the wages of all employees in the United States, including employees of state governments. A state argues that taxing its employees' salaries burdens state operations. **Why:** Under the modern asymmetric doctrine (as the McCulloch lecture describes the post-McCulloch evolution), a generally applicable federal tax applied evenly to state and private employees alike does not "retard, impede, burden, or in any manner control" state governmental operations in the constitutionally relevant sense. The intergovernmental tax immunity that survives today runs one direction: it protects federal instrumentalities from discriminatory state taxation. The reciprocal protection of state employees from generally applicable federal taxation was abandoned in the New Deal era.

### Fence-Sitter

5. **Hypo:** A state imposes a franchise fee on all businesses operating within its borders, calculated as a percentage of gross receipts, with no explicit exemption for federal entities. The fee applies to a federal telecommunications contractor whose work is entirely in support of Defense Department operations. The contractor, not the United States, is the legal taxpayer. **Why:** The analysis is genuinely uncertain. McCulloch's immunity runs to the federal instrumentality itself; fees whose legal incidence falls on a private contractor may pass scrutiny even if the economic burden is ultimately passed to the federal government. But if the state's purpose is to impede a federal contract program, and the fee is calculated in a way that effectively targets federal-contract work, an argument exists that it indirectly retards federal operations. Courts have generally sustained taxes on private federal contractors so long as the federal entity is not the legal taxpayer, but the "in any manner control the operations" language from McCulloch leaves room for an argument at the margin.

## How to Spot on an Exam

**Trigger:** A state government taxes, licenses, or otherwise financially burdens an entity, operation, or program that is federal in nature -- or a federal law is challenged for taxing state employees, officials, or activities.

**Framework:**
1. Identify the direction: is it a state burdening a federal entity, or the federal government taxing a state entity?
2. For the state-side question: (a) Is the legal incidence of the tax on the federal instrumentality itself? (b) Is the tax discriminatory -- does it single out the federal entity or apply generally? A discriminatory, targeted tax on a federal instrumentality fails under McCulloch. A generally applicable tax that happens to reach federal real estate survives under McCulloch's carve-out.
3. For the federal-side question: Under the modern asymmetric doctrine, a generally applicable federal tax that reaches state employees or state bond interest is presumptively constitutional. The federal-side immunity has been effectively dismantled.
4. Red herring: Arguments that "both sovereigns must be equal" invoke the abandoned reciprocal-immunity theory from the nineteenth century (Collector v Day, later overruled). Modern doctrine is not symmetrical; the federal-side immunity has been dismantled through the New Deal-era cases.

**Common exam moves:** Professor Chandler's emphasis (per the McCulloch lecture) is on the connection between the tax-immunity holding and the broader structural point -- the Supremacy Clause does not just resolve conflicts between federal and state statutes, it also bars states from financially incapacitating what the federal government has constitutionally undertaken. The exam is likely to test whether students apply McCulloch's discriminatory-targeting test rather than a blanket immunity for all federal activity.

## Critique

The intergovernmental tax immunity doctrine has drawn sustained criticism from multiple directions.

**The "power to destroy" maxim proves too much.** Marshall's aphorism is rhetorically powerful but logically overinclusive. As Justice Holmes observed in Panhandle Oil Co. v Mississippi (cited in the McCulloch brief's Critique section): "the power to tax is not the power to destroy while this Court sits." Any generally applicable tax creates some friction for its subjects, yet generally applicable taxes on federal contractors, federal employees' private expenditures, and federal real estate are all concededly constitutional. The real principle is not that taxation destroys but that discriminatory taxation aimed at a federal entity is incompatible with federal supremacy. The broad formulation obscures the narrow truth.

**The asymmetry is structurally unstable.** The modern doctrine is asymmetric: states cannot discriminatorily tax federal instrumentalities, but the federal government can tax state entities and their employees on generally applicable terms. The asymmetry is justified on the theory that Congress represents all the people and cannot "oppress" states the way a single state legislature can oppress a federal entity operating within its territory. But this structural argument has limits: Congress can and does impose burdens on states that are felt asymmetrically. The abandonment of reciprocal immunity in the New Deal era was a pragmatic response to the impossibility of administering broad mutual immunity in a modern tax state -- as Justice Holmes had anticipated by observing that "the power to tax is not the power to destroy while this Court sits" -- not a principled resolution of the underlying structural problem. Originalists have questioned whether the Founding-era evidence supports the asymmetric modern rule rather than something closer to the reciprocal-immunity regime the nineteenth-century Court initially established.

**The carve-out for non-discriminatory taxation is difficult to administer.** Determining what counts as "discriminatory targeting" of a federal entity requires courts to look through formal neutrality. A tax that applies to "all banks not chartered by Maryland" is formally neutral but practically targets only the Bank of the United States -- which is exactly what McCulloch held unconstitutional. Courts must examine practical incidence and purpose, not just statutory text. This creates administrability problems: how much of the tax burden must fall on federal entities before the tax becomes "discriminatory"? The doctrine has not generated a precise quantitative threshold.

**The immunity extends beyond the federal constitutional requirement in some contexts.** Federal statutes, not just constitutional doctrine, often extend immunity to federal contractors and agents. This statutory layer sometimes creates confusion about whether a given immunity rule is constitutionally required (under McCulloch) or merely a legislative policy choice that Congress could repeal. Cases involving government contractors operating on government property illustrate the complexity.
