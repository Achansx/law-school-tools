# Establishment Clause

## Overview

The Establishment Clause of the First Amendment, incorporated against the states through the Fourteenth Amendment Due Process Clause by Everson v Board of Education (1947), prohibits government action that formally establishes a religion or aids religion in a way that compromises the neutrality the Clause requires. The central question in modern doctrine is not whether the Clause has been violated in a single narrow sense but what framework governs the inquiry: a mechanical three-prong test, a reasonable-observer endorsement inquiry, a coercion-focused analysis, a totality-of-circumstances balancing of context and history, or a historical-practices and understandings test.

For roughly fifty years after 1971, the controlling framework was the three-part test announced in Lemon v Kurtzman: a challenged law or practice must have a secular legislative purpose, a primary effect that neither advances nor inhibits religion, and no excessive entanglement between government and religion. Over the next three decades the Court retreated from Lemon in stages. Agostini v Felton (1997) collapsed the three prongs into a two-step inquiry. Zelman v Simmons-Harris (2002) carved out a wide private-choice exception for indirect aid that reaches religious schools through parents' independent choices. Van Orden v Perry (2005) produced a fractured decision in which Justice Breyer's controlling concurrence openly declined to apply any single test, substituting a totality-of-circumstances balancing grounded in the monument's 40-year undisturbed history. American Legion v American Humanist Association (2019) announced a presumption of constitutionality for longstanding monuments. Kennedy v Bremerton School District (2022) then formally abandoned Lemon and directed lower courts to interpret the Establishment Clause by reference to "historical practices and understandings."

The current doctrinal position is therefore a tradition-anchored inquiry: government actions that align with practices the Founding generation and early Republic accepted as compatible with non-establishment are presumptively constitutional; actions that deviate from that tradition, or that coerce religious participation in the Lee v Weisman sense, remain prohibited. Lemon's three prongs still influence lower courts by citation habit and by the weight of fifty years of precedent, but they no longer control.

## Governing Rule

> **Rule (current, post-Kennedy v Bremerton, 2022):** The Establishment Clause is interpreted by reference to historical practices and understandings. A government action that aligns with practices the Founding generation recognized as consistent with non-establishment is constitutional; a practice that departs from that historical baseline, or that coerces religious participation, is not. Historical practice is the primary framework; the separate prohibition on coerced religious observance survives independently of Lemon's abandonment. (*Kennedy v Bremerton School District*, 2022)

> **Rule (superseded but still cited, Lemon framework, 1971 to 2022):** A statute satisfies the Establishment Clause if (1) it has a secular legislative purpose, (2) its principal or primary effect neither advances nor inhibits religion, and (3) it does not foster excessive government entanglement with religion. A failure on any single prong renders the law unconstitutional. (*Lemon v Kurtzman*, 1971)

> **Rule (private-choice refinement that survives into the new framework):** Aid that reaches religious institutions through the genuine and independent choices of private individuals does not constitute direct government aid for Establishment Clause purposes. A formally neutral program that provides benefits to a broad class of recipients, and allows those recipients to direct the aid to religious or secular options on an equal footing, does not violate the Establishment Clause even if in practice most aid reaches religious institutions. (*Zelman v Simmons-Harris*, 2002; *Agostini v Felton*, 1997)

> **Rule (coercion prohibition, independent of framework):** Government may not coerce anyone to support or participate in religion or its exercise, including through subtle social or psychological pressure applied in a setting of formal government authority. (*Lee v Weisman*, 1992; preserved by *Kennedy v Bremerton*, 2022)

## Doctrinal Development

### Everson and Incorporation (Foundational)

Everson v Board of Education (1947) incorporated the Establishment Clause against the states through the Fourteenth Amendment Due Process Clause and, while upholding New Jersey's reimbursement of parents for bus transportation to parochial schools, announced the wall-of-separation metaphor that framed the next half-century of doctrine. Justice Black's opinion declared that neither a state nor the federal government could pass laws aiding one religion, all religions, or preferring one over another; the "wall between Church and State" must be kept high and impregnable. The case is foundational rather than doctrinally sharp: it upheld the challenged program on a benefit-neutrality theory while articulating a principle that supported far stricter separation in later cases.

### Lemon: The Three-Prong Synthesis (1971)

Lemon v Kurtzman synthesized the Court's prior Establishment Clause cases into a single rule of decision. Chief Justice Burger distilled Walz v Tax Commission (1970), Board of Education v Allen (1968), Abington v Schempp (1963), and Everson into three prongs that a challenged statute must satisfy: a secular legislative purpose, a primary effect that neither advances nor inhibits religion, and no excessive government entanglement with religion. Applying that framework, the Court struck down Pennsylvania's salary-and-materials reimbursement program and Rhode Island's 15-percent teacher salary supplement for nonpublic schools. The decisive ground was excessive entanglement: because parochial schools are pervasively sectarian and teachers' professional conduct cannot be audited once and certified like a textbook, state verification that public funds went only to secular instruction would require continuing surveillance of religious institutions. That surveillance, plus the annual political battles over religious-aid appropriations, produced exactly the church-state fusion the Clause forbids. See [[Cases/Lemon v Kurtzman (1971)|full brief]].

### Agostini and Zelman: The Private-Choice Softening (1997 to 2002)

Agostini v Felton (1997) partially overruled Aguilar v Felton (1985) and held that public employees providing Title I remedial services on parochial-school premises, under strict protocols prohibiting religious activity, did not violate the Establishment Clause. The majority collapsed Lemon's three prongs into a two-step inquiry: secular purpose plus a refined primary-effect inquiry that folded entanglement concerns into the effect analysis and softened them with a presumption that public employees would not inculcate religion absent evidence to the contrary. Zelman v Simmons-Harris (2002) upheld Ohio's Cleveland voucher program even though over 96 percent of participating families chose religious schools. The majority reasoned that because the aid reached religious schools only through the independent choices of individual parents, the government had not itself advanced religion; formal neutrality and genuine private choice together broke the direct-aid chain that had doomed the Lemon and Rhode Island programs. Zelman's private-choice framework survives unchanged into the new historical-practices regime because it operates at a logically prior step: aid directed by private choice is not government aid for Establishment Clause purposes at all.

### Van Orden and the Breakup of Lemon (2005)

Van Orden v Perry (2005) produced a fractured 5 to 4 decision with no majority opinion. Chief Justice Rehnquist's four-Justice plurality argued that Lemon was inappropriate for passive, longstanding monuments and would have substituted a tolerable-acknowledgment framework grounded in the nation's tradition of government recognition of religious heritage. Justice Breyer's controlling concurrence declined to apply any single test, instead asking whether the monument, in its specific context, carried a predominantly religious purpose or effect. Breyer identified four features that weighed in favor of constitutionality: the monument had stood 40 years without litigation, it was donated by a civic organization (the Fraternal Order of Eagles), it was one of 17 monuments on the Texas Capitol grounds, and it was passive rather than didactic. Same-day companion McCreary County v ACLU of Kentucky (2005) came out the other way on courthouse displays of the Ten Commandments, the same Justice Breyer providing the dispositive vote on opposite facts: newly erected, repeatedly modified under litigation pressure, accompanied by legislative resolutions explicitly endorsing the displays' religious purpose. Read together, Van Orden and McCreary announced that Establishment Clause analysis of religious displays would turn on totality-of-circumstances facts rather than any doctrinal test capable of ex ante application. See [[Cases/Van Orden v Perry (2005)|full brief]].

### American Legion: The Longstanding-Monument Presumption (2019)

American Legion v American Humanist Association (2019) upheld the Bladensburg Peace Cross, a 40-foot cross on public land commemorating World War I dead. The plurality announced a presumption of constitutionality for longstanding monuments: the passage of time raises the likelihood that a religiously themed monument serves multiple purposes beyond religious endorsement (memorial, historical, civic), and removal would project hostility to the religious tradition the monument embodies rather than neutrality. American Legion narrowed but did not eliminate Lemon's continuing role in new-erection cases; it also tightened the doctrinal path from Van Orden's ad hoc context analysis toward the tradition-anchored inquiry Kennedy v Bremerton would later adopt as the general framework.

### Kennedy v Bremerton: Lemon Formally Abandoned (2022)

Kennedy v Bremerton School District (2022) formally abandoned the Lemon test, including its endorsement-test refinement, and directed lower courts to interpret the Establishment Clause "by reference to historical practices and understandings." Joseph Kennedy, an assistant football coach at a public high school, had been placed on administrative leave after continuing to kneel in brief personal prayer at midfield after games. Justice Gorsuch's majority opinion resolved the case on free-exercise and free-speech grounds (Kennedy's postgame prayer was personal rather than government speech, did not coerce students, and was targeted by a school rule that was not neutral or generally applicable), and then held that the Establishment Clause did not require the school to suppress the prayer. The Court distinguished Lee v Weisman (1992) (graduation prayer delivered by school-invited clergy to a captive audience) by emphasizing that Kennedy's prayer was neither coerced nor school-sponsored. Justice Sotomayor's dissent challenged the majority's factual description, arguing that Kennedy's prayers were demonstrative and coercive in their actual social context, and warned that abandoning Lemon left lower courts without workable guidance. See [[Cases/Kennedy v Bremerton School District (2022)|full brief]].

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would be upheld under the current historical-practices framework)

1. **Hypo: Legislative Prayer Before a State Senate Session.** A state senate opens each session with a brief prayer delivered on a rotating basis by local clergy of any faith who volunteer. A senator of no religion sues, claiming the prayer violates the Establishment Clause. **Why:** Legislative prayer is the clearest example of a practice with a continuous historical pedigree stretching back to the First Congress, which hired a chaplain and opened sessions with prayer. Marsh v Chambers (1983) upheld the Nebraska legislature's prayer practice on historical grounds even before Kennedy v Bremerton made history the general framework. Under the current test, the Founding generation's own acceptance of legislative prayer is dispositive. The rotating clergy arrangement further reinforces non-preference among religions. The historical tradition is so thick here that the challenge fails even if a particular senator experiences social discomfort, because the coercion threshold from Lee v Weisman is not met in a setting where adults are free to ignore the prayer.

2. **Hypo: Voucher Program Reaching Religious Schools Through Parental Choice.** A state enacts a need-based school voucher program. Any accredited private school, religious or secular, may accept vouchers. In practice, 85 percent of participating families choose religious schools because that is what is available in most neighborhoods. A taxpayer challenges the program under the Establishment Clause. **Why:** The program is formally neutral with respect to religion (religious and secular schools participate on identical terms), and aid reaches religious schools only through the genuine and independent choices of individual parents. Under Zelman v Simmons-Harris (2002), the program does not constitute direct government aid to religion at all, because the government neither selects the religious recipients nor directs resources to them. Zelman's private-choice framework is logically prior to the historical-practices inquiry: there is no Establishment Clause question because the government has not itself established anything. The 85 percent religious-enrollment figure is constitutionally irrelevant so long as religious participation is incidental to neutral private choice rather than a structural preference built into the program.

### Opposite-Side (Would be struck down)

3. **Hypo: Mandatory Classroom Prayer Led by Teacher.** A state statute requires every public school teacher to lead a brief nondenominational prayer at the start of each class day; students may remain seated but are expected to bow their heads. A student's parents sue. **Why:** This hypothetical combines the core prohibitions that both Lemon and the new framework preserve. Under Lee v Weisman (1992), expressly distinguished by Kennedy v Bremerton and preserved as an independent prohibition on coercion, schoolchildren are the paradigmatic captive audience: they face intense social pressure to conform in the classroom setting, and formal opt-outs do not cure the constitutional defect when the setting is so closely tied to government authority. Kennedy v Bremerton itself emphasized that its holding did not disturb Lee v Weisman or the school-prayer line of cases, and that a coach-led or teacher-led prayer delivered to players or students still gathered would defeat the claim. Historical practices analysis reinforces the result rather than displacing it: teacher-led classroom prayer imposed by statute is not a Founding-era practice, and the use of governmental authority to compose or impose specific religious observances sits at the core of what the Establishment Clause was understood to forbid. The practice fails under every framework.

4. **Hypo: New Ten Commandments Display in a Courthouse with an Explicit Religious Preamble.** A state legislature in 2026 mandates the installation of a Ten Commandments monument in every county courthouse, accompanied by a legislative resolution declaring that "the Ten Commandments are the law of God and the moral foundation of American civil law." A civil-liberties organization sues. **Why:** This is McCreary County v ACLU of Kentucky (2005) as it would return today. The explicit religious-purpose statement in the preamble would have doomed the displays under Lemon's purpose prong. It equally dooms them under the current framework. American Legion's presumption of constitutionality attaches only to longstanding monuments; it does not protect new erections accompanied by explicit religious-endorsement statements. Historical practices analysis provides no rescue either: courthouse Ten Commandments displays installed by legislative resolution declaring their religious authority over civil law are not a Founding-era tradition. They are, to the contrary, a contemporary phenomenon that departs from the Founders' concern with legislative capture of religious authority.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

5. **Hypo: Public University Housing a Religious Group's Weekly Meeting in a Chapel It Owns.** A state university owns a multi-purpose chapel building on campus, originally donated in the 1920s and used for memorial services, weddings, and interfaith gatherings. The university permits a campus Christian group to hold a weekly worship service there rent-free. A student who is not affiliated with the group sues, arguing that the university's rent-free provision of the chapel constitutes direct aid to religion. **Why:** The case draws in multiple frameworks that pull in different directions. Under Rosenberger v Rector of University of Virginia (1995), viewpoint-neutral provision of university resources to student groups (including religious groups) does not violate the Establishment Clause and refusing to provide them on equal terms may itself violate the Free Speech Clause. That framework favors the university. Under the private-choice line of Zelman, one might argue students are choosing to attend the meetings independently, which cleanses the arrangement. Under the historical-practices framework, the answer depends heavily on how the court characterizes the relevant tradition: public universities have a long history of maintaining chapels and allowing religious student groups to use them, but they equally have a history of endowed denominational preferences that a neutrality-minded court might treat as the cautionary side of the historical ledger. The coercion doctrine probably points toward the university (adults attending voluntarily, no authority backing attendance), but a dissenting view could emphasize that the rent-free accommodation on state-owned property is qualitatively different from allowing use of a generally available student-activity fund.

## How to Spot on an Exam

Establishment Clause questions typically arise in one of five fact patterns. Work through this recognition ladder before committing to a framework.

**First, identify the setting.** Classroom (public school), ceremonial (legislative prayer, inaugural), aid (vouchers, grants, reimbursements), display (monument, holiday scene), and employment (government workers' religious expression) each implicate a different slice of the doctrine.

**Second, check for coercion.** If any fact pattern involves schoolchildren, captive audiences, or direct pressure to participate in religious observance, flag Lee v Weisman (1992) and note that the coercion prohibition survives independently of Lemon's abandonment. A finding of coercion usually resolves the case against the government without needing to work through historical practices at all.

**Third, check for private choice.** If government aid reaches religious institutions, ask whether it does so through the independent decisions of private individuals. If yes, Zelman v Simmons-Harris (2002) largely resolves the question; the aid is not government aid for Establishment Clause purposes.

**Fourth, apply the historical-practices framework.** Identify the specific practice at issue, then ask whether the Founding generation or early Republic accepted, tolerated, or rejected analogous practices. Legislative prayer, Thanksgiving proclamations, chaplains, and the "In God We Trust" motto are at the accepted end; state-composed school prayer, state-imposed tithes, and denominationally preferential aid are at the rejected end. Most exam fact patterns will sit somewhere in between; the analytical work is constructing a historical analogy and defending it against counter-analogies.

**Fifth, address Lemon only as background.** Professor Chandler expects awareness that Kennedy v Bremerton (2022) formally abandoned Lemon. Lower courts still cite it by habit, and some fact patterns can be analyzed efficiently under either framework to produce the same result. Show awareness of the shift; do not rely on Lemon as the controlling rule.

Exam flags that signal strict scrutiny or near-automatic invalidity: explicit religious-purpose language in statutory preambles or legislative records; denominational preferences in statutory text; state-composed or state-mandated prayer in public schools; direct aid flowing to pervasively sectarian institutions without private-choice intermediation; coercive settings (captive audiences, employment conditions, benefit denials) that pressure religious participation.

## Critique

**Progressive critique (loss of neutrality, historical cherry-picking).** The most sustained progressive critique of the post-Kennedy v Bremerton framework is that historical practices analysis discards the Establishment Clause's neutrality principle in favor of a selective tour through American history that privileges majority-Christian practices the Founders happened to accept. Justice Sotomayor's dissent in Kennedy v Bremerton warned that history-and-tradition tests give courts wide latitude to pick the level of generality at which to describe the relevant practice, so that a court sympathetic to the government can always find a Founding-era analogue for what it wishes to uphold and a court hostile to the government can always distinguish it. Douglas Laycock, Marci Hamilton, and others have argued that the Van Orden-to-Kennedy-v-Bremerton arc effectively converts the Establishment Clause into a one-way ratchet benefiting established practices: anything that has survived long enough, or that resembles something the Founders accepted, is constitutional, while novel harms to religious minorities lack the historical pedigree needed to generate protection. The progressive critique also notes that the loss of the endorsement test removes a frame designed to protect religious minorities who are outsiders to the political community; historical-practices analysis, by contrast, tends to measure constitutionality against the experience of the majority religious community at the Founding.

**Originalist critique (Lemon was never grounded in original meaning).** The originalist reply begins with the observation that Lemon was a product of twentieth-century judicial invention rather than constitutional text or original public meaning. Scalia's ghoul metaphor in Lamb's Chapel (1993) captured the frustration that Lemon's three prongs were both unpredictable in application and unmoored from any external anchor that could discipline judicial discretion. Originalists argue that the historical-practices framework restores the text to its proper context: the Clause was understood at ratification to forbid specific practices (established state churches, mandatory tithes to a designated denomination, legally compelled church attendance, religious tests for federal office, preferential aid to a particular sect) and was not understood to require strict neutrality between religion and non-religion across all public life. Justice Thomas pushes the argument further, suggesting in his Van Orden and other concurrences that the Establishment Clause was originally a federalism rule protecting state religious establishments from federal interference and that its incorporation against the states through the Fourteenth Amendment is itself a doctrinal error. That incorporation-error argument has not commanded a majority and would destabilize the post-1947 doctrinal architecture if it ever did.

**Doctrinal architecture critique (unpredictability and administrability).** Both camps have converged, for different reasons, on the complaint that Establishment Clause doctrine is unpredictable in lower courts. Under Lemon, the three prongs were applied inconsistently and results were driven by unarticulated judgments about how much church-state interaction was tolerable. Under Kennedy v Bremerton's historical-practices framework, lower courts face a new set of indeterminacy problems: how to identify the relevant practice at the right level of generality, how to resolve conflicting evidence from different Founding-era jurisdictions, and how to handle modern practices with no direct historical analogue (public-school athletics, social-media-mediated religious expression by government employees, large-scale federal funding programs). The coercion doctrine preserves one clear rule, but the rest of the doctrine now sits in a transitional period where lower-court decisions are likely to diverge and the Supreme Court will be asked to supply further structure through case-by-case elaboration.

**The private-choice stability point.** One under-discussed feature of the modern arc is that Zelman's private-choice framework has been remarkably stable across the Lemon-to-Kennedy-v-Bremerton transition. Both progressive and originalist Justices agree that aid directed by private individuals is not government aid for Establishment Clause purposes, and the framework operates at a logically prior step that is independent of whichever doctrinal test currently applies to direct aid. That stability is the strongest evidence that some components of modern Establishment Clause doctrine are durable across the doctrinal shift, even as the general framework has changed.

## Connections

- [[Topics/Free Exercise of Religion|Free Exercise of Religion]]: The two religion clauses are read together as a single structural principle of government neutrality toward religion. The pair of shifts operates in the same direction: away from strict separation and toward accommodation grounded in historical tradition.
- [[Topics/Incorporation Doctrine|Incorporation Doctrine]]: Everson v Board of Education (1947) incorporated the Establishment Clause against the states through the Fourteenth Amendment Due Process Clause.
