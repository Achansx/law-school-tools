# Free Exercise of Religion

## Overview

The Free Exercise Clause of the First Amendment, incorporated against the states through the Fourteenth Amendment Due Process Clause, prohibits government from punishing religious belief and from placing unjustified burdens on religious practice. The central doctrinal question is not whether a law burdens religion, but what level of scrutiny that burden triggers.

From Sherbert v Verner (1963) through the late 1980s, courts applied a compelling-interest test: any substantial burden on religious practice required the government to demonstrate a compelling interest pursued through the least restrictive means. Employment Division v Smith (1990) replaced that framework for most cases. Under Smith, a neutral law of general applicability does not violate the Free Exercise Clause even if it incidentally burdens religious practice; rational basis is the standard. The compelling-interest test survives only in narrow categories: unemployment compensation cases (Sherbert's original context), laws that target religion on their face or through selective exemptions, and cases where the law grants officials discretionary authority to create individualized exceptions.

Church of Lukumi Babalu Aye v City of Hialeah (1993) gave teeth to the neutrality and general-applicability requirements by demonstrating that courts will look past formally neutral statutory text to legislative history and selective exemption patterns to determine whether a law actually targets religion. City of Boerne v Flores (1997) confirmed that Smith represents the constitutional floor: Congress may not use its Section 5 enforcement power to impose a compelling-interest test on state laws by statute (RFRA), because that exceeds the remedial scope of Section 5. Fulton v City of Philadelphia (2021) created a significant doctrinal wrinkle: a law that grants officials discretionary authority to grant individualized exemptions is not generally applicable, and therefore triggers strict scrutiny even when neutral on its face.

---

## Governing Rule

> **Rule:** A neutral law of general applicability that incidentally burdens religious practice does not violate the Free Exercise Clause and is subject only to rational basis review. A law is neutral if it does not single out religious conduct for disfavor on its face or in operation; a law is generally applicable if it does not selectively impose burdens on religious conduct while permitting comparable secular conduct that undermines the government's asserted interests to the same degree. (*Employment Division v Smith*, 1990)

> **Rule:** A law that is not neutral or not generally applicable is subject to strict scrutiny: the government must demonstrate a compelling interest and must use means narrowly tailored to achieve that interest. Non-neutrality is established by showing that the law's object is to target religious conduct, which may be inferred from statutory text using religious vocabulary, statements of legislative animus, or patterns of selective enforcement. Non-general-applicability is established by showing that the law permits secular conduct that undermines the government's asserted interest to the same or greater degree as the burdened religious conduct. (*Church of Lukumi Babalu Aye v City of Hialeah*, 1993)

> **Rule:** A law or government policy is not generally applicable if it vests officials with discretionary authority to grant individualized exemptions, because that mechanism requires the government to justify any denial of an exemption to a religious claimant under compelling-interest scrutiny. (*Fulton v City of Philadelphia*, 2021)

## Doctrinal Development

### The Sherbert Era: Compelling Interest (Pre-Smith)

Before Smith, free exercise doctrine was governed by the framework from Sherbert v Verner (1963). South Carolina denied unemployment benefits to Adell Sherbert, a Seventh-day Adventist fired for refusing Saturday work. The Supreme Court applied a two-stage test: first, does the government's action impose a substantial burden on the claimant's religious practice? If yes, the government must demonstrate a compelling interest that cannot be achieved through less restrictive means. Conditioning unemployment benefits on the willingness to work on one's Sabbath imposed a substantial burden, and South Carolina identified no compelling interest sufficient to justify it. The Sherbert test promised robust protection for religious practice against virtually any government action that burdened it.

### Smith: The Rational Basis Revolution

Employment Division v Smith (1990) fundamentally restructured free exercise doctrine. Alfred Smith and Galen Black were fired from a private drug rehabilitation organization for ingesting peyote as part of a Native American Church ceremony. Oregon denied them unemployment benefits because the conduct was criminal under a generally applicable drug prohibition. Justice Scalia's majority held that the Free Exercise Clause does not require religious exemptions from neutral, generally applicable laws. Applying Sherbert's compelling-interest test to such laws would make each person a law unto themselves and would subject every generally applicable regulation to constitutional challenge by any religiously motivated objector. The Court relegated Sherbert to the unemployment compensation context and held that the political process, not constitutional adjudication, is the proper avenue for seeking religious exemptions from general laws. Smith was immediately controversial and prompted Congress to enact RFRA in 1993 to restore the compelling-interest test by statute.

### Lukumi: Neutrality and General Applicability Defined

Church of Lukumi Babalu Aye v City of Hialeah (1993) arose when Hialeah enacted a series of ordinances specifically designed to suppress Santeria animal sacrifice after the Church of Lukumi announced plans to open a house of worship there. The ordinances prohibited ritual animal slaughter while exempting pest control, hunting, farming, Kosher slaughter (in some applications), and veterinary euthanasia. The Court held the ordinances were neither neutral nor generally applicable. Non-neutrality was shown by the text (using terms like "ritual" and "ceremony" as explicit religious markers), by the legislative record (statements of hostility to Santeria), and by the underinclusiveness of the scheme (exempting all secular animal killing that caused the same harms the city claimed to be addressing). Because the ordinances failed both neutrality and general applicability, strict scrutiny applied. Hialeah could not demonstrate a compelling interest, and its interests were not pursued in a narrowly tailored manner given the extensive secular exemptions. Lukumi's most important methodological contribution is the instruction to look through formally neutral text to legislative purpose and selective exemption patterns.

### Boerne: Constitutional Baseline and Congressional Limits

Congress responded to Smith with the Religious Freedom Restoration Act (1993), which purported to restore the Sherbert compelling-interest test as a statutory floor for all laws substantially burdening religion. City of Boerne v Flores (1997) held RFRA unconstitutional as applied to state and local governments. Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment grants Congress power to enforce constitutional guarantees, but that power is remedial and preventive, not substantive: Congress may remedy and deter constitutional violations but may not redefine the substance of constitutional rights. The congruence-and-proportionality test requires Section 5 legislation to be congruent and proportional to the pattern of constitutional violations it targets. RFRA failed because the legislative record demonstrated no widespread pattern of state religious discrimination at the level Smith defines as unconstitutional, and because imposing a compelling-interest requirement on all generally applicable laws was grossly disproportionate to any documented pattern. RFRA remains valid as applied to the federal government under Article I powers.

### Fulton: The Individualized Exemption Carve-Out

Fulton v City of Philadelphia (2021) arose when Philadelphia stopped referring foster children to Catholic Social Services (CSS) because CSS would not certify same-sex couples. Philadelphia's standard foster-care contract contained a clause permitting the Commissioner to grant individualized exceptions to the non-discrimination requirement at the Commissioner's discretion. Chief Justice Roberts held that because the contract permitted discretionary exemptions, it was not a system of generally applicable rules: the existence of an exemption mechanism meant any denial of an exemption to CSS required compelling-interest justification under Lukumi. Philadelphia could not satisfy that standard. The Fulton majority deliberately declined to overrule Smith, though concurrences by Alito, Gorsuch, and Thomas argued Smith should be overruled. Fulton's practical significance is considerable: any government contracting, permitting, or benefit scheme that grants officials discretionary authority to make case-by-case exceptions may fail general-applicability review, turning routine government discretion into a constitutional vulnerability.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way as strict scrutiny cases)

**Hypo 1: Targeted Ritual Prohibition.** A state enacts a law prohibiting "ceremonial ingestion of any controlled substance" while maintaining a generally applicable drug prohibition with standard exemptions for medical use, research, and law enforcement. A Native American Church member is prosecuted under the ceremonial prohibition for peyote use in a religious ceremony. *Analysis:* The statute is non-neutral because "ceremonial" is a religious marker: it targets conduct defined by its religious character. Under Lukumi, a law that selectively burdens religious conduct while permitting comparable secular conduct must survive strict scrutiny. The state cannot distinguish this from Hialeah's ordinances using "ritual" and "ceremony." The generally applicable drug law already serves any public-health interest the state can assert, so there is no compelling interest served by the targeted ceremonial prohibition that is not already served by the general law.

**Hypo 2: Discretionary Permit with Secular Waiver Authority.** A city requires permits for all outdoor gatherings over 50 people. The permit code grants the Parks Commissioner discretion to waive the permit requirement for "community events of recognized civic value." A church applies to hold outdoor Easter services without a permit; the Commissioner denies the waiver on the grounds that the event is religious, not civic. *Analysis:* Under Fulton, a system granting officials discretionary authority to create individualized exemptions is not generally applicable, regardless of formal neutrality in the permit requirement itself. The Commissioner's power to waive requirements case-by-case means any denial to a religious claimant must survive compelling-interest scrutiny. Unless the city can identify a compelling interest specifically served by denying the church's waiver (not simply by requiring permits generally), the denial is unconstitutional.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently, i.e., rational basis applies)

**Hypo 3: Genuinely Generally Applicable Food Safety Code.** A state's food safety law requires all food establishments to use USDA-inspected slaughterhouses and prohibits all on-site animal slaughter regardless of purpose, religious or secular. A mosque that practices Halal slaughter on-site challenges the law. *Analysis:* Unlike Lukumi, this law contains no secular exemptions: all on-site slaughter is prohibited regardless of purpose. It is neutral (uses no religious vocabulary; not motivated by animus) and generally applicable (no secular conduct performing the same function is permitted). Under Smith, the mosque's challenge fails on rational basis review. The mosque must pursue a legislative exemption through the political process.

**Hypo 4: Neutral Drug Prohibition Without Exemptions.** A state prohibits peyote possession without any exemption for religious use, and the state maintains no exemptions for any other use of peyote (no medical, research, or secular ceremonial exemptions). A member of the Native American Church challenges the prohibition. *Analysis:* This is Smith's own facts. The prohibition is neutral and generally applicable. Rational basis applies. The claimant's religious motivation for the conduct does not convert the neutral prohibition into a Free Exercise violation. The political process, not constitutional adjudication, is the correct avenue.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

**Hypo 5: RFRA, Federal Funding, and Religious Non-Discrimination.** Congress requires all recipients of federal housing-assistance grants to certify compliance with a non-discrimination policy prohibiting denial of housing on the basis of sexual orientation. A religious housing ministry that receives federal funds challenges the requirement under RFRA (still valid against the federal government after Boerne). The ministry argues that its religious beliefs prohibit recognizing same-sex partnerships and that the requirement substantially burdens its religious practice. *Analysis:* RFRA requires the federal government to satisfy compelling-interest-plus-narrow-tailoring when it substantially burdens religious practice. The burden here is real: comply with non-discrimination policy or forfeit federal funds. The government's asserted interest (non-discrimination in federally subsidized housing) is plausibly compelling. The genuine difficulty is at the narrow-tailoring stage: is there a less restrictive means of achieving the non-discrimination goal that does not require the ministry's participation? Courts have split on whether conditioning federal funding (as opposed to directly prohibiting conduct) constitutes a substantial burden under RFRA, and whether the government's non-discrimination interest is sufficiently compelling when the restriction comes through conditions on voluntary federal funding rather than direct legal prohibition.

## How to Spot on an Exam

Free Exercise Clause issues arise whenever a government law or policy burdens a religious belief or practice. The framework almost always turns on one dispositive question: is the law neutral and generally applicable?

Work through these steps. First, identify the burden: does the law require conduct that the claimant's religion forbids, or prohibit conduct that the claimant's religion requires? A burden that targets belief (compelled profession, required denial of belief) is categorically prohibited regardless of Smith. Second, assess neutrality: does the law's text, purpose, or enforcement history target religion? Look for religious vocabulary in the statute, statements of animus in the legislative record, or patterns of selective prosecution against religious but not secular offenders. Third, assess general applicability: does the law exempt secular conduct that undermines the government's asserted interest to the same degree as the burdened religious conduct? Lukumi's key move was identifying all the secular killing the city permitted while prohibiting only religious sacrifice. Fourth, check for individualized exemptions: does the law or policy give any official discretion to grant case-by-case exceptions? Any such mechanism defeats general applicability under Fulton and triggers strict scrutiny.

Exam flags that signal strict scrutiny: (1) statutory text using words like "ritual," "ceremony," or "worship" as limiting terms; (2) a law with extensive secular exemptions but no religious exemption; (3) a permitting, contracting, or benefit scheme with a "Commissioner may waive" clause or equivalent; (4) a federal statute or policy (RFRA potentially applies independently of the constitutional floor); (5) Congress legislating against state religious discrimination under Section 5 (apply Boerne's congruence-and-proportionality test, not the Smith standard).

## Critique

**Smith's democratic-process rationale.** Scalia argued that requiring religious exemptions from generally applicable laws would subject every regulation to constitutional challenge by any religiously motivated objector, making each person a law unto themselves. Critics respond that this argument proves too much: it equally justifies relegating every constitutional right to the political process. The Free Exercise Clause exists precisely because religious minorities whose beliefs the political process does not accommodate need judicial protection. Justices Alito, Gorsuch, and Thomas argued in Fulton that Smith was wrongly decided and that Sherbert's compelling-interest test represents the correct constitutional rule.

**The general-applicability fiction.** Lukumi demonstrates that general applicability is not a clean category: Hialeah's ordinances were formally general in their terms but functionally targeted in their operation. The same problem recurs whenever a legislature drafts exemptions carefully enough to exclude religious conduct while permitting equivalent secular conduct. Lukumi's reliance on legislative-history analysis to catch the targeting is essential, but a more careful legislature could potentially evade scrutiny by omitting incriminating statements from the record.

**Fulton's narrow holding and Smith's uncertain future.** Fulton's majority declined to overrule Smith despite three Justices explicitly urging it. The individualized-exemption route may gradually erode Smith's practical scope by categorizing ever-broader discretionary mechanisms as defeating general applicability, without the Court ever confronting whether Smith itself was correctly decided. This leaves doctrine in an unstable position: the constitutional rule is nominally Smith, but the practical scope of Smith's protection for neutral general laws is contracting.

**RFRA and congressional constitutional interpretation.** Boerne's holding that Congress cannot use Section 5 to impose a substantive rule that exceeds the Constitution's own requirements raises a structural question about the relationship between judicial and legislative authority over constitutional meaning. Congress disagreed with Smith and enacted RFRA to restore what it believed was the proper constitutional standard. The Court held that Congress lacked the power to do so as to state laws. The result is a two-track system: state religious burdens are governed by Smith-level constitutional protection, while federal religious burdens are governed by the more protective RFRA standard, a disparity that has no obvious constitutional basis.

## Connections

- [[Topics/Equal Protection|Equal Protection]]: Lukumi's legislative-animus analysis parallels the discriminatory-purpose inquiry in equal protection. A law targeting religion because of hostility to religious practice is structurally analogous to a law motivated by racial animus; in both contexts, the Court looks past formally neutral text to legislative purpose.
- [[Topics/Substantive Due Process|Substantive Due Process]]: Smith noted, without developing, that hybrid-rights claims combining Free Exercise with another constitutional right (parental rights, free speech, association) might require heightened scrutiny. That dictum remains underdeveloped but may support challenges that pair religious practice with a recognized fundamental right.
