# Intermediate Scrutiny

## Overview

Intermediate scrutiny is the middle tier of equal-protection review. It sits between rational basis (the deferential default) and strict scrutiny (the near-fatal test reserved for race and other suspect classifications). The Court formalized the tier in Craig v Boren (1976) for sex-based classifications after a decade in which Reed v Reed (1971), Frontiero v Richardson (1973), and Stanton v Stanton (1975) had pushed sex review beyond rational basis without committing to a label. The tier asks whether a classification serves an important governmental objective and whether the means are substantially related to that objective. United States v Virginia (1996) added that the state must show an exceedingly persuasive justification, a phrase that tightened the tier without formally moving it.

The tier is doctrinally narrow. It governs sex classifications and illegitimacy, and an unresolved cluster of cases applies a Cleburne-style rational-basis-with-bite analysis to mental disability and other classifications that look quasi-suspect. It does not govern race (strict scrutiny), age or wealth (rational basis), or fundamental rights (strict scrutiny via the substantive due process / equal protection overlap). The tier matters for the exam because every sex-classification question collapses into two sub-questions: which side of the tier line does the classification fall on, and does the asserted state interest survive the anti-stereotyping principle that drives the doctrine.

---

## Governing Rule

> **Rule (Intermediate Scrutiny):** A classification subject to intermediate scrutiny survives equal-protection review only when the government shows that the classification serves important governmental objectives and that the discriminatory means are substantially related to the achievement of those objectives. After United States v Virginia (1996), the government must offer an exceedingly persuasive justification, the proffered objective must be the actual reason for the classification (not invented post hoc), and the means may not rely on overbroad generalizations about the differing talents, capacities, or preferences of males and females.

## Doctrinal Development

### Reed v Reed (1971): The Precursor

Reed invalidated an Idaho probate statute that preferred males over females as estate administrators, the first time the Court struck down a sex classification under equal protection. Chief Justice Burger's opinion did not announce a new tier and formally applied rational basis, but the analysis bit hard: administrative convenience could not justify a sex line. Reed is the precursor that made Craig v Boren (1976) possible because it broke the post-Bradwell pattern of treating sex like any other social classification. See [[Cases/Reed v Reed (1971)|full brief]].

### Craig v Boren (1976): Tier Formalization

Craig invalidated an Oklahoma statute that set the legal drinking age for 3.2% beer at 21 for males and 18 for females. Justice Brennan's majority opinion held that classifications by gender must serve important governmental objectives and must be substantially related to achievement of those objectives. The opinion settled on intermediate scrutiny as the compromise that could hold a five-justice majority after the failed Frontiero v Richardson (1973) push for strict scrutiny. The case is also the canonical statement that statistical correlations between sex and a regulated behavior, even when accurate, will not satisfy the substantial-relationship prong when the fit is loose. See [[Cases/Craig v Boren (1976)|full brief]].

### Geduldig v Aiello (1974): The Boundary-Case Anomaly

Geduldig held that California's exclusion of pregnancy-related disabilities from a state insurance program was not sex discrimination because the classification ran between pregnant women and nonpregnant persons (which includes both sexes). The case is the doctrinal anomaly that complicates intermediate scrutiny: pregnancy classifications, despite their obvious correlation with sex, escape the tier. Geduldig sits awkwardly with the anti-stereotyping principle Craig and Virginia later articulated, and it remains a recurring exam fact pattern because of its unstable boundary. See [[Cases/Geduldig v Aiello (1974)|full brief]].

### United States v Virginia (1996): Tier Tightening

VMI invalidated Virginia's categorical exclusion of women from the Virginia Military Institute. Justice Ginsburg's majority opinion reframed the tier by demanding an exceedingly persuasive justification, requiring that the state's proffered interest be genuine (not invented post hoc), and forbidding reliance on overbroad generalizations about males and females. Virginia did not formally move the tier but tightened every component. The remedial holding (the Virginia Women's Institute for Leadership at Mary Baldwin College was inadequate because it lacked VMI's tangible and intangible benefits) extended the tier to question whether parallel single-sex programs ever can cure exclusion from a unique public institution. See [[Cases/United States v Virginia (1996)|full brief]].

### City of Cleburne v Cleburne Living Center (1985): The Quasi-Tier Sibling

Cleburne struck down a Texas city's denial of a special-use permit to a group home for mentally disabled adults. The Court formally declined to recognize mental disability as a quasi-suspect class but invalidated the permit denial on rational-basis grounds, relying on a fit so loose that scholars call the analysis rational basis with bite. Cleburne sits in awkward proximity to intermediate scrutiny: its operational analysis looks like the tier without the label, and lower courts cite the case for both deferential and heightened propositions depending on the surrounding doctrinal need. See [[Cases/City of Cleburne v Cleburne Living Center (1985)|full brief]].

### Sessions v Morales-Santana (2017): Modern Federal Application

Morales-Santana invalidated a sex-based provision of the Immigration and Nationality Act that imposed different physical-presence requirements on unwed citizen fathers and unwed citizen mothers as a condition for transmitting U.S. citizenship to a child born abroad. Justice Ginsburg's majority opinion applied Virginia's exceedingly-persuasive-justification framing to the federal government via Fifth Amendment due process congruence, confirming that intermediate scrutiny operates symmetrically against state and federal sex classifications. The remedy holding (extending the longer requirement to mothers rather than the shorter to fathers) raises an unresolved question about whether equal protection always requires leveling up. See [[Cases/Sessions v Morales-Santana (2017)|full brief]].

## Comparison: The Three Tiers at a Glance

| Tier | Trigger | Burden | Means Fit | Surviving Examples |
|------|---------|--------|-----------|---------------------|
| **Strict Scrutiny** | Race, national origin, alienage; fundamental rights | Compelling governmental interest | Narrowly tailored; race-neutral alternatives seriously considered | Affirmative action programs sometimes survived pre-SFFA; almost never after SFFA (2023) |
| **Intermediate Scrutiny** | Sex, illegitimacy; arguably mental disability via Cleburne | Important governmental objective; exceedingly persuasive justification (Virginia, 1996) | Substantially related; no overbroad generalizations | Single-sex public nursing schools survived in Mississippi University for Women but only after defending against rather than relying on stereotypes; affirmative-action-style sex preferences rarely survive |
| **Rational Basis** | Age, wealth, most other classifications | Legitimate governmental interest | Rationally related | Almost everything survives unless the law looks like animus (Romer v Evans, 1996) or arbitrary line-drawing (Cleburne) |

The intermediate tier is doctrinally distinctive because it is the only tier whose verbal formulation was tightened (by Virginia) without changing the tier's name. Reading Virginia carefully matters because some lower courts treat exceedingly persuasive justification as a verbal upgrade inside the same tier and others read it as covert strict scrutiny.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side

1. **Hypo:** A state university operates a single-sex women's-only physical-education program on the rationale that women historically had unequal access to athletics under Title IX. A male applicant sues. **Why:** Following Virginia, the state must articulate an exceedingly persuasive justification that the program addresses a continuing inequality, not an overbroad generalization about female athletic preference. A remedial-justification rationale tied to documented past exclusion is the strongest survivable framing; an unsupported assumption that women prefer single-sex athletics fails the anti-stereotyping principle and the program is struck down.
2. **Hypo:** A state highway-patrol agency requires female applicants to meet a lower physical-fitness standard than male applicants on the assumption that women cannot meet male standards. A female officer who exceeded the male standard challenges the differential as stigmatizing. **Why:** The classification rests on an overbroad generalization about female physical capacity that the anti-stereotyping principle (Craig, Virginia) forbids. The agency's important interest in officer fitness is real but the means rely on the kind of generalization Virginia explicitly rejected. The differential is invalidated.

### Opposite-Side

3. **Hypo:** A state allows women but not men to be exempted from selective-service registration. A male challenger sues. **Why:** Rostker v Goldberg (1981, not in vault) upheld a similar exclusion under intermediate scrutiny, deferring heavily to congressional military-affairs judgments and accepting that the exclusion reflected a real difference in combat-eligibility rules then in force. After post-2015 changes opening combat positions to women the analysis would be sharply contested, but on the original record the case survives because the asserted military-affairs interest is important and the classification is substantially related to combat eligibility as defined at the time.
4. **Hypo:** A state pension scheme provides survivor benefits to widows automatically but requires widowers to prove dependency on the deceased spouse. **Why:** Wengler v Druggists Mutual Insurance (1980, not in vault) and Califano v Goldfarb (1977) struck down exactly this pattern under Craig because the survivor-benefit differential rested on the overbroad generalization that wives are dependent on husbands but not vice versa. The exam-ready fact pattern produces the anti-stereotyping result.

### Fence-Sitter

5. **Hypo:** A state law requires that fathers but not mothers of children born to unmarried parents file a formal acknowledgment to claim parental rights, justified on the rationale that the mother is biologically present at birth while the father may not be. **Why:** Real-difference arguments about biology have sometimes been accepted by the Court (Nguyen v INS, 2001, not in vault) and sometimes rejected (Sessions v Morales-Santana, 2017). The hypo turns on whether the court treats the disparate filing requirement as reflecting a genuine biological difference (the mother's known presence at birth versus the father's potential absence) or as a stereotype-laden differential that imposes administrative burden disproportionately on fathers. The case can plausibly be argued either way and is the kind of intermediate-scrutiny boundary case that produces split panels.

## How to Spot on an Exam

A sex-classification fact pattern is the signal. Look for a statute, regulation, or government policy that draws an explicit line between males and females, or between mothers and fathers, or between widows and widowers. Pregnancy classifications require careful handling because Geduldig still controls at the constitutional level (though Title VII and the Pregnancy Discrimination Act of 1978 cover most pregnancy-as-sex statutory claims). Then run the two prongs:

1. Identify the asserted governmental objective. If administrative convenience, it fails (Reed). If based on overbroad generalization about male and female capacities or preferences, it fails (Craig, Virginia). If a remedial response to documented past discrimination, it has a stronger chance.
2. Test the means fit. Statistical correlation alone (Craig) and post hoc rationalization (Virginia) will not satisfy substantial relationship. Real-difference arguments tied to biology can survive (Nguyen) but only when narrowly tied to the asserted objective.
3. Note Virginia's exceedingly persuasive justification language whenever the state's interest is challenged for being invented after the fact or for resting on stereotype rather than evidence.
4. Flag remedial questions. If the challenged law underincludes a benefit (like the citizenship-transmission rule in Morales-Santana), discuss whether the appropriate equal-protection remedy is leveling up or leveling down, and acknowledge that the choice is sometimes left to the state.

For mental-disability or quasi-suspect-category classifications outside sex, run Cleburne. The formal label is rational basis, but the operational analysis is intermediate-tier-like.

## Critique

**The exceedingly-persuasive-justification ambiguity.** Virginia's tightening language has been doctrinally productive (it gave lower courts a verbal handle for skeptical sex-classification review) and doctrinally destabilizing (it leaves unclear whether the tier moved or whether intermediate scrutiny is now operationally indistinguishable from strict scrutiny in sex cases). Chief Justice Rehnquist's Virginia concurrence flagged this concern at the time and his prediction that lower courts would treat the language as a tier change has partially come true.

**The Geduldig anomaly.** Treating pregnancy as a non-sex classification is conceptually awkward under modern anti-stereotyping logic. The case has not been overruled but its reasoning sits in tension with the rest of the doctrine. Critics from both directions complain: feminist scholars argue that Geduldig writes out the most distinctive sex-based biological reality, while doctrinalists argue that treating pregnancy as sex collapses the tier framework because pregnancy classifications can almost always satisfy intermediate scrutiny on real-difference grounds (so the categorical exclusion in Geduldig was unnecessary).

**The tier framework's narrow doctrinal home.** Intermediate scrutiny applies to sex and illegitimacy and arguably to a fuzzy quasi-suspect category captured by Cleburne. It does not govern transgender classifications (Skrmetti, 2025), age, wealth, or most other social classifications. Critics argue the tier framework's narrowness reflects judicial reluctance to take on additional politically charged categories rather than principled doctrinal limits.

**Anti-stereotyping versus anti-subordination.** The doctrine's anti-stereotyping framing (Craig, Virginia) is symmetrical: it forbids generalizations about either sex. Anti-subordination critics (Cass Sunstein, MacKinnon-influenced scholars) argue this symmetry obscures the asymmetric historical reality that sex classifications overwhelmingly burdened women. The symmetry produces results like Morales-Santana's leveling up that look formally equal but feel doctrinally evasive about which sex was actually disadvantaged.
