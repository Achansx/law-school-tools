# Second Amendment

## Overview

The Second Amendment reads, in its entirety: A well regulated Militia, being necessary to the security of a free State, the right of the people to keep and bear Arms, shall not be infringed. For most of the twentieth century the Amendment was a doctrinal backwater. The modern Second Amendment is a creation of four cases decided between 2008 and 2024, and the governing law must be read as that sequence rather than as any single opinion. Heller recognizes an individual right against the federal government. McDonald incorporates that right against the States through the Fourteenth Amendment. Bruen extends the right outside the home and replaces the lower courts' means-end framework with a text-history-and-tradition methodology. Rahimi applies that methodology to sustain a targeted modern regulation, clarifying that the government must identify a relevantly similar historical analogue rather than an identical founding-era twin. Each case answers a question its predecessor left open, and each creates a new question the next case must address.

---

## Governing Rule

> **Rule (post-Bruen, as clarified by Rahimi):** When the Second Amendment's plain text covers an individual's conduct, the Constitution presumptively protects that conduct. The government bears the burden of justifying a challenged regulation by demonstrating that it is consistent with this Nation's historical tradition of firearm regulation. The government need not produce an identical founding-era analogue; it must identify a historical regulation that is relevantly similar in both how it burdens the right and why it does so. Means-end scrutiny has no role in Second Amendment analysis.

The right, as incorporated by McDonald, binds federal, state, and local governments with the same force. The right's core is the possession and use of arms by ordinary, law-abiding citizens for self-defense, centered on but not limited to the home.

## Doctrinal Development

### Heller (2008): Recognition of an Individual Right

Heller is the foundational case. Before Heller the lower-court consensus treated the Second Amendment as protecting a collective right tied to militia service. Justice Scalia, writing for a 5-4 majority, parsed the prefatory and operative clauses separately and concluded that the operative clause codifies an individual right to possess and carry weapons in case of confrontation, with the prefatory clause announcing one purpose without exhausting the Amendment's scope. The holding invalidated the D.C. handgun ban and the trigger-lock requirement. Scalia also provided a list of presumptively lawful regulations (prohibitions on possession by felons and the mentally ill, restrictions in sensitive places such as schools and government buildings, and conditions on commercial sale) and limited protection to weapons in common use for lawful purposes. The opinion left two large questions open: whether the right binds the States, and what standard of review governs challenges to firearm regulations. See [[Cases/District of Columbia v Heller (2008)|full brief]].

### McDonald (2010): Incorporation Against the States

McDonald answered the first Heller-opened question. Alito's plurality applied the standard selective incorporation framework under the Due Process Clause, finding the right to keep and bear arms fundamental to our scheme of ordered liberty and deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition. Thomas's concurrence supplied the fifth vote on Privileges or Immunities grounds and would have overruled the Slaughter-House Cases and Cruikshank. The plurality's choice to walk the well-trodden Due Process path rather than revisit Privileges or Immunities is the most doctrinally revealing feature of the opinion: Alito acknowledged the textual difficulties with the Due Process route while declining to reopen them, and Thomas's concurrence is read by the vault's incorporation lecture as the modern Court's most candid acknowledgment of the clause-shifting that has powered incorporation since Gitlow. McDonald does not redefine the right, does not specify a standard of review, and does not address public carry. Those questions remained open for another twelve years. See [[Cases/McDonald v City of Chicago (2010)|full brief]].

### Bruen (2022): Extension Outside the Home and Methodological Overhaul

Bruen is both a scope-of-right case and a standard-of-review case. On scope, Thomas's six-Justice majority held that the Second Amendment protects a right to carry handguns for self-defense in public, striking down New York's proper-cause requirement for concealed carry licenses. On methodology, the Court rejected the two-step framework the courts of appeals had developed after Heller. That framework asked first whether the regulated conduct fell within the Amendment's text-and-history scope, and then applied means-end scrutiny (typically intermediate scrutiny) at the second step. Bruen held that the second step was one step too many. The new framework has two parts: the challenger shows that the plain text covers the conduct, at which point the Constitution presumptively protects it, and then the government must produce historical evidence of a relevantly similar regulation from the Nation's tradition of firearm regulation. Kavanaugh's concurrence (joined by the Chief Justice) stabilizes the decision by emphasizing that shall-issue licensing regimes with objective criteria remain constitutionally permissible; only discretionary may-issue regimes like New York's fall. Breyer, Sotomayor, and Kagan dissent on both administrability and public-safety grounds, arguing that the history-only test is indeterminate and that the Court is removing the government's interest in public safety from the constitutional equation. See [[Cases/New York State Rifle and Pistol Association v Bruen (2022)|full brief]].

### Rahimi (2024): The Analogue, Not the Twin

Rahimi is the first major post-Bruen application of the text-history-and-tradition framework. The Fifth Circuit had read Bruen to demand a founding-era twin for § 922(g)(8), which criminalizes firearm possession by persons subject to a qualifying domestic-violence restraining order. Chief Justice Roberts, writing for eight Justices, reversed. The clarification is that the Second Amendment is not trapped in amber. The government must produce a historical analogue that is relevantly similar in how it burdens the right and why it imposes that burden, but the regulation need not be a dead ringer for a founding-era law. The two historical traditions supporting § 922(g)(8) are the surety laws, which required persons shown to threaten the peace to post a bond or be jailed, and the common-law prohibition against going armed to the terror of the people. Two features of § 922(g)(8) carry much of the doctrinal load: the disarmament is temporary (it lasts only as long as the protective order) and it is adjudicated (a judge must find a credible threat of physical violence after notice and a hearing). The Court also rejects the government's attempt to convert Heller's descriptive phrase responsible, law-abiding citizens into a free-standing constitutional category. Thomas, the author of Bruen, dissents alone, arguing that the majority has diluted his own framework by mixing and matching the burden of one historical law with the justification of another. See [[Cases/United States v Rahimi (2024)|full brief]].

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side

1. **Hypo:** A city ordinance bans all semi-automatic handguns, which make up the majority of handguns sold in the United States, while permitting revolvers. **Why:** Heller invalidated a ban on the most preferred firearm for self-defense in the home. A ban on the dominant class of handguns is functionally equivalent regardless of whether a less common alternative remains available. Under Heller's in-common-use principle and Bruen's text-covers-the-conduct step, the plaintiff carries step one easily and the government faces the historical-analogue burden for a categorical ban on a dominant firearm class.

2. **Hypo:** A state adopts a may-issue concealed carry regime requiring applicants to demonstrate a specific and articulable threat beyond that faced by the general public, with denial authority vested in a local sheriff. **Why:** This is materially identical to the New York proper-cause regime Bruen struck down. Public carry for self-defense falls within the Second Amendment's text under Bruen, and a discretionary showing of particularized need has no sufficiently similar historical analogue.

### Opposite-Side

3. **Hypo:** A state bars possession of firearms by persons subject to a protective order issued after notice and a hearing that includes a specific finding of credible threat of physical violence to an intimate partner or child. **Why:** Rahimi directly sustains this kind of regulation. Surety laws and going-armed laws supply the historical tradition, and the temporariness and judicial-process features are the doctrinal stabilizers the Court emphasizes.

4. **Hypo:** A state statute prohibits firearm possession in courthouses, legislative chambers, and polling places. **Why:** Bruen (adopting Heller's dicta) preserves the sensitive-places doctrine for these paradigm locations, and the historical record supporting prohibitions in those places is clear. The restriction fits within a long-standing tradition.

### Fence-Sitter

5. **Hypo:** A state bans possession of AR-15-style semi-automatic rifles, the most popular rifle platform in the United States. **Why:** The AR-15 is arguably the most preferred rifle for lawful purposes, which would bring it within Heller's in-common-use protection and trigger Bruen's presumption. The government must then produce a relevantly similar historical analogue. The dangerous-and-unusual-weapons category Heller preserved can cut either way, and analogies to restrictions on Bowie knives or concealable weapons run into the level-of-generality problem Breyer flagged in Bruen dissent and Rahimi tolerates. Lower courts have divided sharply after Bruen and Rahimi.

## How to Spot on an Exam

The natural prompt is a modern firearms regulation challenged under the Second Amendment. The analytical walkthrough that the vault materials support:

1. **Text covers the conduct.** Identify whether the regulation burdens conduct the Second Amendment's plain text protects. Heller (keep and bear for self-defense in the home) and Bruen (public carry) together establish that possession and carry for self-defense are within the text. Look for arguments that the regulated activity falls outside the text (for example, a regulation targeting dangerous and unusual weapons that are not in common use).

2. **Which government is regulating.** If federal, Heller supplies the right directly. If state or local, McDonald incorporates the right against the States with full federal strength; there is no watered-down state version.

3. **Burden-shift to the government.** Under Bruen, once the challenger shows the text covers the conduct, the government must produce a relevantly similar historical analogue. Rahimi clarifies that an analogue, not a twin, is required. Apply the how and why metrics: does the modern law impose a comparable burden on the right, and is it comparably justified?

4. **Doctrinal stabilizers.** Look for features that echo Rahimi: is the restriction temporary? Is it tied to a judicial finding after notice and a hearing? Does it track Heller's list of presumptively lawful regulations (felons, the mentally ill, sensitive places, commercial sale conditions)? Kavanaugh's Bruen concurrence (shall-issue OK, may-issue suspect) is also doing work.

5. **Pressure points.** Two exam-likely pressure points. First, the level of generality of the historical analogue, which is the central line of attack in Breyer's Bruen dissent and Thomas's Rahimi dissent. Second, the frontier questions Rahimi does not resolve: § 922(g)(1) (felon-in-possession), § 922(g)(3) (drug users), status-based disqualifications, and novel technology-specific regulations (microstamping, serialization). Rahimi's temporariness and adjudication features cut against permanent categorical disarmament and argue for fresh historical-analogue analysis in those cases.

## Critique

The modern Second Amendment doctrine is vulnerable on administrability and on methodological selection. Heller's list of presumptively lawful regulations is famously undertheorized: Scalia offered no historical justification for why felon-in-possession laws, sensitive-place restrictions, or commercial-sale conditions survive originalist analysis. Those carve-outs read more like pragmatic concessions than principled applications, and they survive in Bruen via Kavanaugh's concurrence and in Rahimi via the rejection of the irresponsible-citizen theory without the Court ever placing them on a fully coherent textual footing.

Bruen's own methodological ambition generates its largest critique. Replacing means-end scrutiny with historical analogical reasoning sounds disciplining, but the choice of level of generality often determines the outcome. Breyer's Bruen dissent made the point in the abstract; Rahimi proved it concretely. When the Fifth Circuit read Bruen to demand a historical twin, § 922(g)(8) failed. When the Supreme Court read Bruen to demand a historical analogue, § 922(g)(8) passed. The gap between those readings is not trivial, and Thomas's solo Rahimi dissent warned that the majority's loosened standard effectively guts the framework Bruen adopted only two terms earlier. A central open question is how much methodological discipline remains when shared principles are enough and mixing and matching of burdens and justifications across historical regimes is permitted.

From an incorporation-theory perspective the doctrinal footing is also contested. McDonald's plurality took the Due Process path rather than revisit Slaughter-House, which ties the modern Second Amendment to substantive due process and to the load-bearing tension the incorporation lecture names as the main pressure point on twenty-first-century rights jurisprudence. Thomas's McDonald concurrence offered an originalist reappraisal through Privileges or Immunities, and Thomas and Gorsuch renewed that argument in Timbs. If a future Court moves incorporation to Privileges or Immunities, the Second Amendment's textual footing becomes cleaner, but adjacent doctrines (equal protection, unenumerated-rights substantive due process) face their own pressure.

Finally, the shall-issue versus may-issue distinction that makes Bruen administrable is textually thin. The Court offers no principled reason grounded in text, history, or tradition for why shall-issue regimes are permissible while discretionary regimes are not. It is a practical compromise that preserves forty-three States' licensing systems, which is a legitimate choice but sits uneasily with the methodological insistence of the majority.
