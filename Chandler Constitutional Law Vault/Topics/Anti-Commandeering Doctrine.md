# Anti-Commandeering Doctrine

## Overview

The anti-commandeering doctrine is a non-textual structural limit on Congress that emerged in the Rehnquist Court. Although the Tenth Amendment supplies the doctrinal hook, Justice O'Connor in New York v United States (1992) framed the limit as flowing from the Constitution's overall structure, not from the words of the Amendment alone. The principle has three operative branches: (1) Congress may not direct state legislatures to enact federal policy (New York), (2) Congress may not conscript state executive officers into administering a federal regulatory program (Printz), and (3) Congress may not prohibit states from authorizing conduct by forbidding them to repeal their own laws (Murphy v NCAA). Each branch rests on what O'Connor called "political accountability": when federal officials hide behind state implementation, voters cannot identify which government to blame.

The doctrine sits inside the broader Garcia / Tenth Amendment debate. Garcia v San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority (1985) overruled National League of Cities v Usery (1976) and held that the political process, not judicial enforcement, protects state sovereignty. Garcia has never been formally overruled. New York, Printz, and Murphy each impose substantive judicially-enforced limits on federal power that Garcia rejected. The unresolved tension between Garcia's process-federalism and the modern commandeering cases is the doctrine's central scholarly puzzle.

The doctrine matters because it sets the outer boundary of federal regulatory choice. Congress retains broad alternatives: direct federal regulation under the Commerce Clause, conditional spending under Article I § 8 (subject to South Dakota v Dole and NFIB v Sebelius), preemption of conflicting state law under the Supremacy Clause, and cooperative federalism schemes that allow but do not require state participation (Hodel). The anti-commandeering rule narrows only one tool, the direct command, while leaving the rest available.

---

## Governing Rule

> **Rule:** Congress may not directly command a state legislature to enact federal policy, may not conscript state executive officers to administer a federal regulatory program, and may not prohibit a state from repealing its own laws. Federal regulation of states must operate either through generally applicable rules that bind state and private actors alike (Reno v Condon), through preemption of conflicting state law, through conditional spending under the Dole framework, or through cooperative federalism schemes that leave the state genuinely free to participate or step aside.

## Doctrinal Development

### National League of Cities v Usery (1976)

The first modern Tenth Amendment limit on federal regulation of states. Justice Rehnquist held that Congress could not use the Commerce Clause to apply federal wage-and-hour law to state and municipal employees performing "traditional" or "integral" governmental functions. The decision read the Tenth Amendment as a substantive limit on Article I.

### Garcia v San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority (1985)

A 5-4 reversal of National League of Cities. Justice Blackmun, switching his vote, held that the Tenth Amendment imposes no judicially-enforced categorical limit on Congress's power to regulate states under the Commerce Clause. The Garcia majority adopted political-process federalism: state representation in Congress, not the courts, is the structural safeguard. Justice Rehnquist dissented, predicting that the holding would not endure. Garcia is the doctrinal pivot the modern commandeering cases must navigate around.

### Hodel v Virginia Surface Mining and Reclamation Association (1981)

Pre-New York case upholding federal surface-mining standards that gave states a choice: comply with federal regulatory standards as the price of operating a state regulatory program, or leave the field to federal regulation. Hodel established the cooperative-federalism baseline that survives every later commandeering decision: a "take it or leave it" choice does not commandeer.

### New York v United States (1992)

The Low-Level Radioactive Waste Policy Amendments Act required states that had not arranged for waste disposal to "take title" to the waste and become liable for it. Justice O'Connor (6-3) held the take-title provision unconstitutional. The federal government may not "simply commandeer the legislative processes of the States by directly compelling them to enact and enforce a federal regulatory program." O'Connor's two structural reasons reappear in every later case: (1) accountability — federal officials should answer for federal programs, not hide behind state legislators; (2) liberty — the original constitutional design dispersed power between two sovereigns precisely to protect individual liberty from concentrated authority. Congress retained ample alternatives: condition federal grants on state cooperation under Dole, preempt state law directly, or run a federal program itself.

### Printz v United States (1997)

The Brady Handgun Violence Prevention Act required state and local Chief Law Enforcement Officers to perform background checks on firearm purchasers during the interim before the federal NICS system was operational. Justice Scalia (5-4) extended New York's anti-commandeering rule from state legislatures to state executive officers. The opinion's three pillars: (1) historical practice across the Founding era did not include federal conscription of state executives; (2) the structural accountability theme inherited from O'Connor; (3) practical concern that federal cost-shifting onto states would distort the political feedback loop. Justice Scalia distinguished Testa v Katt (1947) as adjudicatory rather than executive, a line Professor Chandler describes as doctrinally fragile.

### Reno v Condon (2000)

A unanimous Court upheld the Driver's Privacy Protection Act against a commandeering challenge. The Act regulated the dissemination of personal data by both state DMVs and private resellers. Because the federal rule applied to states and private parties on identical terms and did not require states to administer any federal program against their citizens, it functioned as ordinary federal regulation, not commandeering. Reno v Condon supplies the governing safe harbor: a generally applicable federal rule that incidentally binds states is not commandeering.

### Murphy v NCAA (2018)

The Professional and Amateur Sports Protection Act prohibited states from "authorizing" sports gambling. New Jersey, after losing an earlier challenge, repealed its existing prohibition on sports gambling at casinos and racetracks. Justice Alito (7-2) held PASPA unconstitutional as applied to the repeal. A federal command that a state must keep certain prohibitions on its books is functionally equivalent to a command that the state legislate, and so violates New York. Alito identified three purposes of the doctrine — preserving liberty, preserving accountability, and preventing federal cost-shifting — and rejected the argument that PASPA only "preempted" state authorizations. Murphy thereby extends New York to a third commandeering form: the prohibition on repeals.

### Testa v Katt (1947) and FERC v Mississippi (1982)

Two pre-doctrine cases in tension with the modern rule. Testa held that state courts must hear federal causes of action and may not refuse to apply "foreign" federal statutes. FERC v Mississippi upheld a federal mandate that state public-utility commissions consider federal energy-conservation rate structures. Scalia in Printz tried to confine these cases to adjudication or to "consideration" requirements that fall short of administration. The doctrinal stability of those distinctions remains contested.

### South Dakota v Dole (1987) and NFIB v Sebelius (2012)

Dole and NFIB sit beside the commandeering cases as the conditional-spending boundary. See [[Cases/South Dakota v Dole (1987)|full brief]] for the four-part test (general welfare, unambiguous, germane, no independent constitutional bar). NFIB added an enforced coercion ceiling: withholding the entirety of an existing program (10 percent of a state's budget in NFIB) to force acceptance of an expansion is "a gun to the head" and crosses from encouragement into compulsion. See [[Cases/NFIB v Sebelius (2012)|full brief]]. Together, Dole and NFIB are the doctrinal substitute that Congress can reach for when commandeering is foreclosed.

### Arizona v United States (2012)

Anti-commandeering and preemption sit on adjacent doctrinal terrain. Arizona v United States is the canonical illustration: federal immigration law preempted core provisions of Arizona's S.B. 1070, but the Court left open whether the state's "show me your papers" inquiry would itself violate the anti-commandeering principle if applied differently. See [[Cases/Arizona v United States (2012)|full brief]] for the preemption analysis; the case demonstrates how preemption and anti-commandeering operate as parallel federalism limits with different doctrinal vocabularies.

## Comparison: Commandeering, Preemption, Conditional Spending

The three federal regulatory tools sit at different points on the federalism spectrum and produce different doctrinal vocabularies for the same underlying policy choice.

| Tool | What Congress May Do | What Congress May Not Do | Source of Limit |
|------|----------------------|--------------------------|-----------------|
| Direct regulation under enumerated powers | Bind states and private parties alike on the same terms (Reno v Condon) | Single out states for obligations not imposed on private parties | Anti-commandeering principle |
| Conditional spending | Attach germane conditions to grants of federal funds (Dole) | Threaten so much of an existing program that states have no real choice (NFIB) | Coercion limit on spending power |
| Preemption | Displace conflicting state law under the Supremacy Clause | Prohibit a state from repealing its own laws (Murphy) | Anti-commandeering principle |
| Direct command to state legislature | (none) | Compel a state to enact a federal program (New York) | Anti-commandeering principle |
| Direct command to state executive | (none) | Conscript state officers into federal program administration (Printz) | Anti-commandeering principle |

The asymmetry is the doctrine's most striking feature. Congress can completely ban sports gambling by criminalizing it federally, yet it cannot tell New Jersey to keep its own ban on the books. Congress can preempt state immigration enforcement, yet it cannot order Arizona officials to enforce federal immigration rules.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side

1. **Hypo:** Congress amends the Clean Air Act to require every state environmental agency to issue federal-format compliance reports for major pollution sources within the state, on penalty of state agency-budget loss imposed by federal court order. **Why:** This conscripts state executive officers (the agency staff) to administer a federal regulatory program against the state's residents, just as Printz forbade. The accountability concern is identical: federal officials would design the reporting regime while state officials would bear the political cost. Holding: unconstitutional commandeering.

2. **Hypo:** Congress passes a statute providing that any state law authorizing the sale of marijuana for recreational use is "without force or effect" and that states must keep marijuana criminalization on the books pending federal review. **Why:** This is Murphy's exact holding applied to a different drug. A federal command that a state must maintain existing prohibitions is functionally a command that a state legislate, and so violates New York. Congress could instead criminalize recreational marijuana federally and preempt state authorizations through prosecution; that path remains constitutional. Holding: unconstitutional commandeering.

### Opposite-Side

3. **Hypo:** Congress enacts a federal data-privacy statute requiring all entities — including state DMVs, state hospitals, and private companies — to obtain affirmative opt-in consent before reselling personal information. **Why:** This is Reno v Condon. The federal rule binds states and private parties on identical terms; states are regulated as actors in the data marketplace, not commandeered to administer a federal program. Holding: not commandeering, valid commerce-power regulation.

4. **Hypo:** Congress conditions 5 percent of a state's federal highway funds on the state enacting a 0.05 BAC drunk-driving threshold within three years. **Why:** This is South Dakota v Dole reapplied. The condition is unambiguous, germane to highway safety, in service of the general welfare, and has no independent constitutional bar. The percentage withheld is squarely in Dole's "mild encouragement" zone and well below the NFIB coercion ceiling. Holding: valid conditional spending, not commandeering.

### Fence-Sitter

5. **Hypo:** Congress passes a statute providing that any state agency that processes driver's-license applications must, as a condition of receiving 25 percent of its existing federal motor-vehicle administration grant, also process federal voter-registration applications using the federal form. **Why:** Two cross-cutting pressures. Reno v Condon supports the federal rule because driver-license processing is a state activity that Congress may regulate through generally applicable rules; the National Voter Registration Act precedent (the "motor voter" structure) historically survived. But Printz cuts the other way because the federal scheme conscripts state executive officers into administering a federal program (voter registration). And the 25 percent withholding sits in NFIB's twilight zone — substantially above Dole's 5 percent but well below NFIB's 10-percent-of-state-budget threshold. The case turns on whether the court frames the federal action as conditional spending (probably valid) or as direct executive commandeering (probably invalid), with the Reno v Condon "generally applicable" defense sitting between them.

## How to Spot on an Exam

The trigger fact pattern is a federal statute that tells a state government to do something. Three diagnostic questions in order:

1. **Who is the target of the federal command?** If it is private parties (with the state regulated only incidentally as a market actor), this is ordinary federal regulation, and the question is preemption / Commerce Clause, not commandeering. If the target is the state government itself, continue.

2. **Is the command direct or indirect?** A direct legislative or executive command is the New York / Printz heartland and almost certainly fails. An indirect command — a condition on a federal grant — runs through Dole's four prongs (general welfare, unambiguous, germane, no independent bar) and NFIB's coercion ceiling.

3. **Is the command "you must legislate" or "you must keep your laws as they are"?** Murphy added the second branch. A federal prohibition on repeals is functionally equivalent to a federal command that a state legislate, and so falls under New York.

Common professor-bait fact patterns: federal preemption that operates through state-officer enforcement (potentially Printz); conditional spending pegged to state policy adoption (Dole / NFIB analysis); generally applicable federal regulation that imposes administrative costs on state agencies (Reno v Condon defense available); cooperative federalism schemes with non-trivial federal funding tied to compliance (Hodel safe harbor available). Always identify Congress's regulatory alternatives — direct federal regulation, conditional spending, preemption — to demonstrate that anti-commandeering does not foreclose policy goals, only one mode of pursuing them.

## Critique

**The Garcia incoherence.** The anti-commandeering cases assume what Garcia denies — that the Tenth Amendment imposes a substantive judicially-enforced limit on Congress. Garcia held that the political process protects state sovereignty; New York, Printz, and Murphy each judicially enforce a substantive limit. The Court has never reconciled the two lines. Professor Chandler's framing in the Tenth Amendment lecture: Garcia survives on paper but loses on the ground. Critics from across the spectrum (Larry Kramer on the left, Michael Greve on the right) argue the Court should either resurrect Garcia openly or formally overrule it; the current "process for some Tenth Amendment cases, substance for others" is unprincipled.

**The Testa problem.** Why may Congress commandeer state courts to hear federal cases (Testa) while it cannot commandeer state sheriffs to enforce federal law (Printz)? Scalia answered "adjudicatory function" in Printz, but state courts adjudicate, sentence, and enforce just as state executive officers do. The Federalist Papers, on which Printz relies for the founding-era structural argument, did not draw the line at adjudication. Several commentators (Roderick Hills, Vicki Jackson) have argued that the Testa line is doctrinally unsustainable and that either Testa or Printz must yield.

**The accountability theme oversells.** The accountability rationale assumes voters cannot identify the federal source of a state-administered program. Empirically, this is doubtful: the Brady Act and PASPA both attracted national press coverage, and state officials in both cases publicly attributed the programs to Congress. The accountability argument also proves too much: every conditional spending grant produces some accountability blurring (do voters credit Congress or the state for state highways?), but Dole and Hodel survive. The doctrine selects only the strongest commandeering forms while permitting accountability-blurring through other channels.

**The preemption paradox.** Murphy's holding produces an awkward policy result: Congress can completely ban sports gambling by federalizing the crime, yet cannot prohibit state authorizations. The Constitution thus requires the heavier federal hand. This may be a feature (forcing Congress to internalize the political cost of preemption) or a bug (incentivizing federal criminalization where state regulation would suffice), depending on one's federalism priors. Murphy itself acknowledged the asymmetry and treated it as a constitutionally compelled feature of dual sovereignty.

**Originalist counter-current.** Justice Thomas (concurring in Printz and writing separately in NFIB) has argued that the proper Tenth Amendment limit is enumerated-powers analysis, not the structural anti-commandeering principle. On this reading, the question in Printz should have been whether the Brady Act regulates interstate commerce in firearms (a closer call than the majority acknowledged), not whether it commandeers state officers. Thomas's framing avoids the Garcia tension by reaching the same anti-federal-overreach result through enumerated-powers narrowing rather than through a non-textual structural inference.
