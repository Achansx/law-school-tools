# State Sovereign Immunity

## Overview

State sovereign immunity is the doctrine that an unconsenting state generally may not be sued in federal court by a private party. Its textual hook is the Eleventh Amendment, whose terms bar suits against a state by citizens of another state or of any foreign state. But the modern doctrine extends far beyond that text. Hans v Louisiana (1890) held that the Amendment reflects a broader structural principle of sovereign immunity that also forbids citizens from suing their own states in federal court on federal claims. Later cases extend the principle into state courts (Alden v Maine) and federal agency proceedings (Federal Maritime Commission v SC Ports Authority), turning the Eleventh Amendment's narrow text into a near-comprehensive shield.

The modern framework has three pillars. First, a clear-statement rule: Congress must make its intent to abrogate state immunity unmistakable in statutory text. Second, a constitutional-authority rule: only enforcement legislation under the Reconstruction Amendments (principally Section 5 of the Fourteenth) can validly abrogate immunity; Article I powers cannot. Third, a congruence-and-proportionality screen borrowed from City of Boerne: even valid Section 5 abrogations must be proportionate to documented constitutional violations by states. Ex Parte Young supplies a limited equitable workaround by permitting suits for prospective injunctive relief against state officers, though not for retroactive damages from the state treasury.

Chandler emphasizes that the doctrine's reach is largely judicial construction atop sparse text. The gap between the narrow text of the Eleventh Amendment and the sweeping immunity the Court has inferred from it is, in Professor Chandler's framing, the strongest argument for judicial minimalism: Bradley in Hans was solving a narrow bondholder problem, and the sweeping principle he announced attached itself to problems he never imagined (disability rights, employment discrimination, intellectual property, environmental enforcement).

## Governing Rule

> **Rule (paraphrased):** An unconsenting state is immune from private suits in federal court, in state courts on federal law, and in federal agency proceedings. Congress may abrogate that immunity only by making its intent unmistakably clear in statutory text and acting pursuant to a constitutional grant that itself limits state sovereignty, namely Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment. Abrogation legislation is tested for congruence and proportionality between documented constitutional violations by states and the remedy imposed. Suits for prospective injunctive relief against state officers remain available under Ex Parte Young when Congress has not otherwise prescribed a remedial scheme.

> **Rehnquist in Seminole Tribe (verbatim):** "Even when the Constitution vests in Congress complete lawmaking authority over a particular area, the Eleventh Amendment prevents congressional authorization of suits by private parties against unconsenting States."

> **Rehnquist in Fitzpatrick (verbatim):** "The Eleventh Amendment, and the principle of state sovereignty which it embodies, are necessarily limited by the enforcement provisions of Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment."

## Doctrinal Development

### Fitzpatrick v Bitzer (1976): The Section 5 Exception

Rehnquist's majority establishes the enduring principle that Congress's Section 5 power pierces state sovereign immunity. The structural logic is temporal: the Fourteenth Amendment postdates the Eleventh, and Section 5 by its own terms directs Congress to enforce constitutional limits on state conduct. When Congress acts under that specific enforcement grant, the Eleventh Amendment's immunity yields. Title VII's 1972 amendments extending liability to states for sex discrimination, backed by retroactive money damages, are a valid Section 5 abrogation. Fitzpatrick creates the single immunity exception that every later case treats as load-bearing. See [[Cases/Fitzpatrick v Bitzer (1976)|full brief]] and [[Lectures/Eleventh Amendment - State Sovereign Immunity|Eleventh Amendment lecture]].

### Seminole Tribe of Florida v Florida (1996): Article I Cannot Abrogate

Rehnquist's majority holds that Article I powers, including the Indian Commerce Clause, cannot authorize Congress to subject unconsenting states to private suits. Pennsylvania v Union Gas Co (1989), a fractured plurality that had read the Commerce Clause to permit abrogation, is overruled. The structural argument is the inverse of Fitzpatrick: Article I does not itself limit state sovereignty, so it cannot override the Eleventh Amendment's protection of that sovereignty. The opinion also restricts Ex Parte Young: where Congress has enacted a detailed remedial scheme (there, IGRA's negotiation-and-mediation mechanism), courts may not supplement that scheme with Young-style equitable relief. Seminole Tribe completes the constitutional map. Section 5 yields abrogation; Article I does not; Fitzpatrick is the sole dependable exception. See [[Cases/Seminole Tribe of Florida v Florida (1996)|full brief]].

### City of Boerne v Flores (1997): Section 5's Scope Is Bounded

Kennedy's majority, decided the Term after Seminole Tribe, narrows the Fitzpatrick exception. Section 5 is remedial and preventive, not substantive; Congress cannot use it to redefine or expand the content of constitutional rights. Valid Section 5 legislation must reflect a congruence and proportionality between documented constitutional violations by states and the legislative remedy. Boerne does not reject Fitzpatrick (Section 5 abrogation is still possible) but raises the bar for what counts as legitimate Section 5 enforcement. The combined Seminole-Boerne regime means many federal statutes cannot reach states at all: Article I statutes cannot abrogate, and would-be Section 5 statutes must pass Boerne's evidentiary screen. See [[Cases/City of Boerne v Flores (1997)|full brief]].

### Florida Prepaid (1999): The Evidentiary Demand Has Teeth

Rehnquist's majority applies Boerne to strike down the Patent Remedy Act's abrogation. State patent infringement does not automatically violate due process; only infringement coupled with denial of an adequate remedy does. Congress had assembled only a handful of instances of state infringement and almost no evidence that states had denied adequate process. The PRA's nationwide abrogation was therefore disproportionate to any documented constitutional violation. Florida Prepaid transforms Boerne into a demanding evidentiary regime and confirms that Article I's Patent Clause cannot supply what Section 5 will not. See [[Cases/Florida Prepaid Postsecondary Education Expense Board v College Savings Bank (1999)|full brief]].

### Board of Trustees v Garrett (2001): Low Scrutiny Narrows the Exception

Rehnquist's majority holds that ADA Title I's abrogation fails. Because disability classifications trigger only rational basis, the constitutional floor for Congress to enforce is low, and the record must show states engaging in conduct that would itself fail rational basis. Most of the record reflected private or local-government conduct or conduct rational basis would excuse. Title I's reasonable-accommodation mandate went far beyond the floor. Garrett ties the abrogation inquiry tightly to equal-protection scrutiny tiers, showing that Fitzpatrick's space is smallest when Congress is enforcing rational-basis rights. See [[Cases/Board of Trustees of University of Alabama v Garrett (2001)|full brief]].

### Nevada v Hibbs (2003): Heightened Scrutiny Broadens the Exception

Rehnquist's majority upholds FMLA family-care leave as a valid Section 5 abrogation. Sex classifications trigger intermediate scrutiny, which lifts the constitutional floor and enlarges the prophylactic space. Congress had also documented a pervasive pattern of state leave policies rooted in gender stereotypes, where states granted women generous maternity leave while denying men comparable family-care leave. The FMLA's uniform leave entitlement was congruent and proportional to that documented constitutional harm. Hibbs demonstrates that the Fitzpatrick exception remains meaningful when Congress enforces heightened-scrutiny rights with a real record. See [[Cases/Nevada Department of Human Resources v Hibbs (2003)|full brief]].

### Tennessee v Lane (2004): Fundamental Rights and As-Applied Abrogation

Stevens's majority upholds ADA Title II as applied to courthouse access. Access to judicial proceedings is a fundamental due process right, which broadens the prophylactic space available to Congress. Congress had documented systemic exclusion of disabled individuals from state courthouses. The Title II requirement of reasonable modifications for court access was congruent and proportional. Lane also introduces as-applied abrogation analysis: a Section 5 statute may validly abrogate immunity in some applications and fail in others, letting courts police the line without a facial invalidation. See [[Cases/Tennessee v Lane (2004)|full brief]].

### Allen v Cooper (2020): Florida Prepaid Endures

Kagan's unanimous Court applies Florida Prepaid to the Copyright Remedy Clarification Act and reaches the same result. The CRCA and PRA were materially equivalent in scope and record, and twenty years did not close the evidentiary gap. Concurrences by Thomas and Breyer signal openness to reconsidering Florida Prepaid in a future case with a richer record, but the framework itself stands. Allen v Cooper confirms that the combined Seminole-Boerne-Florida Prepaid regime denies states uniform exposure to federal copyright and patent suits and leaves Congress to build a stronger factual foundation if it wants abrogation. See [[Cases/Allen v Cooper (2020)|full brief]].

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side

1. **Hypo:** Congress amends Title VII to extend protected-class coverage and expressly abrogates state sovereign immunity. A state employee sues in federal court for racial discrimination seeking back pay and compensatory damages. **Why:** Like Fitzpatrick, the statute is Section 5 enforcement of equal protection, makes the abrogation unmistakable, and regulates race-based state employment decisions that themselves trigger strict scrutiny. Valid abrogation; the suit proceeds.

2. **Hypo:** Congress enacts the Voting Access for the Elderly Act, grounded in Section 5, authorizing damages suits against states that impose facially neutral voter-ID requirements shown to disenfranchise elderly voters, after congressional hearings document a pattern of state refusal to implement absentee accommodations. **Why:** Political-participation rights trigger heightened review in this domain, the record documents constitutional-level state violations, and the remedy targets the documented harm. Abrogation upheld under Hibbs-Lane.

### Opposite-Side

3. **Hypo:** Congress enacts the Environmental Justice Communities Act, grounded solely in the Commerce Clause, expressly abrogating state immunity and authorizing private damages suits against states for any permitting decision that disproportionately affects a minority community. **Why:** Seminole Tribe forecloses Article I abrogation; without Section 5 grounding, immunity stands regardless of the statutory clarity. Invalid.

4. **Hypo:** Congress enacts a State Trademark Remedy Act modeled on the Patent Remedy Act, abrogating state immunity for trademark infringement. The legislative record contains generalized concern about state universities using third-party trademarks in marketing but no evidence of denial of adequate state-court remedies. **Why:** Controlled by Florida Prepaid and Allen v Cooper. Rational-basis-plus-remedy analysis means the floor is low, the record fails to show constitutionally inadequate process, and nationwide abrogation is disproportionate. Invalid.

### Fence-Sitter

5. **Hypo:** Congress enacts a Digital Access to Government Services Act, grounded in Section 5, requiring state online benefits portals to meet accessibility standards and authorizing damages suits against noncompliant states. The record includes Lane-style evidence of disability exclusion from in-person courtrooms combined with newer but thinner evidence of online-portal exclusion. A disabled plaintiff sues a state whose unemployment-insurance portal is inaccessible. **Why:** Lane upheld Title II as applied to court access because access to judicial proceedings is fundamental. Access to online benefits portals sits closer to the Garrett end (rational-basis equal protection) than to Lane's fundamental-rights core, though due process arguments about access to benefits complicate the line. Courts could adopt Lane's as-applied methodology and either uphold or strike the specific application. Genuinely unclear.

## How to Spot on an Exam

Sovereign immunity issues surface whenever a private party sues a state, a state agency, or a state official for money damages under federal law. Look for four triggers. First, the defendant is a state, a state department, or a state university; arms of the state count. Second, the plaintiff seeks retroactive money damages or some form of relief that runs against the state treasury. Third, the source of the federal cause of action is a statute rather than the Constitution directly; Constitution-based claims proceed under Ex Parte Young's officer fiction. Fourth, the statute either does or does not contain express language abrogating state immunity.

The analytical path follows four steps. Step one: ask whether the defendant is the state itself or a political subdivision; sovereign immunity protects states and arms of states, not counties, municipalities, or private actors. Step two: identify the constitutional grant Congress invoked. If Article I only, Seminole Tribe forecloses abrogation and the suit fails absent consent. If Section 5 or other Reconstruction enforcement clause, continue. Step three: check the clear-statement rule; Congress must unmistakably express intent to abrogate. Step four: apply the Boerne congruence-and-proportionality test with attention to the scrutiny tier (Garrett for rational basis, Hibbs for intermediate, Lane for fundamental rights). Consider as-applied analysis if the statute sweeps broadly.

If a money-damages suit is blocked, ask whether Ex Parte Young supports a prospective-injunctive alternative against the responsible state official. That alternative is unavailable where Congress has enacted a detailed remedial scheme of its own (Seminole Tribe's gloss on Ex Parte Young).

## Critique

The doctrine's deepest criticism is that its text cannot support its weight. The Eleventh Amendment by its terms covers only suits against a state by a citizen of another state or a foreign citizen. Hans extended the principle to suits by a state's own citizens on a non-textual structural theory that Stevens and Souter have repeatedly challenged on historical grounds. The Souter dissent in Seminole Tribe is the leading academic statement that the Framers did not understand immunity to bar federal-court suits by a state's own citizens, and that Hans was wrong about the pre-ratification record.

A second line questions the practical effect. The combined regime of Seminole Tribe (no Article I abrogation) plus Boerne-Florida Prepaid (heavy record requirements under Section 5) leaves many federal rights without a federal damages remedy against states. For environmental, labor, intellectual property, and general civil rights enforcement against states, the doctrine produces a structural gap: Congress may define the right, state conduct may violate it, and yet the injured party has no damages remedy because the state is immune and the statutory basis for suit is Article I. Critics argue this distorts federal-rights enforcement and disproportionately protects states against the populations the Reconstruction Amendments were designed to protect.

A third line challenges the scrutiny-tier dependency. The Garrett-Hibbs-Lane pattern ties Congress's abrogation authority to the Court's own equal-protection hierarchy. Because the tier scheme was never textually mandated and has shifted over time (sex moved to intermediate in 1976; sexual orientation's classification remains contested), the scope of congressional enforcement power tracks judicial choices about tiers. That is, Professor Chandler notes, a distinctive Rehnquist-era inversion of Katzenbach v Morgan's McCulloch-style deference: Congress defines what enforcement is needed, but the Court defines the tiers that set the prophylactic ceiling.

A fourth line questions the Ex Parte Young escape valve's coherence. If sovereign immunity is a serious constitutional principle, the officer fiction that permits suits against state officials for prospective relief is a large concession. If the fiction is defensible, critics ask why it does not extend to the Edelman line barring retroactive relief that ultimately comes from the state treasury. The doctrine has answered with pragmatic lines rather than principled ones, which makes the entire structure vulnerable to further judicial reconfiguration.
