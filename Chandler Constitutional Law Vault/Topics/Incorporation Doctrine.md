# Incorporation Doctrine

## Overview

The Incorporation Doctrine is the mechanism by which guarantees of the Bill of Rights, originally binding only the federal government, are applied to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment. The doctrinal story is less a clean textual exposition than a century-long workaround. The most natural textual vehicle, the Privileges or Immunities Clause, was gutted in 1873 by the Slaughter-House Cases, and the Court has since accomplished through the Due Process Clause what the Fourteenth Amendment's drafters expected the Privileges or Immunities Clause to do. The vault's lecture on incorporation frames the result as an achievement produced by doctrinal sleight of hand: clause shifting from Privileges or Immunities to Due Process, fundamental-rights framing that treats protections as inherent in due process rather than as direct Bill of Rights application, selective application one right at a time, and the silent treatment under which most incorporation decisions never mention Slaughter-House or Cruikshank.

---

## Governing Rule

> **Rule:** A provision of the Bill of Rights applies against the States if it is fundamental to our scheme of ordered liberty or deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition. The modern selective-incorporation framework operates through the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. Once incorporated, a right applies against the States with full federal strength; there are no watered-down state versions of an incorporated right.

A separate methodological question, currently unsettled, is whether incorporation should instead proceed through the Privileges or Immunities Clause. Two Justices (Thomas and Gorsuch) have urged the Court to revisit Slaughter-House; the Court has not done so.

## Doctrinal Development

### Antebellum Baseline: Barron v Baltimore (1833)

Before the Civil War, Chief Justice Marshall held in Barron that the Bill of Rights applies only to the federal government. The Anti-Federalists who demanded the first ten amendments feared centralized power, so applying them against the States would have inverted their purpose. Under Barron a State could violate what would have been a federal constitutional right with no recourse under the Bill of Rights. See [[Lectures/The Incorporation Doctrine|lecture]].

### The Fourteenth Amendment and the Slaughter-House Detour (1868 to 1873)

The Fourteenth Amendment ratified in 1868 contained three clauses with independent potential bearing on state power: Due Process, Equal Protection, and Privileges or Immunities. Representative John Bingham, the Amendment's principal drafter, intended the Privileges or Immunities language to apply the Bill of Rights against the States. Five years later, a five-to-four majority in the Slaughter-House Cases drew a fatal distinction between the privileges of state citizenship and those of national citizenship and held that the Fourteenth Amendment protected only the latter, which the majority identified as a near-trivial list (access to seaports, the right to travel to Washington, protection on the high seas). Justice Field's dissent warned that the majority had rendered the Clause a vain and idle enactment. Cruikshank in 1876 then held that the First and Second Amendments operate as restrictions on the powers of Congress, not as limitations on the powers of the States, slamming the door on any early path to incorporation. After Slaughter-House and Cruikshank, the Privileges or Immunities Clause was a dead letter. See [[Lectures/The Incorporation Doctrine|lecture]].

### The Back Door Opens: Due Process Incorporation (1897 to 1947)

Chicago Burlington and Quincy Railroad v Chicago (1897) held that States cannot take private property without just compensation, without calling the move incorporation. Justice Harlan characterized just compensation as inherent in due process itself, achieving a functionally identical result by textual relocation. Gitlow v New York (1925) casually assumed that freedom of speech and of the press are among the fundamental personal rights and liberties protected by the Due Process Clause, almost as an aside, with no discussion of Slaughter-House or Cruikshank. Palko v Connecticut (1937) supplied the theoretical scaffolding: Justice Cardozo wrote that the Due Process Clause may absorb specific privileges or immunities to the extent that neither liberty nor justice would exist without them, asking whether a right is implicit in the concept of ordered liberty. Adamson v California (1947) staged the great debate: Justice Black in dissent argued for total incorporation of the Bill of Rights and urged the Court to overrule Barron directly; Justice Frankfurter for the majority defended selective incorporation. Selective won. See [[Lectures/The Incorporation Doctrine|lecture]].

### The Warren Court Refinement: Duncan v Louisiana (1968)

Duncan shifted the Palko formulation to whether a right is fundamental to the American scheme of justice, a move from abstract philosophy toward American legal tradition that favored incorporation. Duncan also announced a key principle: once incorporated, rights apply with full federal strength, with no watered-down state versions. This is still the governing baseline for selective incorporation inquiries. See [[Lectures/The Incorporation Doctrine|lecture]].

### The Modern Centerpiece: McDonald v City of Chicago (2010)

McDonald is the doctrinally revealing modern case because Justice Thomas in concurrence finally names what the Court had spent over a century avoiding. The plurality opinion by Justice Alito applied the standard selective-incorporation framework, finding the right to keep and bear arms recognized in [[Cases/District of Columbia v Heller (2008)|Heller]] fundamental to our scheme of ordered liberty and deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition. Alito explicitly declined to revisit Slaughter-House, characterizing the question as an academic debate and preferring the well-trodden Due Process path. Thomas's concurrence reached the same result through the Privileges or Immunities Clause and would have overruled Slaughter-House and Cruikshank, arguing that substantive due process is a contradiction in terms and that the Privileges or Immunities Clause, as originally understood, protected the rights of national citizenship, including at a minimum the rights enumerated in the Bill of Rights. McDonald is therefore the modern case in which both methodological routes are laid side by side: Due Process incorporation (four Justices) and Privileges or Immunities incorporation (Thomas, supplying the fifth vote). See [[Cases/McDonald v City of Chicago (2010)|full brief]].

### Post-Incorporation Application: New York State Rifle and Pistol Association v Bruen (2022)

Bruen presupposes McDonald. The entire opinion is framed around the Fourteenth Amendment because the regulator is New York, not the federal government; Thomas's majority relies expressly on McDonald's incorporation as the predicate for the Fourteenth Amendment holding against New York. Bruen is therefore the clearest post-incorporation application case in the vault: once a Bill of Rights guarantee is incorporated, its scope and standard of review can continue to evolve at the federal level and the States remain bound to whatever the federal standard requires. The Duncan principle (no watered-down state versions) means that Bruen's text-history-and-tradition framework applies to state and federal firearms regulation on identical terms. See [[Cases/New York State Rifle and Pistol Association v Bruen (2022)|full brief]].

## Doctrinal Comparison: Palko, Duncan, McDonald, Timbs

The selective-incorporation test has been reformulated three times in ways the case briefs make visible at close range. The columns below track the shift in test language, the clause invoked, the treatment of Privileges or Immunities, and the operative rule for state-versus-federal strength.

| Axis | [[Cases/Palko v Connecticut (1937)\|Palko]] | [[Cases/Duncan v Louisiana (1968)\|Duncan]] | [[Cases/McDonald v City of Chicago (2010)\|McDonald]] | [[Cases/Timbs v Indiana (2019)\|Timbs]] |
|------|--------|----------|-----------|--------|
| Test language | "Implicit in the concept of ordered liberty" / "neither liberty nor justice would exist without" the right | "Fundamental to the American scheme of justice" and "deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition" | McDonald collapses both formulations: "fundamental to our scheme of ordered liberty" or "deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition" | Applies McDonald's combined formulation mechanically to the Excessive Fines Clause |
| Frame of reference | "Civilized societies" in the abstract (Anglo-European legal traditions broadly) | Narrower: American legal tradition and Anglo-American practice | American tradition with history-and-tradition evidentiary focus | Magna Carta to the Founding to post-Reconstruction state constitutions |
| Clause invoked | Due Process (selective; no discussion of Privileges or Immunities) | Due Process | Due Process (plurality); Privileges or Immunities (Thomas concurrence supplying the fifth vote) | Due Process (majority); Privileges or Immunities (Gorsuch concurrence, Thomas concurrence in judgment) |
| Watered-down state versions? | Not directly addressed | Expressly rejected: once incorporated, state right applies at full federal strength | Endorses and applies Duncan's no-watered-down rule | Same as McDonald |
| Result for defendant | Palka loses; double-jeopardy protection not incorporated at the time (overruled by Benton, 1969) | Duncan wins; jury trial incorporated | McDonald wins; Second Amendment incorporated | Timbs wins; Excessive Fines Clause incorporated |

Reading the table vertically reveals how little the underlying test has actually moved. The shifts are rhetorical relocations within a continuous selective-incorporation tradition, with the substantive change being the decision in each generation about which specific rights meet the relevant formulation. The Privileges or Immunities alternative remains dormant but consistently signaled (Thomas in McDonald, Thomas and Gorsuch in Timbs), and a doctrinal originalist Court could reopen that question without formally disturbing the incorporated rights already on the books.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side

1. **Hypo:** A State enacts a statute barring newspapers from publishing editorials critical of the Governor, subject to criminal penalties. **Why:** Freedom of speech and the press were assumed to be incorporated in Gitlow and have been applied against the States ever since. The State statute is subject to the same First Amendment analysis that would apply to a federal statute (Duncan's no-watered-down-versions principle), and content-based speech restrictions on political criticism fall at the core of the protected right. The statute fails.

2. **Hypo:** A city passes an ordinance that effectively bans operable handguns in the home for self-defense. **Why:** The Second Amendment right recognized in [[Cases/District of Columbia v Heller (2008)|Heller]] was incorporated against the States in [[Cases/McDonald v City of Chicago (2010)|McDonald]]. The Chicago and Oak Park ordinances in McDonald had precisely this character, and McDonald held they are subject to the Second Amendment's protections. Under Duncan, the state-level right is identical in strength to the federal right. The ordinance fails on materially the same grounds as D.C.'s ordinance did in Heller.

### Opposite-Side

3. **Hypo:** A State prosecutes a defendant before a grand jury panel of nine rather than by indictment. **Why:** The Fifth Amendment's grand jury indictment guarantee has never been incorporated against the States. Hurtado v California (1884), discussed in the lecture, held that due process does not require grand jury indictment in state prosecutions, and nothing since has disturbed that holding. The State may prosecute by information or by a non-traditional grand jury without violating the Fourteenth Amendment, notwithstanding that the federal government would be bound by the Fifth Amendment's text.

4. **Hypo:** A State sets an excessive-bail threshold that differs in operation from the federal excessive-bail doctrine under the Eighth Amendment. **Why:** The Eighth Amendment's Excessive Bail Clause has not been expressly incorporated. The lecture identifies grand jury indictment, civil jury trial, and excessive bail as the principal unincorporated provisions. A State is not bound by the federal doctrine as such; any challenge must travel through Fourteenth Amendment Due Process directly, which is a narrower channel than the incorporated Bill of Rights guarantee.

### Fence-Sitter

5. **Hypo:** A State enacts a handgun-microstamping statute that imprints a unique identifier on each cartridge case discharged. A resident sues, arguing the regulation conflicts with the Second Amendment as applied to the State under [[Cases/McDonald v City of Chicago (2010)|McDonald]]. **Why:** Under Duncan's no-watered-down-versions principle, the challenge is analyzed under the same [[Cases/New York State Rifle and Pistol Association v Bruen (2022)|Bruen]] text-history-and-tradition framework that would apply at the federal level. Whether the statute falls depends not on incorporation but on whether the government can identify a relevantly similar historical analogue under Bruen. Incorporation does the threshold work; it does not supply the substantive test.

## How to Spot on an Exam

Signals that an incorporation issue is in play:

- A state or municipal government is the regulator, and the challenger invokes a Bill of Rights guarantee directly rather than through a generic Fourteenth Amendment liberty theory.
- The case asks whether a particular Bill of Rights guarantee has ever been incorporated. When the answer is yes, the analysis proceeds exactly as if the federal government were the regulator (Duncan principle). When the answer is unsettled, both the Palko-Duncan selective incorporation framework and the Thomas-Gorsuch Privileges or Immunities alternative should be canvassed.
- A state law conflicts with a doctrine the Supreme Court has articulated only in a federal-regulator case. Ask whether the underlying Bill of Rights guarantee has been incorporated. If yes, Duncan controls and there is no room for a weaker state-level standard.

Analytical framework to deploy:

1. Identify the Bill of Rights guarantee allegedly at issue.
2. Confirm incorporation status. The modern incorporation line is long and the safest catalogue comes from the vault's lecture on incorporation: First Amendment speech, press, and religion; Fourth Amendment search, seizure, and exclusionary rule; Fifth Amendment self-incrimination, double jeopardy, just compensation (and the grand jury guarantee, unincorporated); Sixth Amendment jury trial, confrontation, compulsory process, and counsel; Eighth Amendment cruel and unusual punishment, and excessive fines (Timbs); Second Amendment keep and bear arms (McDonald). Note the holdouts.
3. If incorporated, apply the federal doctrine with full force against the state (Duncan).
4. If unincorporated, either (a) argue for incorporation under the Palko-Duncan framework asking whether the right is fundamental to the American scheme of justice and deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition, or (b) frame the claim as a freestanding Fourteenth Amendment Due Process claim, which is a narrower channel.
5. Flag the Privileges or Immunities alternative if the question invites methodological engagement. Two votes for revival (Thomas, Gorsuch) and intellectual sympathy from others.

## Critique

The Incorporation Doctrine is textually awkward. It uses the Due Process Clause, a procedural guarantee, to deliver substantive Bill of Rights protections against the States. Justice Thomas's McDonald concurrence argues that this is a contradiction in terms: a clause guaranteeing only process before deprivation of liberty cannot define the substance of protected rights. The honest critique is that the Court has accomplished through the Due Process Clause what the Fourteenth Amendment's drafters expected the Privileges or Immunities Clause to do. The clause-shifting move lets the Court claim fidelity to Slaughter-House while reaching the opposite result.

The Due Process route also creates a doctrinal entanglement with substantive due process. The same theoretical move that powers incorporation also powers Lochner, Griswold, and Obergefell. If substantive due process is illegitimate, as originalist critics argue, then so, in theory, is the entire incorporation edifice. Alito in McDonald deliberately declined to reconsider Slaughter-House in part because opening the Privileges or Immunities question would unsettle substantive due process, equal protection, and unenumerated rights. The lecture treats this linkage as a load-bearing wall of modern doctrine.

A progressive critique of the selective-incorporation framework, associated with Justice Black's Adamson dissent, is that the Palko-Duncan test gives the Court boundless power under natural law to expand and contract constitutional standards periodically. The test's subjectivity, which is also its utility for a Court proceeding one right at a time, resists principled application and invites level-of-generality manipulation. Black's proposed cure, total incorporation grounded in the historical evidence that the Fourteenth Amendment's framers intended the Privileges or Immunities Clause to apply the full Bill of Rights, has never commanded a majority but remains theoretically plausible.

A Privileges or Immunities revival is a live possibility but an uncertain one. The textual argument has real force: the Clause speaks directly to privileges and immunities of citizens, language that maps naturally onto the Bill of Rights. Thomas's McDonald concurrence offered an extensive originalist reappraisal, and in Timbs v Indiana (2019) both Thomas and Gorsuch urged reconsideration. But what the Clause would protect beyond the Bill of Rights is the uncertainty that may explain judicial hesitation. The Court has not shown willingness to open that question.

Open questions the doctrine leaves unresolved include: whether the remaining unincorporated provisions (grand jury indictment, civil jury trial, excessive bail) would be incorporated if squarely presented today; whether a Privileges or Immunities revival would change substantive outcomes or merely change the textual hook; and whether incorporation's Due Process scaffolding survives if substantive due process itself is narrowed or abandoned.
