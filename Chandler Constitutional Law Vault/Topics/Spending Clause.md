# Spending Clause

## Overview

The Spending Clause is the textual hook for one of Congress's most important regulatory levers: the power to influence state and private behavior by attaching conditions to federal grant funds. Article I Section 8 Clause 1 authorizes Congress to lay and collect taxes and provide for the general welfare. United States v Butler (1936) settled, against Madison's narrower view, that general welfare is an independent grant of authority not limited to the other enumerated powers (the Hamiltonian reading). South Dakota v Dole (1987) supplied the canonical four-part test that limits conditional spending without seriously constraining it: general welfare, unambiguous condition, germaneness, no independent constitutional bar, with a non-judicially-enforceable coercion overlay tucked into the dictum. NFIB v Sebelius (2012) finally activated the coercion prong, holding the ACA's Medicaid expansion condition unconstitutional because it threatened all preexisting Medicaid funding for refusing the new severable expansion.

The Clause does double duty in modern federalism. It is the structural workaround for anti-commandeering: Congress cannot order states to legislate (Printz, New York v United States) but can pay them to do so. It is also the primary vehicle for conditioning private-recipient behavior on compliance with federal policy preferences, raising unconstitutional-conditions questions that the Court has worked out in cases like Rust v Sullivan (1991), Legal Services Corp v Velazquez (2001), and Agency for International Development v Alliance for Open Society International (2013).

For exam purposes, conditional-spending questions sort into two clusters: state-recipient questions (Dole / NFIB framework) and private-recipient questions (Rust / Velazquez / AID framework). Both clusters share the structural insight that government funding is power, and both produce doctrinal compromises that limit the most coercive uses without prohibiting the leverage entirely.

## Governing Rule

> **Rule (Conditional Spending, State Recipients):** Congress may attach conditions on federal funds offered to states under the Spending Clause if (1) the spending is for the general welfare; (2) the condition is unambiguously stated so states can knowingly accept; (3) the condition is germane to the federal interest in the particular program; and (4) no other constitutional provision independently bars the condition. After NFIB v Sebelius (2012), a fifth limit applies: the financial inducement may not be so coercive that it operates as economic dragooning, leaving states no real choice but to accept.

> **Rule (Unconstitutional Conditions, Private Recipients):** The government may selectively fund activities and define the limits of its own programs (Rust v Sullivan, 1991), but may not impose conditions that reach beyond the funded program to restrict the recipient's First-Amendment-protected activity outside the program (Legal Services Corp v Velazquez, 2001; Agency for International Development v Alliance for Open Society International, 2013). The operative distinction is between defining the program (permitted) and leveraging funding to control the recipient's outside-the-program speech and policy (forbidden).

## Doctrinal Development

### United States v Butler (1936): The Hamiltonian Reading

Butler invalidated the Agricultural Adjustment Act on Tenth Amendment grounds but, in dictum that has structured every subsequent case, adopted Hamilton's reading of the General Welfare Clause: Congress's spending authority is an independent power, not limited to the subjects of the other enumerated powers. The narrower Madisonian reading (that spending must serve a separately enumerated end) was rejected. The Hamiltonian reading is the foundation of every modern conditional-spending doctrine. No vault brief yet; developed in [[Lectures/NFIB v Sebelius - ACA and Taxing Power|NFIB v Sebelius - ACA and Taxing Power]].

### South Dakota v Dole (1987): The Four-Part Test

Dole upheld 23 U.S.C. § 158, which directed the Secretary of Transportation to withhold 5% of federal highway funds from states allowing the purchase or public possession of alcohol by persons under twenty-one. Chief Justice Rehnquist articulated the four-part test that has dominated conditional-spending doctrine since: general welfare (with substantial deference), unambiguous condition, germaneness, and no independent bar. The opinion floated a coercion principle drawn from Steward Machine Co v Davis but treated it as essentially non-justiciable. The result was a quarter century of doctrinal drift: Congress could buy state regulatory compliance on almost any subject by pricing the withholding below the coercion threshold. See [[Cases/South Dakota v Dole (1987)|full brief]].

### Rust v Sullivan (1991): The Define-the-Program Principle

Rust upheld HHS regulations prohibiting Title X family-planning grantees from providing abortion counseling. Chief Justice Rehnquist's majority held that the government does not infringe a constitutional right when it selectively funds one activity to the exclusion of another. The condition defined the program (federal family-planning funds support pre-conception services and childbirth, not abortion); it did not restrict the recipient's protected activity outside the program. Rust is the foundational define-the-program case and the conceptual sibling to Dole's deferential framework on the private-recipient side of conditional spending. See [[Cases/Rust v Sullivan (1991)|full brief]].

### Legal Services Corp v Velazquez (2001): The Distortion Limit

Velazquez invalidated a Legal Services Corporation funding condition that prohibited grantees from challenging existing welfare law. Justice Kennedy's majority held that the condition distorted the legal-aid function in ways that interfered with the recipient's protected speech in court, exceeded define-the-program (Rust), and impermissibly silenced legal arguments the recipient was professionally obligated to make. Velazquez supplies the distortion limit: define-the-program permits Congress to set program scope but does not permit Congress to use funding leverage to suppress speech inherent to the recipient's protected activity. See [[Cases/Legal Services Corp v Velazquez (2001)|full brief]].

### NFIB v Sebelius (2012): Coercion Activated

NFIB held that the ACA's Medicaid expansion condition was unconstitutionally coercive. The condition threatened all preexisting Medicaid funding (about 10% of the average state's budget) for refusing the severable new expansion. Chief Justice Roberts found that the threat of losing all Medicaid funding was a gun to the head that crossed Dole's coercion line. The remedy was severance: states were free to accept or reject the expansion without losing existing funds. NFIB is the first time since Steward Machine that the Court actually enforced the coercion prong, and it reset the conditional-spending baseline by signalling that very large dollar stakes attached to severable expansions are constitutionally suspect. See [[Cases/NFIB v Sebelius (2012)|full brief]].

### Agency for International Development v Alliance for Open Society International (2013): The Policy Requirement Limit

AID v AOSI invalidated a federal HIV/AIDS funding condition that required private-organization recipients to adopt a policy explicitly opposing prostitution and sex trafficking. Chief Justice Roberts's majority held that the condition exceeded define-the-program because it required the recipient to adopt the government's viewpoint as its own, even outside the federally-funded program. Rust permits the government to set program scope; it does not permit the government to commandeer the recipient's identity. AID v AOSI sharpened the Rust-Velazquez line by adding the policy-requirement limit: conditions on funding may not require recipients to profess a particular viewpoint as their own. See [[Cases/Agency for International Development v Alliance for Open Society International (2013)|full brief]].

## Comparison: Define-the-Program versus Distort-the-Recipient

The central question on the unconstitutional-conditions side of the Spending Clause is whether a funding condition defines what the federal program does or controls what the recipient says and is.

| Case | Condition | Result | Reason |
|------|-----------|--------|--------|
| **Rust** | Title X grantees may not provide abortion counseling | Upheld | Defines the program (federal funds support family planning leading to childbirth, not abortion) |
| **Velazquez** | LSC grantees may not challenge existing welfare law in court | Struck down | Distorts the recipient's protected legal-aid function and silences arguments inherent to the work |
| **AID v AOSI** | HIV/AIDS recipients must adopt policy explicitly opposing prostitution | Struck down | Requires the recipient to adopt the government's viewpoint as its own outside the program |

The line is operational rather than verbal. A condition that says we will not fund X unless Y is done with the funds is define-the-program (Rust). A condition that says we will not fund X unless the recipient also says Z about Y outside the funds is distort-the-recipient (Velazquez, AID v AOSI).

## Comparison: Spending Clause versus Appropriations Clause

The Spending Clause and the Appropriations Clause are easily blurred. They cover overlapping fact patterns but ask categorically different questions.

| Axis | Spending Clause (Art I § 8 cl 1) | Appropriations Clause (Art I § 9 cl 7) |
|------|----------------------------------|----------------------------------------|
| Question asked | May Congress spend for the asserted purpose, and may it attach this condition? | Has the money been validly authorized for disbursement by enacted law? |
| Doctrinal frame | Substantive / four-part Dole test plus NFIB coercion ceiling | Procedural / formalist — three-element CFPB v CFSA test (enactment, source, designated purpose) |
| Relevant actor | Polices Congress's substantive authority over money plus state acceptance of conditions | Polices Congress (must enact) and the President (must execute, not revise) |
| Lead modern case | South Dakota v Dole (1987); NFIB v Sebelius (2012) | CFPB v CFSA (2024); Clinton v City of New York (1998) |
| Failure mode | Substantive overreach, ambiguous condition, non-germane condition, or coercion of states | No appropriation, or unilateral executive cancellation/impoundment of an appropriation |
| Co-implication | When Congress overspends or coerces, both clauses may be raised; but the Spending-Clause inquiry presupposes a valid appropriation | When Congress fails to enact at all, only the Appropriations Clause is implicated; Spending Clause has no work to do |

The shorthand: the Spending Clause asks whether Congress *may* spend; the Appropriations Clause asks whether Congress *has* spent in the form the Constitution requires (a duly enacted law identifying source and purpose). See [[Topics/Appropriations Clause|Appropriations Clause]] for the procedural counterpart and the impoundment line.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side

1. **Hypo:** Congress conditions 5% of federal Title I education funds on state adoption of a uniform reading-proficiency benchmark aligned with a federal literacy program. **Why:** Under Dole the condition is unambiguous, germane to the federal interest in literacy spending, pursues the general welfare, and the 5% withholding falls in Dole's mild encouragement zone. No independent constitutional bar applies. The condition is constitutional.
2. **Hypo:** Congress conditions a federal arts grant on the recipient agreeing not to use grant funds for politically partisan campaign activity. **Why:** The condition defines the limits of the federal arts program (federal arts funds support art, not partisan campaigning) and does not silence the recipient's protected non-arts speech outside the program. Under Rust, the condition is constitutional.

### Opposite-Side

3. **Hypo:** Congress conditions 95% of federal Medicaid funding on state adoption of a new federal long-term care insurance program that Congress could not constitutionally compel directly. **Why:** Under NFIB v Sebelius the financial pressure crosses the coercion line; threatening to withdraw 95% of Medicaid leaves states no real choice. The condition is unconstitutional as economic dragooning regardless of whether it would survive Dole's four-part formal test.
4. **Hypo:** Congress conditions an HIV-prevention grant on the recipient organization's officially adopting a policy supporting abstinence-only education in all of its programs (federally-funded and otherwise). **Why:** Under AID v AOSI the policy requirement reaches beyond the funded program and forces the recipient to adopt the government's viewpoint as its own outside-the-program identity. The condition is unconstitutional.

### Fence-Sitter

5. **Hypo:** Congress conditions federal sanctuary-city-related law-enforcement grants on state and local jurisdictions agreeing to share specified categories of immigration-status information with federal authorities, with a 25% withholding for noncompliance. **Why:** The percentage is large enough to raise NFIB-style coercion concerns but smaller than NFIB's all-or-nothing Medicaid threat; the germaneness analysis depends on whether immigration-status sharing is treated as related to law-enforcement grant purposes (Rehnquist's loose germaneness reading) or as falling outside the grant's specifying-how-funds-are-spent function (O'Connor's stricter Dole dissent). Lower courts have split on similar fact patterns. The hypo turns on which germaneness reading and which coercion threshold the court adopts; the case is genuinely contested.

## How to Spot on an Exam

A spending-power question carries one of two structural signatures: a federal grant of money to a state or private recipient with strings attached (the conditional-spending pattern), or a federal restriction on what the recipient may do or say with or alongside the funded activity (the unconstitutional-conditions pattern). Spot the structure first, then run the right framework.

For state-recipient conditional spending:

1. Apply the four-part Dole test in order: general welfare (deference); unambiguous condition; germane; no independent bar.
2. After Dole's four prongs, ask whether the financial pressure crosses NFIB's coercion line. Look for: very large dollar stakes; threat to existing-and-separable funding for refusing a new expansion; conditions that leave states no realistic choice. Quote Roberts's gun to the head and economic dragooning language.
3. If the condition fails, identify whether the failure is at germaneness (O'Connor-style narrow reading) or at coercion (NFIB-style severance remedy).

For private-recipient unconstitutional-conditions:

1. Identify whether the condition defines the program (Rust) or distorts the recipient's protected outside-the-program activity (Velazquez, AID v AOSI).
2. Apply the policy-requirement limit (AID v AOSI): conditions that compel the recipient to adopt a particular viewpoint as its own are unconstitutional.
3. For speech-related distortions, run the First Amendment alongside the spending-power analysis; the unconstitutional-conditions doctrine sits at the intersection.

For Tenth Amendment / anti-commandeering overlap, note that conditional spending is the structural workaround that allows Congress to influence state policy in commandeering-forbidden areas. The workaround survives Dole easily, NFIB at the coercion margin, and would fail if the condition itself violates an enumerated-powers limit on Congress's regulatory authority.

## Critique

**The four-part test as paper tiger.** Lynn Baker, Mitchell Berman, and federalism-tradition scholars argue that Dole's four prongs are essentially non-justiciable. General welfare gets total deference; unambiguous is a drafting fix; independent bar is a null set if even the Twenty-first Amendment cannot qualify. That leaves germaneness as the only operative limit, and Rehnquist applied it so loosely (any plausible nexus suffices) that Congress can purchase state regulatory compliance on any subject by pricing the withholding below the coercion threshold. NFIB tightened the coercion margin but did not address the structural critique that Dole permits an end-run around enumerated-powers limits.

**NFIB's coercion line as judicially unworkable.** Roberts's gun-to-the-head test in NFIB has no specifiable dollar threshold. Lower courts have struggled to apply it consistently to ACA-era conditional grants and to Trump-era immigration-enforcement grants. Critics argue that drawing the coercion line case-by-case produces unpredictability that incentivizes Congress to test new conditional schemes rather than respect a stable doctrinal limit. Defenders respond that a totality-of-circumstances test is the only plausible approach when financial pressure interacts with state budget structure differently in different programs.

**The Hamiltonian reading as historically contested.** Justice Thomas (in his Cutter v Wilkinson and NFIB concurrences) and a growing originalist literature argue that the Madisonian reading (general welfare limited to the other enumerated subjects) is the more defensible historical reading. The Court rejected this view in Butler (1936) but the rejection was dictum and the historical case for Madison has not weakened. Whether a future Court could reanimate the Madisonian reading without overturning Butler is a serious question that current spending-power doctrine quietly leaves open.

**Unconstitutional-conditions line drawing.** The Rust / Velazquez / AID v AOSI line is more rule-of-thumb than principled rule. Critics from the left argue that Rust permits viewpoint discrimination through funding choices that would be unconstitutional if imposed directly. Critics from the right argue that Velazquez and AID v AOSI improperly limit Congress's authority to define federal programs narrowly. Both sets of critics are partially right because the line itself rests on a contestable normative judgment about how much speech autonomy private recipients retain when they accept federal funds.

**The presidential-impoundment frontier.** The conditional-spending doctrine is built around congressional power to attach conditions to spending. The complementary question of whether the President may refuse to spend appropriated funds is governed by statute (the Impoundment Control Act of 1974) and by contested Article II claims. The Spending Clause is silent on impoundment, and modern administrations have tested the boundaries of Article II spending discretion. The lecture coverage in Impoundment and Presidential Spending Power develops this dimension; the case law is contested and unsettled.
