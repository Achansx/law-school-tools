# Substantive Due Process - Privacy and Autonomy

## Overview

Substantive due process for privacy and autonomy is the doctrinal arc in which the Fourteenth Amendment Due Process Clause has been used, since the early twentieth century, to protect a set of unenumerated personal-liberty interests against state regulation. The arc has a foundational layer in the Meyer / Pierce parental-authority cases of the 1920s, a survival layer in Skinner v Oklahoma (1942) and the Buck v Bell (1927) eugenics counter-precedent that Skinner partly limits, a privacy-launching moment in Griswold v Connecticut (1965), an extension to marriage and reproduction in Loving v Virginia (1967) and Roe v Wade (1973), a reaffirmation-with-modification in Planned Parenthood v Casey (1992), a methodology-wars period in Michael H v Gerald D (1989), Bowers v Hardwick (1986), Lawrence v Texas (2003), Washington v Glucksberg (1997), and Obergefell v Hodges (2015), and a doctrinal earthquake in Dobbs v Jackson Women's Health Organization (2022).

This Topic is one of three sister Topics covering substantive due process. [[Topics/Substantive Due Process|Substantive Due Process]] (the parent / incorporation Topic) treats the Bill-of-Rights-against-the-States lineage. [[Topics/Substantive Due Process - Economic|Substantive Due Process - Economic]] treats the Lochner / Carolene / Williamson / BMW v Gore lineage. The privacy-and-autonomy arc differs from the other two on three structural dimensions. First, it is the most controversial of the three: the Bill-of-Rights line is near-unanimous, the economic line is dead letter except in BMW, but the privacy line generated five-to-four splits at every doctrinal turn. Second, it is the live-methodology arc: the Glucksberg careful-description test versus the Kennedy emerging-awareness approach is the principal methodological fault line, and Dobbs explicitly reaffirmed Glucksberg as the controlling SDP methodology. Third, it is the most volatile of the three after Dobbs (2022) — Thomas's Dobbs concurrence flagged Griswold, Lawrence, and Obergefell as candidates for reconsideration, and the Court's post-Dobbs trajectory remains uncertain.

---

## Governing Rule

> **Glucksberg controlling test (post-Dobbs default):** A claimed substantive due process right is fundamental only if (a) the asserted liberty interest is "carefully described" at the most specific traditional level, AND (b) the right so described is both "deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition" AND "implicit in the concept of ordered liberty." Washington v Glucksberg (1997). If the right is fundamental, strict scrutiny applies. If it is not fundamental, rational-basis review applies and the regulation virtually always survives. Dobbs v Jackson Women's Health Organization (2022) reaffirmed this as the controlling SDP methodology, expressly rejecting the higher level of generality at which Roe and Casey had framed the abortion right.

> **Kennedy emerging-awareness alternative (formally available within its holdings):** A claimed substantive due process right may be recognized at a higher level of generality (the "right to marry" rather than "the right to same-sex marriage") when the historically denied group was excluded from the tradition-forming process; tradition's arc matters even when specific practices were historically denied. Obergefell v Hodges (2015); Lawrence v Texas (2003). Dobbs disclaimed any direct attack on Obergefell or Lawrence but methodologically narrowed the space in which Kennedy's approach can operate.

> **Pre-Glucksberg foundational rule:** The Fourteenth Amendment's "liberty" includes, "without doubt, not merely freedom from bodily restraint but also the right of the individual to contract, to engage in any of the common occupations of life, to acquire useful knowledge, to marry, establish a home and bring up children, to worship God according to the dictates of his own conscience, and generally to enjoy those privileges long recognized at common law as essential to the orderly pursuit of happiness by free men." Meyer v Nebraska (1923).

## Doctrinal Development

The arc moves in five stations: foundational liberties, privacy launch, reaffirmation-with-modification, methodology wars, and earthquake.

### Foundational liberties: Meyer, Pierce, Buck, Skinner (1923-1942)

Meyer v Nebraska (1923) supplied the foundational catalog of unenumerated liberties: the right to acquire useful knowledge, to marry, establish a home, bring up children, worship, and engage in common occupations. The case struck down a Nebraska statute prohibiting instruction in foreign languages below the eighth grade, on the ground that it interfered with parental authority over education. Pierce v Society of Sisters (1925) extended Meyer two years later, striking down Oregon's compulsory-public-school statute as a violation of parental authority to direct the education of children. Together, Meyer and Pierce supply the parental-authority root of the autonomy arc.

Buck v Bell (1927) and Skinner v Oklahoma (1942) sit awkwardly in the same period. Buck v Bell upheld Virginia's compulsory-sterilization law for "feeble-minded" women under a brief Holmes opinion that ended with "three generations of imbeciles are enough" — a ruling never formally overruled and standing as the most-cited example of substantive due process gone wrong. Skinner v Oklahoma struck down Oklahoma's habitual-criminal sterilization law, treating procreation as "one of the basic civil rights of man" and applying strict-scrutiny equal-protection review to Oklahoma's distinction between larceny (sterilizable) and embezzlement (not). Buck and Skinner together establish that substantive due process can protect bodily integrity and procreation, but Buck remains a canonical embarrassment that the modern Court neither overrules nor relies on.

### Privacy launch: Griswold (1965)

Griswold v Connecticut (1965) struck down Connecticut's anti-contraception statute as applied to married couples, on the ground that the marital relationship occupies a "zone of privacy" formed by penumbras emanating from the First, Third, Fourth, Fifth, and Ninth Amendments. Douglas's majority refused to anchor the right in the Due Process Clause directly to avoid Lochner-era associations, but Harlan's concurrence took the explicit Due Process route, framing the inquiry as whether the right is implicit in the concept of ordered liberty. Harlan's concurrence becomes the doctrinal source for what Glucksberg later canonizes; Douglas's penumbras-and-emanations idiom became famous but was rapidly abandoned in subsequent cases.

### Reaffirmation-with-modification: Loving (1967), Eisenstadt (1972), Roe (1973), Casey (1992)

Loving v Virginia (1967) struck down Virginia's anti-miscegenation statute on dual grounds: the Equal Protection Clause (racial classifications demand the most rigid scrutiny) and the Due Process Clause (marriage is a fundamental right). Warren's opinion did not theorize the level of generality at which "marriage" was being defined, but the dual-holding structure becomes load-bearing for subsequent cases that pick and choose between the two grounds.

Roe v Wade (1973) extended the privacy line to abortion, striking down Texas's near-total abortion ban under a trimester framework: in the first trimester the right is virtually absolute; in the second trimester the state may regulate to protect maternal health; at viability (third trimester) the state's interest in potential life becomes compelling. Roe was the high-water mark of the reproductive-autonomy line.

Planned Parenthood v Casey (1992) reaffirmed Roe's "essential holding" while replacing the trimester framework with the "undue burden" standard. Casey's joint opinion (O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter) treated stare decisis itself as a substantive value: even if Roe was wrongly decided, settled reliance and the Court's institutional legitimacy counsel against overruling. The Casey reformulation governed for thirty years until Dobbs.

### Methodology wars: Bowers, Michael H, Lawrence, Glucksberg, Obergefell (1986-2015)

Bowers v Hardwick (1986) upheld Georgia's anti-sodomy statute by carefully describing the right as "a fundamental right to homosexual sodomy" and finding no historical pedigree at that level. White's majority became the canonical example of how careful description can predetermine the outcome of an SDP inquiry.

Michael H v Gerald D (1989) is the methodological centerpiece. Scalia's plurality articulated the careful-description rule in Footnote 6: courts should look to the "most specific level at which a relevant tradition protecting, or denying protection to, the asserted right can be identified." Brennan's dissent argued for higher-generality framing. Michael H is the case where the methodological fight became explicit, and Glucksberg later picks Scalia's side.

Washington v Glucksberg (1997) canonized the careful-description test and rejected a fundamental right to physician-assisted suicide. Rehnquist's unanimous opinion articulated the two-part test (careful description + deeply rooted) that becomes the controlling SDP methodology. The opinion was joined by Kennedy and Souter, both of whom would later author or co-author opinions that operated in Kennedy's emerging-awareness register; the joinder is one of the case's interpretive puzzles.

Lawrence v Texas (2003) overruled Bowers, with Kennedy's majority refusing to describe the right at the Bowers level of specificity. Lawrence framed the inquiry as adult autonomy in intimate life, applying what later commentators call the emerging-awareness methodology. Scalia's dissent attacked the methodology as standardless and warned that Lawrence's logic would inexorably extend to same-sex marriage.

Obergefell v Hodges (2015) confirmed Scalia's prediction. Kennedy's majority extended Loving's fundamental-right-to-marry holding to same-sex couples by defining the right at a level of generality ("marriage") that excluded historical exclusion of same-sex partners. Kennedy's emerging-awareness move was explicit, and the four-Justice dissent (Roberts, Scalia, Thomas, Alito) attacked it as unprincipled.

### Earthquake: Dobbs (2022)

Dobbs v Jackson Women's Health Organization (2022) overruled Roe and Casey, holding that the Constitution confers no right to abortion and returning the issue to the political branches. Alito's majority applied the Glucksberg test verbatim: at a careful-description level (the right to abortion specifically, not the right to bodily autonomy generally), abortion has no deep historical pedigree, having been criminalized in most states at the time of the Fourteenth Amendment. Casey's stare-decisis defense was rejected because the underlying constitutional reasoning was thought to be wrong, the standard was unworkable, and reliance interests were deemed intangible.

Thomas's concurrence urged reconsideration of every other privacy-line case (Griswold, Lawrence, Obergefell), reasoning that they all rest on the same SDP methodology Dobbs has just discredited. The majority disclaimed any threat to those cases. Whether Thomas's view will eventually carry the day is the single largest open doctrinal question in the privacy-and-autonomy arc.

## Comparison: Glucksberg Two-Part Test vs Kennedy Emerging-Awareness Methodology

| Axis | Glucksberg Two-Part Test (post-Dobbs default) | Kennedy Emerging-Awareness (Lawrence, Obergefell) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Step one: framing the right | Careful description at the most specific traditional level (e.g., "right to physician-assisted suicide," not "right to bodily autonomy") | Right defined at a higher level of generality capturing the broader liberty interest (e.g., "right to marry," not "right to same-sex marriage") |
| Step two: historical inquiry | Deeply rooted in Nation's history and tradition AND implicit in the concept of ordered liberty; pedigree at the specific level claimed is required | Historical practice plus evolving understanding; tradition's arc matters even where specific practice was historically denied to a discrete group |
| Effect on novel claims | Presumptively defeats claims for rights without specific historical pedigree at the carefully described level | More receptive to rights denied to identifiable groups, on the theory that excluded groups did not participate in tradition-forming |
| Post-Dobbs status | Reaffirmed as the controlling SDP methodology (Dobbs) | Formally available within Lawrence and Obergefell holdings; methodologically narrowed; no longer extends to new claims |
| Critical case | Washington v Glucksberg (1997); Dobbs (2022) | Lawrence v Texas (2003); Obergefell v Hodges (2015) |
| Key dissents to the methodology | Kennedy and Souter in Glucksberg argue careful description pregames the outcome; Stevens argues history of denial is evidence against, not for, traditional protection | Roberts, Scalia, Thomas in Obergefell call Kennedy's emerging-awareness move "unprincipled" because it lets the Court reach any result by choosing the level of generality |

## Comparison: Stare Decisis at the SDP Overruling Threshold

This table maps the two modern SDP overrulings against the Court's stare decisis framework, tracking how each majority justified departing from prior constitutional precedent.

| Axis | Lawrence overruling Bowers (1986) | Dobbs overruling Roe (1973) and Casey (1992) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Years overruled precedent stood | 17 years (Bowers 1986 to Lawrence 2003) | Roe 49 years; Casey 30 years |
| Nature of claimed error | Bowers "not correct when it was decided"; Kennedy found Bowers's historical claim about anti-sodomy law specifically targeting homosexual conduct was inaccurate | Roe invented a right with "no textual, historical, or traditional foundation"; Casey compounded the error by retaining Roe's holding while discarding its reasoning |
| Quality-of-reasoning critique | Kennedy: Bowers's narrow restatement of the liberty question pregame-determined the outcome | Alito: Roe's trimester framework was invented without constitutional basis; Casey's "undue burden" standard is inherently standardless |
| Reliance interests | Kennedy dismissed reliance: anti-sodomy convictions generated only criminal records, not concrete economic reliance | Alito: women's reliance is "intangible" and speculative; Breyer / Sotomayor / Kagan dissent disputes this sharply |
| State-level trend | Cited modern decriminalization (24 states) and European human-rights law as evidence of consensus | Inverted: continued state regulation of abortion shows no settled consensus in favor; absence of "deeply rooted" tradition itself dispositive |
| Stare decisis factors applied | Cited Casey framework but did not systematically apply five-factor test | Applied five Casey-derived factors (error, reasoning, workability, doctrinal effect, reliance) and found all five counsel overruling |
| Scope of overruling | Limited to consensual private adult intimacy context; did not formally unsettle morals-based legislation more broadly | Limited to abortion on potential-life ground; Thomas concurrence urged reconsideration of Griswold, Lawrence, Obergefell as well |

## Comparison: Loving's Dual-Holding Deployment Across Downstream Cases

Loving (1967) carried two independently sufficient grounds (Equal Protection and Due Process). Subsequent cases pick and choose, often deploying one but not the other.

| Axis | Loving (1967) | Obergefell (2015) | Dobbs (2022) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| EP deployment | Core holding: racial classifications demand the "most rigid scrutiny"; "equal application" no defense | Present but secondary; Kennedy treats EP and DP as interwoven; EP analysis comparatively thin | Acknowledges Loving's EP branch as the reason Loving is distinguishable from Roe (whose single-ground SDP holding lacked Loving's racial-classification anchor) |
| DP deployment | Core holding: marriage is a fundamental right; Meyer / Skinner are the authority chain | Primary ground: Kennedy extends Loving's fundamental-right-to-marry holding via emerging-awareness | Distinguished and partially narrowed: Alito reads Loving's DP branch as inseparable from its EP branch, avoiding a Loving-grounded affirmative rule for non-racial SDP claims |
| Level-of-generality move | Implicit, not defended: Warren treats the right as "marriage" without theorizing why | Explicit: Kennedy makes the move and defends it; "marriage" rather than "same-sex marriage" | Repudiated: Alito explicitly reaffirms Glucksberg's careful-description rule |
| Loving-paradox status | Originating tension: history-and-tradition test would defeat Loving on anti-miscegenation precedent dating to 1691 Virginia | Resolved in Loving's favor: equality filter made explicit | Reopened: Alito reaffirms Glucksberg without addressing how anti-miscegenation traditions fit |

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side

**1. State criminalizes possession of contraceptives by married couples.** A state statute, drafted to mirror Connecticut's pre-Griswold law, makes it a misdemeanor for any person, married or unmarried, to use contraceptives. A married couple is convicted and challenges the statute. Under Griswold, the marital-privacy holding is directly on point: the right to use contraceptives within the marital relationship is implicit in the concept of ordered liberty. The state loses; even under the post-Dobbs Glucksberg test, Griswold itself remains good law because the Dobbs majority disclaimed any direct attack on it. (Thomas's concurrence is a separate question; the majority is what governs.)

**2. State sterilizes prison inmates as a condition of parole.** A state offers prison inmates accelerated parole in exchange for voluntary sterilization. An inmate sues, arguing the conditional-sterilization scheme violates substantive due process. Skinner v Oklahoma (1942) treated procreation as "one of the basic civil rights of man" and applied heightened review. Even if rational basis controlled the inquiry today, the "voluntary" framing under conditions of confinement raises Cruzan-style concerns about the integrity of consent for irreversible medical interventions. The inmate likely wins on SDP and on EP grounds.

### Opposite-Side

**3. State requires three-day waiting period for abortion.** A state imposes a three-day waiting period for abortion services, with mandatory counseling about fetal pain. A clinic challenges the law. Under Casey's undue-burden standard the law would have survived (waiting periods were generally upheld pre-Dobbs). After Dobbs, the question is whether the law has any rational basis. It plainly does — reflective decision-making in matters of medical consequence — and rational basis is forgiving. The clinic loses on the SDP claim. (A federal preemption claim under EMTALA in true medical-emergency cases is a different doctrine.)

**4. State criminalizes assisted suicide.** A terminally ill patient sues to enjoin a state's prohibition on physician-assisted suicide. Glucksberg (1997) is on all fours: at a careful-description level, there is no fundamental right to physician-assisted suicide, and the state's interests in preserving life, preventing abuse of the vulnerable, and protecting medical-professional integrity easily satisfy rational basis. Patient loses.

### Fence-Sitter

**5. State prohibits gestational surrogacy.** A state criminalizes gestational-surrogacy contracts on the ground that they commodify reproduction. A would-be surrogate mother and intended parents challenge the statute. The honest answer is that this is hard. Under Glucksberg, the right (carefully described) is the right to enter a paid gestational-surrogacy contract, and that has no deep historical pedigree — gestational surrogacy is a 1980s technological innovation and has been variably regulated since. The plaintiffs would invoke Meyer-Pierce-Loving-Obergefell as anchors for a broader family-formation right, but the post-Dobbs Court would resist higher-generality framing. Plaintiffs probably lose on SDP, but a strong argument exists that surrogacy implicates Skinner-Cruzan bodily-autonomy concerns (the surrogate's right to medical decision-making) plus parental-authority concerns under Troxel for the intended parents. The exam answer is the recognition that this is exactly where the Glucksberg-Kennedy methodology dispute becomes load-bearing.

## How to Spot on an Exam

Watch for four triggers. First, anything implicating contraception, abortion, marriage, intimate sexual conduct, end-of-life decisions, or parenting rights. Identify which precedent supplies the closest doctrinal anchor: Griswold for contraception, Roe / Casey / Dobbs for abortion, Loving / Obergefell for marriage, Lawrence for intimate adult sexual conduct, Glucksberg / Cruzan for end-of-life, Meyer / Pierce / Troxel for parenting.

Second, the methodology choice. Ask whether the prompt invites Glucksberg-style careful description or Kennedy-style emerging-awareness framing. The post-Dobbs default is Glucksberg, but a court still has interpretive discretion in framing the right; spot the level-of-generality move when it appears.

Third, the stare-decisis layer. If the prompt involves a case that overrules or is positioned to overrule a prior SDP precedent, run the five-factor Casey / Dobbs framework: nature of error, quality of reasoning, workability of the test, disruptive doctrinal effect, and concrete reliance. The five factors do real work in modern overruling debates.

Fourth, the Loving paradox. If the prompt implicates a right that has historically been denied to a discrete group (interracial couples, same-sex couples, women), flag that the Glucksberg careful-description test cuts against the right and that Kennedy's emerging-awareness move was the workaround. Note that Dobbs reaffirmed Glucksberg without resolving the Loving paradox, and that Loving's dual-holding (EP + DP) structure is what insulates it from Dobbs-style narrowing.

The phrase to bury in the answer is "level of generality." That is the load-bearing methodological move in every privacy-line case after Michael H, and identifying it is worth real points.

## Critique

The textual objection is the same one that applies to the entire SDP arc: Justice Thomas's claim that "substantive due process is a contradiction in terms." If the framers wanted to protect substantive rights, they had a clause for that — Privileges or Immunities — and the post-Slaughter-House routing through the Due Process Clause is the result of doctrinal accident, not constitutional design. Thomas's Dobbs concurrence makes this point most aggressively, urging reconsideration of Griswold, Lawrence, and Obergefell on the premise that all three rest on the same textually unmoored methodology Dobbs has discredited.

The level-of-generality objection is more specific. The Glucksberg careful-description test and the Kennedy emerging-awareness approach reach opposite results on the same fact patterns, and the choice between them is itself substantive. Critics from the right (Scalia in Obergefell) argue that emerging-awareness methodology is unprincipled because it lets the Court reach any result by choosing the level of generality. Critics from the left (Stevens, Sotomayor) argue that careful description is itself unprincipled because it lets the Court defeat any unenumerated-liberty claim by framing it narrowly.

The Loving-paradox objection is the structural deep cut. Anti-miscegenation laws were "deeply rooted" at the time of the Fourteenth Amendment in a literal historical sense — they go back to 1691 Virginia and were on the books in most states well into the 1960s. A faithful application of the Glucksberg test should have defeated Loving's DP holding. Loving's actual reasoning works only if "history and tradition" is filtered through a separate equality principle, which is exactly what Kennedy's emerging-awareness approach makes explicit and Dobbs declines to engage. The unresolved Loving paradox is the doctrinal bomb planted at the heart of the post-Dobbs SDP framework.

The pragmatic critique runs in two directions. From the right, the Lochner-Roe parallel: the Court should not be in the business of enforcing unenumerated substantive liberties against state legislative judgment, regardless of whether the unenumerated liberty is freedom of contract (Lochner) or reproductive autonomy (Roe). From the left, the Casey-Dobbs reliance critique: when courts cultivate constitutional precedent for half a century, ordinary people structure their lives around it; an overruling-on-the-merits methodology that discounts that reliance treats stare decisis as merely formal rather than substantive.

- [[Topics/Originalism|Originalism]] — the methodological cousin; Glucksberg's deeply-rooted-in-history-and-tradition prong is originalism's natural home in SDP.
- [[Topics/Text History and Tradition|Text History and Tradition]] — the methodology explicitly used by Dobbs and Bruen; the post-Dobbs convergence question.
- [[Cases/Meyer v Nebraska (1923)|Meyer v Nebraska (1923)]] — the foundational catalog of unenumerated liberties.
- [[Cases/Pierce v Society of Sisters (1925)|Pierce v Society of Sisters (1925)]] — Meyer's parental-authority companion.
- [[Cases/Buck v Bell (1927)|Buck v Bell (1927)]] — the canonical SDP embarrassment; never formally overruled.
- [[Cases/Griswold v Connecticut (1965)|Griswold v Connecticut (1965)]] — the privacy launch; Douglas's penumbras and Harlan's DP concurrence.
- [[Cases/Loving v Virginia (1967)|Loving v Virginia (1967)]] — the dual-holding (EP + DP) anchor for marriage rights.
- [[Cases/Roe v Wade (1973)|Roe v Wade (1973)]] — the trimester framework; overruled by Dobbs.
- [[Cases/Moore v City of East Cleveland (1977)|Moore v City of East Cleveland (1977)]] — the family-relationships extension of Meyer-Pierce.
- [[Cases/Bowers v Hardwick (1986)|Bowers v Hardwick (1986)]] — the careful-description low point; overruled by Lawrence.
- [[Cases/Michael H v Gerald D (1989)|Michael H v Gerald D (1989)]] — Scalia's Footnote 6 articulates the careful-description rule that Glucksberg later canonizes.
- [[Cases/Cruzan v Director Missouri Department of Health (1990)|Cruzan v Director Missouri Department of Health (1990)]] — bodily autonomy and the right to refuse medical treatment.
- [[Cases/Planned Parenthood v Casey (1992)|Planned Parenthood v Casey (1992)]] — Roe's "essential holding" reaffirmed and modified; replaced by Dobbs.
- [[Cases/Washington v Glucksberg (1997)|Washington v Glucksberg (1997)]] — the canonical articulation of the two-part SDP test.
- [[Cases/Troxel v Granville (2000)|Troxel v Granville (2000)]] — Meyer-Pierce parental-authority extended to third-party visitation.
- [[Cases/Lawrence v Texas (2003)|Lawrence v Texas (2003)]] — Bowers overruled; Kennedy's emerging-awareness methodology.
- [[Cases/Obergefell v Hodges (2015)|Obergefell v Hodges (2015)]] — the apex of the emerging-awareness arc; same-sex marriage extends Loving's DP branch.
- [[Cases/Dobbs v Jackson Women's Health Organization (2022)|Dobbs v Jackson Women's Health Organization (2022)]] — Roe / Casey overruled; Glucksberg reaffirmed; Thomas concurrence flags the rest of the privacy line.
