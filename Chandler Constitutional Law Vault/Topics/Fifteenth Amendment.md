# Fifteenth Amendment

## Overview

The Fifteenth Amendment is the third of the Reconstruction Amendments, ratified in 1870 to prohibit race-based denials and abridgements of the franchise. Together with the Thirteenth and Fourteenth Amendments, it forms what the Reconstruction lecture calls the "Second Constitution": a textual repudiation of Dred Scott v Sandford (1857) and a structural reallocation of authority over civil rights from the states to Congress and the federal courts. Section 1 of the Amendment prohibits state denial or abridgement of the right to vote on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude. Section 2 grants Congress the power to enforce the Amendment by appropriate legislation.

Doctrinally, the Fifteenth Amendment occupies a distinctive place in the enforcement-power canon. Like the Fourteenth Amendment, it postdates the Eleventh Amendment and abrogates state sovereign immunity in its own field of operation (Fitzpatrick v Bitzer (1976) confirms the structural point for the Reconstruction Amendments generally). Unlike the Fourteenth Amendment, its Section 1 prohibition is narrower, reaching only race-based franchise restrictions, but its Section 2 enforcement power has historically been read more deferentially. Where City of Boerne v Flores (1997) imposed a congruence-and-proportionality limit on Fourteenth Amendment Section 5 legislation, City of Rome v United States (1980) and Katzenbach v Morgan (1966) read Fifteenth Amendment Section 2 to allow prophylactic federal legislation reaching electoral changes with discriminatory effect, even where the underlying conduct does not itself violate Section 1.

The Amendment's history is one of judicial narrowing in the late nineteenth century followed by legislative and judicial revival in the mid-twentieth. Slaughter-House Cases (1873), Civil Rights Cases (1883), and United States v Cruikshank (1876) collectively read the Reconstruction Amendments narrowly enough that federal protection of the Black franchise effectively collapsed for nearly a century. The Voting Rights Act of 1965 and decisions including Katzenbach v Morgan (1966) and City of Rome v United States (1980) restored Section 2 of the Fifteenth Amendment as a working source of congressional authority; Shelby County v Holder (2013) pulled back from that high-water mark by invalidating the coverage formula of the preclearance regime.

## Governing Rule

> **Section 1 (verbatim, U.S. Const. amend. XV, sec. 1):** "The right of citizens of the United States to vote shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or by any State on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude."

> **Section 2 (verbatim, U.S. Const. amend. XV, sec. 2):** "The Congress shall have power to enforce this article by appropriate legislation."

> **Rule (paraphrased):** Section 1 prohibits, of its own force, intentional state denial or abridgement of the franchise on account of race. Section 2 empowers Congress to enforce the Amendment by appropriate legislation, measured by the McCulloch v Maryland standard: any means rationally adapted to the Amendment's anti-discrimination purpose, including prophylactic measures that reach state electoral changes with a discriminatory effect even where no purposeful discrimination is shown.

> **Marshall in City of Rome v United States (verbatim, Midpage-verified):** "[P]rinciples of federalism that might otherwise be an obstacle to congressional authority are necessarily overridden by the power to enforce the Civil War Amendments 'by appropriate legislation.' Those Amendments were specifically designed as an expansion of federal power and an intrusion on state sovereignty." ## Doctrinal Development

The doctrinal arc moves from pre-Reconstruction repudiation, through nineteenth-century judicial narrowing, to twentieth-century legislative and judicial revival, and finally to Rehnquist Court calibration. Each case in the arc sits in the vault as a full brief; this section summarizes their place in the arc and links to the briefs.

### Dred Scott v Sandford (1857): the case the Fifteenth Amendment overruled

Chief Justice Taney's majority held that Black persons of African descent, free or enslaved, were not and could never be citizens of the United States within the meaning of Article III diversity jurisdiction, and that the Missouri Compromise's prohibition on slavery in federal territory exceeded Congress's power. The Reconstruction lecture treats the Thirteenth, Fourteenth, and Fifteenth Amendments together as the textual repudiation of Dred Scott: the Thirteenth abolished slavery, the Fourteenth created national citizenship and equal protection, and the Fifteenth prohibited race-based franchise restrictions. Reading the Fifteenth Amendment without the Dred Scott context loses the constitutional purpose, the Amendment was a textual response to a Court that had read Black citizens out of the constitutional community. See [[Cases/Dred Scott v Sandford (1857)|full brief]].

### Slaughter-House Cases (1873): the early narrow reading

Justice Miller's majority read the Fourteenth Amendment's Privileges or Immunities Clause to protect only the narrow set of privileges of national (as opposed to state) citizenship. The opinion did not directly construe the Fifteenth Amendment, but its narrow reading of the Reconstruction trilogy set the interpretive default for the next several decades. The Fifteenth Amendment as a working source of federal authority over the franchise effectively went dormant for a generation under this interpretive frame, the Court read the Reconstruction Amendments as narrow correctives rather than as a structural reallocation of civil-rights authority. See [[Cases/Slaughter-House Cases (1873)|full brief]].

### Civil Rights Cases (1883): narrowing the enforcement project

Justice Bradley's majority invalidated the public-accommodations sections of the Civil Rights Act of 1875, holding that the Fourteenth Amendment's enforcement clause reaches only state action and not private conduct. The decision did not by its terms reach the Fifteenth Amendment, but the Civil Rights Cases lecture and the Reconstruction lecture both treat it as the shape that constrained Fifteenth Amendment enforcement as well: by the early twentieth century, federal prosecutions of private interference with Black voting (the original target of the 1870 Enforcement Acts) had largely collapsed, and what remained was a narrow Section 1 ban on overt state discrimination. The Civil Rights Cases lecture frames this as the "narrowing of Section 5 enforcement authority" that, alongside the Slaughter-House reading, shaped the Reconstruction Amendments' diminished scope until the Second Reconstruction. See [[Cases/Civil Rights Cases (1883)|full brief]].

### Katzenbach v Morgan (1966): McCulloch revives the enforcement clauses

Justice Brennan's majority upheld Section 4(e) of the Voting Rights Act of 1965, which suspended New York's English literacy requirement for voters who had completed sixth grade in an American-flag school where instruction was in a language other than English. The case formally rests on Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment, but its reasoning revives the parallel enforcement clause of the Fifteenth Amendment as well. Brennan adopted the McCulloch v Maryland standard as the measure of "appropriate legislation": Congress may enact any means appropriate and plainly adapted to enforcing the Amendment's guarantees, so long as the means are not prohibited by the Constitution. The opinion does not require a prior judicial finding that the state law was unconstitutional; it is enough that the Court can perceive a rational basis for Congress's judgment. City of Rome later reads Katzenbach as the operative frame for Section 2 of the Fifteenth Amendment as well. See [[Cases/Katzenbach v Morgan (1966)|full brief]].

### City of Rome v United States (1980): Section 2 reaches discriminatory effect

Justice Marshall's majority squarely interpreted Section 2 of the Fifteenth Amendment. Marshall held that Congress had authority under Section 2 to prohibit state electoral changes with a discriminatory effect, even where no discriminatory purpose was shown, and that the Voting Rights Act's preclearance requirement was a constitutional exercise of that authority. The reasoning has three pieces. First, the Voting Rights Act's "purpose and effect" language is conjunctive: a covered jurisdiction must show both purpose and effect are non-discriminatory to obtain preclearance. Second, Congress's Section 2 enforcement power, like its Section 5 power under Katzenbach, follows the McCulloch frame and reaches prophylactic measures that go beyond Section 1's own intentional-discrimination floor. Third, the federalism objection to overriding state authority over voter qualifications is overridden by the Civil War Amendments' textual reallocation of authority to Congress. City of Rome is the canonical Fifteenth Amendment Section 2 enforcement case in the vault. See [[Cases/City of Rome v United States (1980)|full brief]].

### City of Boerne v Flores (1997): the calibration that preserved the voting-rights core

Justice Kennedy's majority struck down the Religious Freedom Restoration Act as applied to the states, holding that Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment empowers Congress to enact remedial and preventive legislation but not to redefine the substantive scope of the Amendment's rights. The remedy must show congruence and proportionality between the documented pattern of state-actor constitutional injury and the means adopted. Boerne explicitly recast Katzenbach v Morgan as a remedial voting-rights precedent rather than a general license for substantive expansion: when Congress legislates on the strength of a documented record of state racial discrimination in voting, the McCulloch frame holds; when Congress legislates beyond that core, congruence and proportionality apply. The opinion does not address Section 2 of the Fifteenth Amendment by its terms, leaving open whether the more deferential Katzenbach-Rome frame survives intact for voting-rights enforcement on a Fifteenth Amendment grounding, or whether congruence and proportionality migrate over. See [[Cases/City of Boerne v Flores (1997)|full brief]].

## Comparison: Section 2 of the Fifteenth Amendment and Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment

The two enforcement clauses share text and the McCulloch frame, but doctrinal practice has diverged.

| Feature | Fifteenth Amendment Section 2 | Fourteenth Amendment Section 5 |
|---------|------|------|
| Operative test | McCulloch rational adaptation (Katzenbach v Morgan, City of Rome v United States) | Congruence and proportionality outside the voting-rights core (City of Boerne v Flores) |
| Reach beyond Section 1 | Reaches electoral changes with discriminatory effect; no purposeful-discrimination floor required for prophylactic legislation | Limited to remedial and preventive legislation responsive to a documented state-actor injury record |
| State sovereignty interaction | Federalism objections overridden; Civil War Amendments are designed as an intrusion on state sovereignty (City of Rome v United States) | Same in principle, but the documented-injury record constrains the volume of state conduct Congress can reach |
| Modern challenge | Shelby County v Holder (2013) invalidated the preclearance coverage formula; preclearance currently dormant | RFRA invalidated as applied to states (City of Boerne); ADA Title II as applied to courthouse access upheld (Lane); employment-discrimination provisions partially upheld depending on the documented record |

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side

1. **Hypo:** Congress enacts a federal statute requiring covered states to obtain preclearance from the Department of Justice before changing any precinct boundary or polling-place location, on a record showing recent purposeful discrimination in those covered states. **Why:** The statute is a prophylactic measure rationally adapted to the anti-discrimination purpose of the Fifteenth Amendment. Under Katzenbach v Morgan and City of Rome v United States, Congress may reach state electoral changes with a discriminatory effect on a documented record, and the federalism objection is overridden by the Civil War Amendments' textual reallocation of authority.

2. **Hypo:** Congress enacts a federal statute prohibiting any state from imposing voter-identification requirements that disproportionately exclude Black voters in counties with a documented history of racially polarized voting and discriminatory election administration. **Why:** This is the City of Rome pattern. Congress is reaching electoral changes with discriminatory effect on a documented record. The McCulloch rational-adaptation frame applies under Section 2.

### Opposite-Side

3. **Hypo:** Congress enacts a federal statute prohibiting all states from imposing English-language requirements on any voter, regardless of any documented record of racial discrimination, on the theory that such requirements always reflect implicit racial bias. **Why:** The statute likely fails. Without a documented state-actor record connecting English-language requirements to race-based franchise denial, the legislation looks substantive rather than remedial. Even on the more deferential McCulloch frame, the statute redefines what the Fifteenth Amendment prohibits rather than enforcing the existing prohibition. Compare City of Boerne v Flores (substantive expansion of rights is not Section 5 enforcement); the same logic likely cabins Section 2.

4. **Hypo:** Congress enacts a federal statute creating a private cause of action against any private employer who fires an employee for taking time off to vote, justified solely under Section 2 of the Fifteenth Amendment. **Why:** The statute likely fails. Section 1 of the Fifteenth Amendment reaches only state action (denial or abridgement by the United States or by any State). Section 2 enforcement of a Section 1 right is presumptively limited to state action as well, unlike the Thirteenth Amendment's badges-and-incidents power, which the Court has read to reach private conduct (Jones v Alfred H Mayer Co (1968)). The Reconstruction lecture identifies this as one of the structural distinctions between the Thirteenth and Fifteenth Amendments.

### Fence-Sitter

5. **Hypo:** Congress enacts a federal statute reviving Section 5 preclearance with a new coverage formula tied to current voter-registration disparities, racially polarized voting indicators, and recent voting-rights consent decrees, on a legislative record assembled in 2025. **Why:** This is the open question Shelby County v Holder (2013) left for Congress. Whether the new coverage formula satisfies the equal-sovereignty principle depends on how closely the formula tracks current conditions, and whether the Court reads City of Boerne's congruence-and-proportionality test as applying to Section 2 of the Fifteenth Amendment or whether the more deferential Katzenbach-Rome McCulloch frame survives. The statute is plausibly within Section 2 authority but stress-tests both the equal-sovereignty doctrine and the question of which enforcement-power frame applies.

## How to Spot on an Exam

Trigger facts include any federal statute regulating state electoral conduct, voter qualifications, or election administration; any prosecution or civil-rights claim grounded in race-based franchise interference; and any federalism objection to congressional regulation of voting. The analytical framework runs in two steps. First, ask whether the challenged conduct violates Section 1 of the Fifteenth Amendment of its own force: state action denying or abridging the franchise on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude. If yes, the Amendment itself supplies the rule of decision. Second, if Congress is acting under Section 2, apply the McCulloch frame from Katzenbach v Morgan and City of Rome v United States. Congress may legislate by any means rationally adapted to the Amendment's anti-discrimination purpose, and may reach state electoral changes with a discriminatory effect even absent proven purpose, so long as the legislation is connected to the Amendment's core.

Common pivot points to flag: the contrast with the Fourteenth Amendment's congruence-and-proportionality limit (City of Boerne); the abrogation of state sovereign immunity (City of Rome's federalism-yields-to-Civil-War-Amendments principle, paralleling Fitzpatrick v Bitzer); the Shelby County overlay on the preclearance regime; and the state-action limit on Section 1, which by extension cabins Section 2 in a way the Thirteenth Amendment does not cabin its own enforcement clause.

## Critique

The doctrinal asymmetry between Section 2 of the Fifteenth Amendment and Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment invites several lines of critique. The textualist objection asks why two enforcement clauses with materially identical language ("appropriate legislation") generate two different operative tests. One answer, drawn from City of Rome's reading of Katzenbach, is that the Fifteenth Amendment's narrower substantive scope (race-based franchise restrictions only) makes the prophylactic reach of Section 2 less prone to substantive overreach: a statute reaching electoral changes with a discriminatory effect in covered jurisdictions stays close to the Amendment's race-based core. The same prophylactic logic applied to the Fourteenth Amendment's broader substantive scope (due process, equal protection, privileges or immunities) would risk Boerne's substantive-expansion problem. A second critique, reflected in Stewart's City of Rome dissent, runs the other way: if Section 1 of the Fifteenth Amendment prohibits only purposeful discrimination, then Section 2 cannot authorize legislation reaching merely disproportionate effect, because Congress cannot define the scope of constitutional rights beyond what the Amendment independently prohibits. That argument prefigures the Boerne logic.

The temporal critique focuses on Shelby County v Holder (2013) and the equal-sovereignty principle. Chief Justice Roberts's majority did not formally extend Boerne's congruence-and-proportionality test to Section 2 of the Fifteenth Amendment, but the equal-sovereignty principle functions as a parallel constraint by requiring that current burdens be supported by current conditions. The result is that the most deferential enforcement-clause frame in the Court's modern doctrine (Katzenbach-Rome) now coexists with a stringent equal-sovereignty filter on the legislation it would otherwise authorize. Whether the McCulloch frame survives the next coverage formula or whether the Court will fold the equal-sovereignty principle into a Boerne-style proportionality test is the live doctrinal question.

The historical critique looks back to Reconstruction. The 1870 Enforcement Acts and the Civil Rights Act of 1875 represented an aggressive deployment of Section 2 authority that included reaching private conspirators interfering with the franchise. Slaughter-House and the Civil Rights Cases narrowed that project to a ghost of itself; Cruikshank effectively ended federal criminal enforcement against private racial violence at the polls. The modern Section 2 doctrine has not recovered the private-conduct reach the Reconstruction Congress assumed it had, even though Section 2's text matches Section 2 of the Thirteenth Amendment, which the Court has read to authorize legislation against private actors. The asymmetric treatment is a function of Section 1's state-action language, but the Reconstruction-era assumption was that Section 2 reached further than Section 1 for both Amendments alike.
