# Section 5 Enforcement Power

## Overview

Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment grants Congress power to enforce, by appropriate legislation, the substantive guarantees of Section 1 (due process, equal protection, and the privileges or immunities of national citizenship). Section 5 functions as the Fourteenth Amendment's analogue to the Necessary and Proper Clause: it equips Congress to give operational effect to the Amendment's limits on state conduct. The doctrine has oscillated between two poles. A broad reading treats Section 5 as a positive grant of legislative power measured by the McCulloch v Maryland standard, letting Congress define the scope of needed enforcement with deference. A narrower reading treats Section 5 as strictly remedial and preventive, barring Congress from enlarging the substance of constitutional rights beyond what the Court itself has recognized.

The modern framework synthesizes both. Congress may reach conduct that is not itself unconstitutional, but only when the prophylactic remedy is congruent and proportional to a documented pattern of actual or threatened constitutional violations by states. Section 5 also supplies the sole reliable path for Congress to abrogate state sovereign immunity: because the Fourteenth Amendment postdates the Eleventh and by its own terms restricts the states, Section 5 legislation can subject states to private federal-court suits for money damages, while Article I statutes cannot.

---

## Governing Rule

> **Rule (paraphrased):** Congress may enact legislation under Section 5 that enforces the Fourteenth Amendment's guarantees, including prophylactic measures that reach conduct not itself unconstitutional, provided the legislation reflects a congruence and proportionality between the constitutional injury to be prevented or remedied and the means adopted to that end. Section 5 authority is remedial and preventive, not substantive: Congress cannot use Section 5 to redefine the scope of the rights announced by the Court.

> **Kennedy in Boerne (verbatim):** "There must be a congruence and proportionality between the injury to be prevented or remedied and the means adopted to that end. Lacking such a connection, legislation may become substantive in operation and effect."

> **Brennan in Katzenbach v Morgan (verbatim):** "Correctly viewed, § 5 is a positive grant of legislative power authorizing Congress to exercise its discretion in determining whether and what legislation is needed to secure the guarantees of the Fourteenth Amendment."

## Doctrinal Development

### Civil Rights Cases (1883): The State-Action Floor

Bradley's majority reads Section 5 as an authority to correct state action only. Congress could not use Section 5 to reach private discrimination in inns, theaters, and public conveyances, because the Fourteenth Amendment itself did not forbid such private conduct. The Section 5 enforcement power had to be "adapted to the mischief and wrong which the amendment was intended to provide against." Professor Chandler flags the forward echo: Bradley's adaptation requirement anticipates the congruence-and-proportionality test adopted a century later in Boerne. The Civil Rights Cases also establish the enduring limit that Section 5 is coextensive with the substance of Section 1, not a freestanding police power. See [[Cases/Civil Rights Cases (1883)|full brief]] and [[Lectures/Civil Rights Cases - State Action and Enforcement Limits|Civil Rights Cases lecture]].

### Katzenbach v Morgan (1966): The Positive-Grant Reading

Brennan's majority upholds Section 4(e) of the Voting Rights Act, which suspended New York's English-language literacy requirement as applied to Puerto Rican voters educated in Spanish-medium schools. The Court treats Section 5 as a positive grant of legislative power, measured by the McCulloch v Maryland test: Congress may enact any means "appropriate" and "plainly adapted" to enforcing the Amendment's guarantees, so long as the means are not themselves constitutionally prohibited. Crucially, Congress does not need a prior judicial determination that the state law violates the Fourteenth Amendment; a rational basis for Congress's own constitutional judgment suffices. The opinion also articulates the "ratchet theory": Section 5 lets Congress expand protection of equal protection values beyond what the Court has required, but not dilute them. Harlan's dissent is the intellectual seed of Boerne, arguing that the majority's approach improperly lets Congress define the substance of constitutional rights. See [[Cases/Katzenbach v Morgan (1966)|full brief]].

### Fitzpatrick v Bitzer (1976): Section 5 Pierces the Eleventh Amendment

Rehnquist's majority holds that Congress's Section 5 power overrides the Eleventh Amendment, permitting private suits for retroactive money damages against states when Congress acts to enforce the Fourteenth Amendment. The structural argument is temporal and textual: the Fourteenth Amendment postdates the Eleventh and expressly directs limits at the states, and Section 5 expressly empowers Congress to enforce those limits. When the constitutional source of congressional authority is itself a restriction on state sovereignty, that sovereignty cannot block the enforcement. Title VII's 1972 extension to states, backed by back pay liability, is a valid Section 5 exercise. Fitzpatrick survives every later retrenchment: it remains the sole dependable path by which Congress can abrogate state sovereign immunity. See [[Cases/Fitzpatrick v Bitzer (1976)|full brief]] and [[Lectures/Eleventh Amendment - State Sovereign Immunity|Eleventh Amendment lecture]].

### City of Boerne v Flores (1997): The Congruence-and-Proportionality Limit

Kennedy's majority strikes down the Religious Freedom Restoration Act as applied to states. RFRA had sought to restore the Sherbert compelling-interest test for Free Exercise after Employment Division v Smith (1990), applying it to every governmental actor in every context. The Court holds that Section 5 authority is remedial and preventive, not substantive. Congress may not use Section 5 to create new rights or to expand rights beyond the Court's own interpretation of the Fourteenth Amendment, because that would displace the Court's Marbury role as expositor of the Constitution. The test for valid Section 5 legislation is congruence and proportionality between the documented constitutional injury and the legislative remedy. RFRA fails because the legislative record contained almost no evidence of state religious discrimination and RFRA's sweep across all generally applicable laws was grossly disproportionate. Boerne confines Katzenbach v Morgan to its remedial core and supplies the modern test that governs every subsequent Section 5 case. See [[Cases/City of Boerne v Flores (1997)|full brief]].

### Florida Prepaid (1999): A Thin Record Dooms Abrogation

Rehnquist applies Boerne to strike down the Patent Remedy Act's abrogation of state sovereign immunity for patent infringement. The threshold constitutional question is what Fourteenth Amendment violation states actually commit by infringing patents. Because states typically provide a takings remedy or other adequate process, infringement alone does not violate due process; only infringement coupled with denial of remedy does. Congress had almost no record of denied-remedy violations, yet abrogated nationwide for all patent suits. The remedy is so out of proportion to the constitutional injury that it cannot be understood as responsive to unconstitutional state behavior. Florida Prepaid transforms Boerne's test into a serious evidentiary demand: a legislative record is not a formality, and thin records cannot support sweeping abrogations. See [[Cases/Florida Prepaid Postsecondary Education Expense Board v College Savings Bank (1999)|full brief]].

### Board of Trustees v Garrett (2001): Rational-Basis Floor Shrinks the Remedial Space

Rehnquist applies Boerne to ADA Title I and holds that Congress cannot subject states to private damages actions for disability-based employment discrimination. The critical move is the scrutiny tier. Because disability classifications trigger only rational-basis review, the constitutional floor that Congress enforces is low, and the record must establish a pattern of genuinely unconstitutional state conduct (conduct that would fail rational basis). Most of the record described private conduct, local-government conduct, or conduct that rational basis would excuse. Title I's reasonable-accommodation mandate swept far beyond that constitutional floor. Garrett shows that Section 5 authority is tightly tied to the Court's equal-protection tiers: the lower the tier, the narrower the prophylactic space. See [[Cases/Board of Trustees of University of Alabama v Garrett (2001)|full brief]].

### Nevada v Hibbs (2003): Heightened Scrutiny Expands the Remedial Space

Rehnquist now writes for the liberal bloc to uphold FMLA's family-care leave provision as a valid Section 5 abrogation. The distinguishing variable is scrutiny. Sex classifications trigger intermediate scrutiny, which lifts the constitutional floor and broadens the prophylactic zone available to Congress. Congress had also assembled a substantial record documenting state leave policies that reinforced gender stereotypes about caregiving: women received paid maternity leave while men received nothing, entrenching stereotyped roles that equal protection forbids. The FMLA's uniform family-care leave requirement was congruent and proportional to that documented pattern. Hibbs clarifies that the Boerne-Florida Prepaid-Garrett line is not hostile to Section 5 legislation as such; it is hostile only where the remedy outruns the constitutional floor. See [[Cases/Nevada Department of Human Resources v Hibbs (2003)|full brief]].

### Tennessee v Lane (2004): Fundamental Rights and As-Applied Congruence

Stevens's majority upholds ADA Title II as applied to courthouse access. The Court sidesteps Title II's general validity by focusing on the specific subset of cases implicating the Due Process Clause's fundamental right of court access. Fundamental-rights enforcement, like heightened-scrutiny enforcement, supplies a higher constitutional floor that supports broader prophylactic authority. The legislative record documented systemic exclusion of disabled persons from courthouses, a pattern that threatened a fundamental right, and Title II's reasonable-modification requirement was congruent and proportional to that harm. Lane also introduces a methodological innovation: as-applied congruence analysis. A Section 5 statute may be valid in some applications and invalid in others, and courts can police the line without a facial strike-down. See [[Cases/Tennessee v Lane (2004)|full brief]].

### Allen v Cooper (2020): Florida Prepaid's Endurance

Kagan's unanimous Court applies Florida Prepaid to the Copyright Remedy Clarification Act and finds the same fatal flaw. The CRCA's scope mirrored the PRA's (nationwide abrogation without any inadequate-remedy trigger), and the legislative record was materially equivalent. Thomas and Breyer concur separately to suggest Florida Prepaid should be reconsidered in a future case with a richer record, but neither would abandon the framework. Allen v Cooper confirms that Florida Prepaid's evidentiary demands are durable: twenty years of additional state infringement did not generate the record Boerne requires. See [[Cases/Allen v Cooper (2020)|full brief]].

## Comparison: Scrutiny Tier as the Master Variable

Post-Boerne Section 5 outcomes track the constitutional floor Congress is trying to enforce. A lower floor means a narrower prophylactic zone and a heavier record burden; a higher floor means more breathing room for Congress.

| Case | Right Enforced | Scrutiny Tier | Record Strength | Outcome |
|------|----------------|---------------|-----------------|---------|
| Boerne | Free Exercise (Smith baseline) | Neutral-law standard (no heightened review) | Thin | Invalid |
| Florida Prepaid | Due process (property / patent) | Rational basis plus remedy analysis | Thin | Invalid |
| Garrett | Equal protection (disability, employment) | Rational basis | Thin on genuinely unconstitutional state conduct | Invalid |
| Hibbs | Equal protection (sex, family leave) | Intermediate | Substantial stereotype record | Valid |
| Lane | Due process (court access) | Fundamental right | Substantial exclusion record | Valid as applied |
| Allen v Cooper | Due process (copyright) | Rational basis plus remedy analysis | Thin | Invalid |

The pattern is the working exam framework: identify the constitutional right, fix the scrutiny tier, then ask whether the legislative record and statutory sweep fit that tier.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side

1. **Hypo:** Congress enacts a federal Voting Accessibility for the Disabled Act that requires states to provide accessible polling places, abrogating state sovereign immunity and authorizing private damages suits. The legislative record contains congressional findings of repeated documented exclusion of disabled voters from state polling sites. **Why:** Like Tennessee v Lane, the right enforced is fundamental (participation in the political process, arguably due process and equal protection), the record documents a pattern of state violations, and the remedy (accessibility retrofits and damages for denial) is congruent and proportional. Valid Section 5 legislation.

2. **Hypo:** Congress amends Title IX to explicitly abrogate state sovereign immunity and authorize damages suits by students denied educational opportunities on the basis of sex at state universities. The record includes decades of documented sex discrimination in state-operated athletics and STEM programs. **Why:** Intermediate scrutiny plus a Hibbs-style stereotype record makes the sweep of the remedy congruent to the constitutional injury. Valid Section 5 legislation under the Hibbs framework.

### Opposite-Side

3. **Hypo:** Congress enacts a federal Statute of Ethics mandating that every state official recuse from any proceeding in which the official's college classmate appears, backed by private damages liability against the state. No legislative record of due process violations is compiled. **Why:** No documented pattern of state constitutional violations, no heightened scrutiny tier, and the remedy is grossly overbroad. Like RFRA in Boerne, the statute operates as a substantive redefinition of due process rather than enforcement. Invalid.

4. **Hypo:** Congress enacts a Patent Reform and Enforcement Act that, as in the original Patent Remedy Act, abrogates state sovereign immunity for patent infringement. The record contains only aggregate statistics on patent filings by state universities without any showing that states have denied adequate remedies to infringed patent holders. **Why:** Controlled by Florida Prepaid and Allen v Cooper. Rational-basis-plus-remedy analysis means the constitutional floor is low, the record fails to show denial of adequate process, and the sweep of the abrogation is disproportionate. Invalid.

### Fence-Sitter

5. **Hypo:** Congress enacts a Comprehensive Disability Access Act applying to all state programs and services. The legislative record contains dense evidence of disability exclusion from courts (Lane-territory) mixed with lighter evidence of employment and general-services exclusion. A state sued for denying accessible online unemployment-insurance portals asserts sovereign immunity. **Why:** Lane upheld Title II as applied to courthouse access because that subset implicated a fundamental right. An online benefits portal arguably implicates due process (access to state benefits) but less clearly than physical courtroom access. Courts may borrow Lane's as-applied methodology, asking whether the specific application enforces a fundamental or heightened right with a supporting record, or they may confine Lane to court access specifically. Genuinely unclear under the congruence-and-proportionality test.

## How to Spot on an Exam

Section 5 issues surface whenever Congress purports to regulate states or state actors under a civil rights statute. Look for three trigger facts. First, a federal statute that either expressly abrogates state sovereign immunity or imposes substantive obligations on state conduct. Second, a state defendant asserting Eleventh Amendment immunity or challenging the statute as exceeding Congress's enumerated power. Third, a link (express or implied) to the Fourteenth Amendment, commonly through equal protection (race, sex, disability) or due process (fundamental rights, property, liberty).

The analytical framework follows four steps. Step one: identify the Fourteenth Amendment right Congress is enforcing. Step two: fix the scrutiny tier (strict, intermediate, rational basis, or fundamental-rights due process). Step three: assess the legislative record for a pattern of actual or threatened constitutional violations by states, remembering that rational-basis rights require a showing of conduct that would itself fail rational basis. Step four: test the statute's sweep against the record for congruence and proportionality. If the statute reaches beyond the constitutional floor without adequate justification, it fails.

Two cross-cutting points reward attention. First, Fitzpatrick is the only reliable abrogation route after Seminole Tribe v Florida; Article I power does not suffice. Second, Lane's as-applied methodology lets a statute survive in some applications and fail in others, so do not automatically facial-strike if one application is sound.

## Critique

The doctrine's central tension is whether the remedial-substantive line is stable. Kennedy in Boerne insists Congress cannot define the content of rights, yet Section 5 legislation inevitably operates by identifying behavior Congress thinks is problematic, which requires some judgment about the Constitution's content. Katzenbach v Morgan's deference to Congress reflected a belief that Congress has comparative advantages (fact-finding, democratic legitimacy) in deciding how much prophylaxis is needed. Boerne replaces that deference with a congruence-and-proportionality screen that, in Florida Prepaid and Allen v Cooper, looks a lot like reviewing the quality of Congress's fact-finding, a task courts are not obviously better at.

A second line of criticism focuses on the interaction with sovereign immunity. Seminole Tribe blocks Article I abrogation; Boerne then narrows Section 5 as a substitute. The combined effect is that many federal statutes (environmental, intellectual property, wage-and-hour) cannot reach states at all, even for conduct that ordinary statutory interpretation would deem clearly covered. Critics (including the Hibbs-through-Stevens dissenting tradition) argue this produces a federal-rights enforcement gap that lacks any textual basis in either Amendment.

A third line tracks the scrutiny-tier dependency. Garrett and Hibbs turn on whether disability or sex is the classification at issue. That makes congressional enforcement authority contingent on the Court's equal-protection tier scheme, which was itself contested and could shift. If the Court revisits the tiers (for instance, heightening scrutiny for sexual orientation or retreating from intermediate scrutiny for sex), the prophylactic space available to Congress moves with it. The doctrine is therefore structurally unstable: Section 5's scope tracks the Court's rights jurisprudence, which the Court, not Congress, controls, perpetuating the very supremacy asymmetry Harlan complained about in Katzenbach v Morgan.
