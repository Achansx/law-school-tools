# Standing

## Overview

Standing is the threshold doctrine that enforces Article III's limitation of federal jurisdiction to "Cases" and "Controversies." Before a federal court may reach the merits of any constitutional question, it must ask whether the plaintiff is the right party to bring the claim. Chandler presents the doctrine not as a procedural technicality but as a structural determinant of the scope of judicial review: when the Supreme Court tightens standing, it shrinks the category of government conduct that federal courts can scrutinize at all. The advisory-opinion baseline (Jay's refusal to answer Washington's neutrality questions) is the starting point; [[Cases/Marbury v Madison (1803)|Marbury v Madison]] supplies the case-or-controversy premise that the modern doctrine operationalizes.

The constitutional minimum crystallized in [[Cases/Lujan v Defenders of Wildlife (1992)|Lujan v Defenders of Wildlife]] requires three elements: an injury-in-fact that is concrete, particularized, and actual or imminent; a causal connection that is fairly traceable to the challenged conduct; and redressability that is likely, not merely speculative. Congress cannot lower this minimum by statute, though Congress can override prudential limits. Beyond the constitutional floor, a third layer of judge-made prudential limits (the bar on asserting third-party rights, on generalized grievances, and on claims outside the zone of interests) has progressively eroded, with two of its three pillars reclassified as either ordinary statutory interpretation or folded into Article III injury-in-fact.

In the Con Law I pool, the arc from 1976 through 2025 traces a trajectory of contraction. [[Lectures/Constitutional Standing - From Allen v Wright to Trump v CASA|Chandler's main standing lecture]] presents the sequence as progressive narrowing: Simon and Allen v. Wright installed the third-party-action problem, Lujan canonicalized the three-part test, Clapper imposed the chain-of-speculation bar, and Trump v. CASA paired standing limits with universal-injunction limits to reduce the federal judiciary's structural role. The [[Lectures/State Standing|State Standing lecture]] overlays a parallel contraction for state plaintiffs: Massachusetts v. EPA's "special solicitude" gloss was effectively set aside by United States v. Texas (2023) in the enforcement-discretion context, even as Biden v. Nebraska opened a new avenue through the state-instrumentality theory.

---

## Governing Rule

> **Rule (Lujan three-part test):** A plaintiff invoking federal jurisdiction must demonstrate (1) an injury-in-fact that is concrete and particularized and actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical; (2) a causal connection showing the injury is fairly traceable to the challenged conduct and not the result of the independent action of some third party not before the court; and (3) redressability, meaning it is likely (not merely speculative) that a favorable judicial decision will redress the injury.

> **Rule (congressional non-substitution):** Congress cannot convert a bare procedural right or a generalized public interest in executive compliance with law into a concrete individual injury. A statute granting "any person" a right to sue cannot manufacture Article III standing.

> **Rule (state special solicitude, contested):** States litigating quasi-sovereign interests are entitled to a more lenient standing threshold than private plaintiffs, but the gloss may not operate in cases challenging federal enforcement discretion, where the traditional-judicial-cognizance factor controls.

## Doctrinal Development

### Marbury v Madison (1803): The Case-or-Controversy Baseline

Every modern standing doctrine traces back to Marshall's acceptance of the proposition that federal judicial power extends to "cases" and "controversies" rather than abstract questions. The Jay advisory-opinion refusal of 1793 is the operational companion: federal courts will not issue legal advice, even to a sitting President. Marbury's second question (can the courts provide a remedy?) and its ministerial-discretionary line are the first Supreme Court articulations of the threshold inquiry that standing now performs. See [[Cases/Marbury v Madison (1803)|full brief]].

### Simon v Eastern Kentucky Welfare Rights Organization (1976): The Third-Party-Action Problem

The IRS relaxed charitable-care requirements for tax-exempt hospitals, and indigent patients who were turned away sued. Powell's majority held it purely speculative whether the denials of service were fairly traceable to the IRS policy, because hospitals had independent financial reasons for limiting care. The pattern that Simon installs (a causal chain running through the choices of an independent third party) recurs throughout the doctrine: Allen v. Wright, Clapper, and the enforcement-discretion cases all apply the same skepticism.

### Valley Forge Christian College v Americans United (1982): The Victimless Violation

The federal government transferred a 77-acre campus to a religious college for free. Americans United sued on Establishment Clause grounds and lost on standing on two independent bases. First, no taxpayer standing because the transfer was under the Property Clause (Article IV), not the Taxing and Spending Clause that the narrow Flast exception requires. Second, "psychological consequence" of observing governmental conduct one disagrees with is a generalized grievance, not an Article III injury. The implication: some constitutional violations may have no judicial remedy, not because no violation occurred but because no plaintiff has standing to press it. The offended-observer extension developed by lower courts collapsed after American Legion (2019) and [[Cases/Kennedy v Bremerton School District (2022)|Kennedy v Bremerton School District]].

### Allen v Wright (1984): The Central Case

Parents of Black schoolchildren in seven desegregating states sued the IRS for failing to deny tax-exempt status to segregation academies established to evade desegregation orders. Two claimed injuries: stigmatic (federal endorsement of racial discrimination) and educational (tax exemptions subsidized white flight, diminishing the quality of desegregated education). O'Connor's 5-3 majority rejected both. The stigmatic harm was a generalized grievance because the plaintiffs were not personally denied equal treatment by the challenged tax rulings. The desegregation harm was cognizable and, the Court acknowledged, "one of the most serious injuries in our legal system," but was not fairly traceable: the causal chain depended on independent choices by schools and white parents and was "attenuated at best."

Two moves in Allen drive the modern doctrine. The first is O'Connor's explicit grounding of standing in separation of powers: the federal judiciary is not a roving enforcer of executive faithful-execution obligations. The second is the circularity problem that Brennan and Stevens attacked in dissent: O'Connor demanded specificity about particular schools losing exemptions and enrollment declining, but that specificity is obtainable only through discovery, and standing must be established before discovery is permitted. Brennan's dissent argued "common sense alone" establishes causation where the schools exist solely for racial exclusion. Stevens, joined by Blackmun, argued the majority's objection ignored elementary economics: subsidize something and you get more of it, remove the subsidy and you get less.

### Lujan v Defenders of Wildlife (1992): Canonicalization

Environmental groups challenged a regulation limiting the Endangered Species Act's consultation requirement to domestic actions. Plaintiffs who planned to return "some day" to foreign habitats failed the imminence requirement. Scalia's majority formalized the three-part test, elevated it to "irreducible constitutional minimum," and tied standing directly to separation of powers: if Congress could convert a general public interest in executive compliance with law into an individual right to sue, it would transfer from the President to the courts the Chief Executive's most important constitutional duty, the faithful execution of the laws. Lujan is the doctrine's methodological anchor: every later standing case reads against its three elements and its congressional-non-substitution rule.

### City of Los Angeles v Lyons (1983): Past Injury and Prospective Relief

Adolph Lyons was subjected to a potentially lethal chokehold by LAPD officers during a traffic stop. He sued for damages and for an injunction barring future chokeholds. White's majority separated the two. Past injury establishes standing for damages but not for injunctive relief, which requires an imminent prospect of future personal harm. To obtain the injunction, Lyons would have had to show he would again be stopped by LAPD and again subjected to a chokehold. The structural implication is that standing doctrine resists structural-reform litigation: when the plaintiff's real objective is to change institutional policy rather than compensate one individual, the damages/injunction asymmetry channels the claim away from the reform the plaintiff seeks.

### Clapper v Amnesty International USA (2013): Chain of Speculation

Journalists and lawyers challenged NSA surveillance under Section 702 of FISA. Because targeting decisions were classified, plaintiffs could not prove interception; they could only point to a possibility that their communications had been swept up. Alito's 5-4 majority held the threatened injury was not "certainly impending" and the causal chain was "highly attenuated." The pattern echoes Allen: the Court demands near-deterministic certainty and refuses the probabilistic reasoning accepted in other legal contexts. The systemic consequence is structural insulation: the more secretive and diffuse the government conduct, the harder it is to establish standing, meaning the most constitutionally sensitive programs face the least judicial scrutiny.

### Friends of the Earth Inc v Laidlaw Environmental Services (2000): Voluntary Cessation

Environmental plaintiffs sued a permit holder for discharging mercury into a South Carolina river. Laidlaw came into compliance during the litigation and argued the case was moot. The Court held that a defendant cannot moot a case by voluntarily ceasing the challenged conduct unless there is "no reasonable expectation" that the conduct will resume. Laidlaw also treated the plaintiffs' ongoing fear of swimming and fishing near the polluted river as a concrete injury even absent proof that the water was actually unsafe, a doctrinal move that sits uneasily alongside Lujan's rigor.

### Abbott Laboratories v Gardner (1967): Ripeness and Pre-Enforcement Review

The ripeness companion to standing. Abbott Labs challenged FDA regulations before any enforcement action. The Court adopted a two-part test: (1) fitness of the issues for judicial decision, meaning legal, concrete, and not dependent on future contingencies, and (2) hardship to the parties of withholding court consideration. Regulatory compliance choices that impose immediate practical hardship (change your labels or risk enforcement) satisfy the second prong; pure statutory-interpretation questions satisfy the first. Abbott Labs makes pre-enforcement review routine in the administrative-law setting and is the doctrinal anchor for modern declaratory-judgment practice in regulated industries.

### Flast v Cohen (1968) and FEC v Akins (1998): The Narrow Exceptions

Flast is the narrow taxpayer-standing exception: plaintiffs may challenge specific congressional appropriations under the Taxing and Spending Clause on Establishment Clause grounds. Valley Forge limited Flast to that precise fact pattern. Hein (2007) held the exception does not reach executive-branch expenditures absent specific congressional earmarking. DaimlerChrysler v Cuno held state taxpayer standing is not recognized. The Flast exception is treated as essentially limited to its facts.

FEC v Akins cuts the other direction: Breyer's majority held that a widely shared "informational injury" (the denial of legally required disclosures) can support standing, even where many people share the grievance. Vermont Agency of Natural Resources v United States (2000) approved qui tam suits as an assignment of the government's claim to the private relator. These two doctrines operate as narrow workarounds to the generalized-grievance bar without unsettling the Lujan framework.

### Raines v Byrd (1997): Legislator Standing

Individual members of Congress lacked standing to challenge the Line Item Veto Act. Their injury was institutional (dilution of their legislative vote) rather than particularized, and so failed the injury-in-fact requirement as applied to individual legislators. The decision left open whether the House or Senate as an institution could sue in some circumstances; that question remains unsettled. Raines is the doctrinal foundation for the ongoing dispute over congressional-committee standing against the Executive.

### Massachusetts v EPA (2007): Special Solicitude for States

Massachusetts sued EPA for refusing to regulate CO2 as a pollutant, alleging rising sea levels were submerging state-owned coastal land. The Court found standing and articulated a "special solicitude" gloss: states have quasi-sovereign interests in protecting their natural resources and the welfare of their citizens, and when federal action or inaction threatens those interests, states face diminished capacity to protect themselves compared to fully sovereign entities. The concrete injury (coastal erosion of state-owned land) was fairly traceable to the EPA's regulatory gap and was redressable through the regulation the plaintiffs sought. The special-solicitude phrase became the pivot of state-standing doctrine for the next decade and a half.

### Biden v Nebraska (2023): State Instrumentality Theory

Missouri sued over the Biden administration's mass student-loan-forgiveness program, asserting standing through MOHELA, a state-created public corporation that services federal loans and would lose roughly $44 million a year in servicing fees. Roberts's majority treated MOHELA's structural relationship to Missouri as controlling: Missouri created MOHELA, designated it an "instrumentality" of the State, supervises its board, and relies on it to perform the public function of facilitating student access to higher-education finance. Harm to MOHELA in the performance of its public function is a direct injury to Missouri, and Missouri did not need to demonstrate that MOHELA itself chose to sue. Kagan's dissent argued MOHELA was a legally and financially separate entity that had chosen not to sue and whose revenues did not flow to Missouri, and accused the majority of "blowing through a constitutional guardrail." The case opens a new avenue for state standing through state-created government corporations, subject to Kagan's separateness objection. See [[Cases/Biden v Nebraska (2023)|full brief]].

### California v Texas (2021) and United States v Texas (2023): The Retrenchment

California v Texas dismissed a challenge to the zeroed-out ACA individual mandate for lack of standing. The individual plaintiffs suffered no penalty; Texas's inseverability theory (the zero-penalty mandate's unconstitutionality meant Texas bore the burden of the ACA's other provisions) was too attenuated. Special solicitude was not invoked to relax the requirements.

United States v Texas (2023) went further. Texas challenged the Mayorkas enforcement-priority memo as violating mandatory-detention statutes. Kavanaugh's 8-1 majority held the states lacked standing: financial costs are real but must be "legally and judicially cognizable," rooted in something "traditionally thought to be capable of resolution through the judicial process." There is no historical tradition of courts ordering the Executive to change its arrest or prosecution policies to make more arrests, and the separation-of-powers concern is acute when such relief would intrude on a core Article II function. Special solicitude effectively disappeared from the analysis. Kavanaugh left four caveats: selective prosecution (equal-protection challenges to being singled out), congressional authorization (if Congress creates a specific right to sue, standing might exist), total abdication (wholesale abandonment of statutory responsibilities), and benefits versus enforcement (challenges to affirmative grants of status are distinguishable from challenges to non-enforcement). See [[Lectures/United States v Texas - Standing and Enforcement Discretion|the dedicated lecture]].

### Trump v CASA (2025): Standing and Universal Injunctions Together

Chandler's main standing lecture ends with CASA because the case connects standing limits (who can sue) to universal-injunction limits (what relief courts can grant). Together, the two mechanisms narrow the structural role of the federal judiciary over government conduct: even when a plaintiff clears standing, the relief a court can issue is constrained to the plaintiff before it. The lecture frames the arc from Simon (1976) through CASA (2025) as a progressive contraction of the federal judiciary's reach, in which Article III's case-or-controversy limitation does more and more of the regulatory work.

### Prudential Standing: The Eroding Third Layer

Three judge-made limits operated alongside the constitutional minimum: the bar on asserting the legal rights of third parties (jus tertii), the bar on generalized grievances shared by all citizens, and the zone-of-interests test. Congress has always been able to override prudential standing; it cannot override constitutional standing. Lexmark International v Static Control (2014) reclassified zone of interests as ordinary statutory interpretation rather than a prudential overlay, calling "prudential standing" a misnomer. Generalized grievances were constitutionalized, folded into Article III injury-in-fact under Lujan. FDA v R.J. Reynolds Vapor Co. (2025) confirmed the zone-of-interests reclassification and abandoned the label as "misleading." Third-party standing remains the last holdout, and Chandler treats it as on borrowed time.

## Comparison: The Lujan Three-Part Test and Common Failure Modes

| Element | Doctrinal Test | Paradigm Failure | Leading Case |
|---|---|---|---|
| Injury-in-fact | Concrete, particularized, actual or imminent, not conjectural | Generalized grievance; "some day" plans; psychological offense | Lujan (imminence), Valley Forge (psychological), Allen (stigmatic) |
| Traceability | Fairly traceable to defendant's conduct, not to independent third-party action | Causal chain running through independent choices of intermediaries | Simon (hospitals), Allen (schools and parents), Clapper (classified targeting) |
| Redressability | Likely (not speculative) that a favorable decision will redress the injury | Past injury that no injunction can undo; relief that depends on unrelated third parties | Lyons (past chokehold, no injunction), Steel Co. (past violation, no prospective relief) |

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side

1. **Hypo:** An environmental group's members state they will travel to a specific foreign habitat within the next calendar year, with itineraries already booked, and argue that a new regulation will remove endangered species they planned to observe. **Why:** Concrete and particularized (named members, specific travel), actual or imminent (fixed itineraries), traceable to the regulation (the species removal is a direct consequence), and redressable (vacating the regulation restores the listing). The Lujan imminence defect is cured by the booking specificity. Injury-in-fact and the full three-part test are satisfied.

2. **Hypo:** A regulated pharmaceutical manufacturer challenges a labeling rule under the APA before any enforcement action is brought. Compliance requires immediate and costly relabeling of existing inventory. **Why:** Abbott Laboratories v Gardner supplies the analysis. The legal question (statutory authority for the rule) is fit for resolution without additional factual development, and compliance imposes immediate hardship (relabel or risk enforcement). Pre-enforcement review is available.

### Opposite-Side

3. **Hypo:** A citizen sues the President for violating the Foreign Emoluments Clause by receiving payments from foreign governments through hotel bookings. The citizen has no distinctive personal injury beyond the general interest in presidential compliance with the Constitution. **Why:** The claim is a generalized grievance. The Lujan particularity requirement and the constitutionalized generalized-grievance bar operate together to defeat standing. The citizen's interest is one shared with every American, and Allen v. Wright's separation-of-powers framing bars recasting a public-interest grievance as a judicial claim.

4. **Hypo:** Attorneys and journalists sue to enjoin the NSA's surveillance of foreign targets, alleging their communications with overseas clients are likely being intercepted, although they have no classified information confirming interception. **Why:** Clapper controls. The threatened injury is not "certainly impending" because plaintiffs cannot show interception, and the chain of speculation runs through covert executive targeting decisions that plaintiffs cannot probe. The classified nature of the program is not a workaround; it is the structural obstacle that the doctrine accepts.

### Fence-Sitter

5. **Hypo:** A state sues to enjoin federal enforcement-priority guidance that directs officers not to pursue certain categories of immigration violations. The state documents specific incremental costs to its social-services system attributable to persons who would have been detained under prior guidance but have been released under the new priorities. **Why:** This is the hardest variant post-2023. Massachusetts v EPA style special solicitude and documented financial costs point toward standing; United States v Texas (2023) points the other way, treating challenges to federal enforcement discretion as not "traditionally thought to be capable of resolution through the judicial process" regardless of demonstrated financial harm. The answer turns on whether the state can fit its claim into Kavanaugh's four caveats: selective prosecution, express congressional authorization to sue, total abdication of statutory responsibilities, or a benefits-versus-enforcement framing. Without one of those footholds, the modern cases run against the claim even where the injury is real and substantial.

## How to Spot on an Exam

**Trigger facts.** A constitutional challenge filed by a plaintiff whose injury is diffuse, probabilistic, or depends on the independent choices of non-parties. A state suit against the federal Executive, particularly over enforcement discretion. A pre-enforcement APA challenge. A damages-plus-injunction complaint where the injunction depends on future personal harm. A citizen or legislator suit over institutional compliance with the Constitution.

**Analytical framework.**

1. Run the three-part Lujan test in order. Identify the asserted injury and test it against concreteness, particularity, and imminence. Then test the causal chain for independent intervening actors. Then test whether the requested relief actually redresses the injury.
2. Separate damages standing from injunctive standing. Lyons is the reflex to apply whenever the complaint includes a forward-looking injunction: concrete past injury is a damages theory, not an injunction theory.
3. Identify third-party-action patterns. Any case where the causal chain runs through independent actors (hospitals, schools, parents, covert agency targeting) invites a Simon or Allen v Wright traceability challenge.
4. If the plaintiff is a state, identify whether the dispute is over federal enforcement discretion. If so, apply United States v Texas (2023): financial cost alone is not enough; the claim must fit one of Kavanaugh's four caveats, or special solicitude is unavailable.
5. For citizen-suit provisions and informational-injury claims, run the Lujan congressional-non-substitution rule. Congress can grant procedural rights but cannot convert a generalized interest in executive compliance into Article III injury.
6. Distinguish ripeness (is the dispute ready now?) from mootness (has the dispute disappeared?). Abbott Labs governs pre-enforcement ripeness; Friends of the Earth v Laidlaw governs voluntary-cessation mootness.
7. Keep standing distinct from the political-question doctrine. Standing asks whether the plaintiff is the right party; political-question asks whether the subject matter is committed to a coordinate branch. See [[Topics/Political Question Doctrine|Political Question Doctrine]] for the subject-matter inquiry.

**Common traps.**

- Do not confuse "injury" with "harm from government conduct the plaintiff dislikes." Valley Forge and Allen v. Wright both rejected theories of stigmatic or psychological harm that sounded intuitively compelling.
- Do not assume Congress can fix a standing problem by writing a citizen-suit provision. Lujan and FDA v R.J. Reynolds Vapor Co. foreclose statutory shortcuts around the constitutional minimum.
- Do not treat state standing as a separate doctrine. State plaintiffs run the same three-part test, with the contested special-solicitude gloss and the United States v Texas retrenchment overlaid.
- Do not treat prudential standing as a live doctrine with three independent pillars. Zone-of-interests is now statutory interpretation (Lexmark, FDA v R.J. Reynolds Vapor); generalized grievances are constitutionalized; only third-party standing remains as a prudential limit, and even it is under pressure.

## Critique

Four tensions run through the modern doctrine.

The first is the circularity that Brennan and Stevens identified in Allen v Wright. Standing must be established before discovery is permitted, but O'Connor demanded specificity about causation and redressability that is available only through discovery. The problem is structural rather than case-specific: the Lujan three-part test is deployed at the pleading stage, and plaintiffs must plead facts they often cannot yet access. The result is that structurally diffuse programs (IRS enforcement, covert surveillance, executive non-enforcement) are effectively insulated from any merits review, because no plaintiff can plead to the specificity the doctrine demands before discovery is available. Critics describe this as a one-way ratchet against challenges to structural government conduct.

The second is the asymmetry between the standing bar's rigor and the executive branch's practical insulation. The more systemic and covert the conduct, the harder the standing burden becomes. Clapper illustrates the paradox: surveillance programs that most implicate civil liberties are structurally immune from standing challenges because the injury cannot be proved without classified information. The same asymmetry drives Simon and Allen v Wright. If the aim of Article III's case-or-controversy requirement is to keep courts from supervising general executive policy, the modern doctrine operates as a thumb on the scale in favor of whichever branch's conduct is most opaque.

The third is the uneasy status of special solicitude after United States v Texas (2023). Massachusetts v EPA articulated the doctrine as a structural accommodation to the states' diminished capacity to protect themselves against federal overreach. United States v Texas did not formally overrule the gloss, but it functionally removed it from enforcement-discretion cases by making "traditional judicial cognizance" the controlling factor. The result is a party-neutral asymmetry: any administration can deprioritize enforcement of any statute, and states injured by non-enforcement face the courthouse door regardless of the political valence of the underlying policy. Whether the Biden v Nebraska instrumentality theory offsets this contraction depends on how broadly it is read.

The fourth is the structural stakes of the Trump v CASA pairing. Standing limits (who can sue) and universal-injunction limits (what relief a court can issue) together narrow the judiciary's structural role. Critics argue the combination produces a doctrinal regime in which constitutional rights are formally recognized but practically unenforced because no plaintiff can both get into court and obtain effective relief. Defenders of the trajectory argue that it restores the Article III limitation on the federal judiciary to what the Framers contemplated and reassigns enforcement of diffuse constitutional interests to the political branches, where the Framers left them.
