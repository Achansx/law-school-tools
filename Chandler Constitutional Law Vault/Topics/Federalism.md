# Federalism

## Overview

Federalism in Con Law I is the architecture that distributes governmental authority between the national government and the States. The vault's case pool approaches it through three complementary faces. The first is judicial federalism: the Supreme Court's appellate authority over state-court judgments on federal questions, which [[Cases/Martin v Hunter's Lessee (1816)|Martin v Hunter's Lessee]] establishes. The second is legislative federalism: the scope of Congress's enumerated and implied powers and the limits on state interference with federal instrumentalities, which [[Cases/McCulloch v Maryland (1819)|McCulloch v Maryland]] defines. The third is emergency and incorporation federalism: how federal power expands in wartime ([[Cases/The Prize Cases (1863)|The Prize Cases]]), where the Court draws the line on presidential domestic seizure in the absence of congressional authorization ([[Cases/Youngstown Sheet and Tube Co v Sawyer (1952)|Youngstown]]), and how Bill of Rights guarantees bind the States through the Fourteenth Amendment ([[Cases/McDonald v City of Chicago (2010)|McDonald]]). The through-line is the rejection of compact theory: the federal government's authority flows from the people directly, not from the States as intermediaries, and state institutions cannot insulate themselves from federal law even when they disagree with it.

---

## Governing Rule

> **Rule:** The Constitution establishes a national government of enumerated powers supreme within its sphere. Federal law, when made in pursuance of the Constitution, is the supreme law of the land under Article VI; state law yielding to federal law when the two conflict, and state institutions yield to federal appellate authority on federal-question matters. Congress may use any means appropriate and plainly adapted to its enumerated ends so long as the means are not prohibited (McCulloch). States may not single out federal instrumentalities for discriminatory taxation or regulation (intergovernmental tax immunity). The Supreme Court's appellate power extends to state-court judgments on federal questions, because it is the case and not the court that gives jurisdiction (Martin). The Fourteenth Amendment overrides the antebellum rule that the Bill of Rights binds only the federal government, via selective incorporation against the States.

## Doctrinal Development

### The Antebellum Baseline: Barron v Baltimore (1833)

Chief Justice Marshall held in Barron that the Bill of Rights applies only to the federal government. Before the Civil War, a State could violate what would have been a federal constitutional right with no recourse under the Bill of Rights. The Anti-Federalists who demanded the first ten amendments feared centralized power, so applying those amendments against the States would have inverted their purpose. Barron sets the pre-Fourteenth baseline that the incorporation doctrine later displaces. See [[Lectures/Barron v Baltimore - Pre-Fourteenth Amendment Baseline|lecture]] and [[Topics/Incorporation Doctrine|Incorporation Doctrine]].

### Judicial Federalism: Martin v Hunter's Lessee (1816)

Justice Story, writing for the Court (Marshall recused), held that Section 25 of the Judiciary Act of 1789 was constitutional and that the Supreme Court's appellate power extends to cases pending in state courts. The argument proceeds from Article III plus the Supremacy Clause: it is the case, not the court, that gives jurisdiction, and federal law would fracture into as many readings as there are state supreme courts if state judgments on federal questions were unreviewable. Martin rejects the Virginia Court of Appeals' compact-theory reasoning (Judge Spencer Roane) that state courts are coordinate sovereigns immune from federal appellate revision. The modern adequate-and-independent-state-ground doctrine is the limiting principle that preserves state-law autonomy while leaving Martin's federal-question holding intact. See [[Cases/Martin v Hunter's Lessee (1816)|full brief]] and [[Lectures/Martin v Hunter's Lessee - Appellate Authority|lecture]].

### Legislative Federalism: McCulloch v Maryland (1819)

Marshall's unanimous opinion answered two questions with lasting structural consequences. First, Congress possesses implied powers under the Necessary and Proper Clause: let the end be legitimate, let it be within the scope of the Constitution, and all means which are appropriate, which are plainly adapted to that end, which are not prohibited, but consist with the letter and spirit of the Constitution, are constitutional. Second, the Supremacy Clause bars States from taxing federal instrumentalities, because the power to tax involves the power to destroy, and a State that taxes a federal instrumentality could defeat a power Congress constitutionally exercised. Marshall limited the tax holding: non-discriminatory ordinary property taxes and taxes on citizens' interests in the Bank remain permissible. McCulloch also rests on popular sovereignty rather than state compact theory, grounding federal authority in the people acting directly rather than in the States as intermediaries. See [[Cases/McCulloch v Maryland (1819)|full brief]].

### Wartime Federalism: The Prize Cases (1863)

Justice Grier for a five-to-four majority upheld Lincoln's unilateral naval blockade of Confederate ports without a congressional declaration of war. The Court held that when a civil war was actually pending, the President was bound to meet it in the shape it presented itself, without waiting for Congress to baptize it with a name. The federalism significance is that the federal war power responds to the actual state of affairs and can be invoked defensively in a civil conflict that threatens the Union itself, without first clearing the constitutional formality of a declaration. Prize is the canonical precedent for expansive federal emergency power in response to rebellion against the federal system. See [[Cases/The Prize Cases (1863)|full brief]] and [[Topics/War Powers|War Powers]].

### Separation Plus Federalism: Youngstown Sheet and Tube Co v Sawyer (1952)

Black's opinion for the Court, joined by Frankfurter and Douglas, rejected President Truman's steel-mill seizure during the Korean War because no statute authorized the seizure, no implied executive power reached domestic industrial takings in the absence of congressional authorization, and the Taft-Hartley Act's legislative history affirmatively reflected Congress's rejection of seizure as a labor-dispute remedy. The federalism significance is that federal power, even in a wartime setting, is not a free-standing executive authority that overrides statutes; Congress's institutional choices bind the President within the federal government, and by extension the federal-state balance presupposed by Article I's enumerated-powers structure remains intact. Jackson's concurrence supplies the three-zone framework the modern doctrine applies. See [[Cases/Youngstown Sheet and Tube Co v Sawyer (1952)|full brief]] and [[Topics/Executive Power|Executive Power]].

### Incorporation Federalism: McDonald v City of Chicago (2010)

Alito's plurality held the Second Amendment right recognized in [[Cases/District of Columbia v Heller (2008)|Heller]] applies against the States through the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. The federalism significance is twofold. First, the rule that the Bill of Rights binds only the federal government (Barron's baseline) no longer holds for incorporated provisions; state and municipal regulations are measured against the same federal standard that binds Congress. Second, Justice Thomas's concurrence, supplying the fifth vote, argues that the textually correct vehicle is the Privileges or Immunities Clause rather than Due Process, reviving a methodological dispute that Slaughter-House had appeared to settle in 1873. Duncan's no-watered-down-state-versions principle controls the post-incorporation application: a State is bound to the full federal standard, not a weaker state-level version. See [[Cases/McDonald v City of Chicago (2010)|full brief]] and [[Topics/Incorporation Doctrine|Incorporation Doctrine]].

Reading the rows horizontally captures the vault's federalism architecture: the federal government's primacy operates differently when the tribunal is a state court (Martin), when the regulator is a state legislature taxing a federal entity (McCulloch), when the actor is the President responding to rebellion (Prize Cases), and when the regulator is a State acting against an individual right (McDonald). The compact-theory response that recurs in each register is consistently rejected in the vault pool.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side

1. **Hypo:** A state supreme court reads the Fourth Amendment to permit a warrantless search of a vehicle that the Supreme Court has previously held unconstitutional. The losing criminal defendant petitions for certiorari on the federal constitutional question. **Why:** Martin's case-not-court principle applies directly. The state-court judgment's interpretation of the Fourth Amendment is a federal question subject to Supreme Court appellate review, and on the merits the Supreme Court may reverse. The state-court origin does not insulate the federal-law ruling from federal appellate revision. This is the paradigmatic Martin hypothetical.

2. **Hypo:** Congress charters a federal student-loan corporation to administer education-lending programs, and a state imposes a special tax on its operations. **Why:** Under McCulloch, if the corporation is appropriate and plainly adapted to executing Congress's spending and taxing powers, it is constitutional. The state tax that singles out the federal instrumentality for discriminatory burden fails because the power to tax is the power to destroy a federal instrumentality. The state retains power to levy ordinary non-discriminatory property taxes on the corporation's real estate, the carve-out Marshall preserved.

### Opposite-Side

3. **Hypo:** A state supreme court resolves a boundary dispute under a state adverse-possession statute with no federal element, and the losing party invokes Martin to press Supreme Court review. **Why:** Martin is framed around federal questions (cases arising under the Constitution, laws, and treaties of the United States). A pure state-law question falls outside Article III's enumerated categories, so neither Section 25 nor Martin's case-not-court reasoning reaches it. Federal appellate jurisdiction does not lie.

4. **Hypo:** A state imposes a general property tax that applies equally to all real property within its borders, including a parcel owned by a federal instrumentality. **Why:** Marshall explicitly carved out this scenario in McCulloch. States retain the power to tax real property of a federal entity in common with the other real property within the State. The tax is not discriminatory and does not single out a federal instrumentality, so it does not violate intergovernmental tax immunity. The vice of Maryland's original law was discrimination, not taxation as such.

### Fence-Sitter

5. **Hypo:** A state enacts a handgun-microstamping statute that imprints a unique identifier on each cartridge case discharged. A resident sues, arguing the regulation conflicts with the Second Amendment as incorporated against the State under McDonald. **Why:** Under Duncan's no-watered-down-state-versions principle, the challenge is analyzed under the same [[Cases/New York State Rifle and Pistol Association v Bruen (2022)|Bruen]] text-history-and-tradition framework that would apply at the federal level. Whether the statute falls depends not on incorporation federalism but on whether the government can identify a relevantly similar historical analogue under Bruen. Incorporation does the threshold federalism work; it does not supply the substantive test. The difficulty is that the substantive test is itself contested on the level-of-generality axis (see [[Cases/United States v Rahimi (2024)|Rahimi]] majority and Thomas dissent).

## How to Spot on an Exam

Federalism issues appear under several signals:

- A state court has decided a federal-law question and the losing party seeks Supreme Court review. Trigger: Martin appellate-jurisdiction analysis. Ask whether the judgment rests on an adequate and independent state-law ground before assuming the federal question is reviewable.
- A state regulates (especially taxes) a federal entity or activity. Trigger: McCulloch intergovernmental-immunity analysis. Ask whether the regulation singles out federal operations for discriminatory burden, or whether it is a generally applicable measure that happens to reach federal activity.
- Congress acts under an enumerated power and the question is whether the means chosen are constitutional. Trigger: McCulloch N&P Clause analysis. Ask whether the end is legitimate, within the scope of the Constitution, and whether the means are plainly adapted and not prohibited.
- The President acts domestically during a military or foreign-policy emergency. Trigger: compare Prize Cases (war power responds to actual state of affairs) with Youngstown (executive seizure of domestic industry requires statutory authorization). Ask whether Congress has spoken, whether the conduct is abroad or at home, and whether the claimed authority is inherent or statutory.
- A state regulates conduct that arguably implicates a Bill of Rights guarantee. Trigger: incorporation analysis. Ask whether the right has been selectively incorporated against the States (speech, religion, search and seizure, jury trial, arms, excessive fines), and if so apply the full federal standard under Duncan's no-watered-down rule.

Analytical framework to deploy:

1. Identify which face of federalism is at issue (judicial, legislative, executive, or rights).
2. Identify the constitutional clause or provision the federal rule rests on (Article III and Supremacy Clause for Martin; N&P Clause and Supremacy Clause for McCulloch; Article II Commander in Chief for Prize Cases; Fourteenth Amendment for McDonald).
3. Identify the state-level response or theory (compact theory, coordinate sovereignty, intergovernmental immunity in reverse, pre-Fourteenth baseline) and note that the vault's case pool consistently rejects it.
4. Apply the governing test from the anchor case, and flag the limiting principle (adequate-and-independent-state-ground for Martin; non-discriminatory ordinary taxation for McCulloch; congressional-authorization requirement for domestic executive action under Youngstown; Duncan's no-watered-down rule for incorporation).

## Critique

Federalism doctrine is uneven across the vault's four registers. The judicial-federalism strand (Martin) is settled in a way the others are not: the case-not-court principle commands near-universal assent among modern commentators, and the adequate-and-independent-state-ground limiting principle supplies a workable accommodation to state-law autonomy. The legislative-federalism strand (McCulloch) has been contested for two centuries. Marshall's test, that any means appropriate and plainly adapted to a legitimate enumerated end are constitutional, is sufficiently permissive that it is unclear what congressional action fails it. Modern originalist critics (Thomas especially) argue the test effectively reads the enumerated-powers limitation out of the Constitution. The executive-federalism strand (Prize Cases, Youngstown) leaves the hardest unresolved question: where does the line between war-responsive federal power and statute-bound federal power actually run? The vault lecture on [[Lectures/Presidential War Powers - Iran Hypothetical|Iran]] suggests the line is messier than Prize or Youngstown alone admits.

The rights-federalism strand (incorporation) is textually awkward. The Court has accomplished through the Due Process Clause what the Fourteenth Amendment's drafters expected the Privileges or Immunities Clause to do. A Privileges or Immunities revival (McDonald Thomas concurrence, Timbs Thomas and Gorsuch concurrences) would preserve the same substantive outcomes while repositioning their textual foundation. Whether such a revival would change outcomes at the margins, especially for unenumerated rights, is the live open question.

A progressive critique from the Adamson-dissent tradition (Justice Black) argues that the selective-incorporation framework gives the Court boundless power under natural law to expand and contract constitutional standards, while a total-incorporation approach grounded in the historical evidence that the Fourteenth Amendment's framers intended Privileges or Immunities to apply the full Bill of Rights would produce a more principled doctrine. The Black position has never commanded a majority but remains theoretically plausible and is treated by the vault's incorporation lecture as the leading alternative to the status quo.

An originalist critique treats the compact-theory tradition (Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions, Judge Spencer Roane's Martin dissent-in-form, Calhoun's nullification doctrine) as a serious strand of Founding-era constitutional thought that Martin and McCulloch rejected without fully engaging. The modern adequate-and-independent-state-ground doctrine is one concession to that tradition: it preserves the space for state-law autonomy that compact theory sought to protect, without yielding the federal-question ground Martin secured.

## Comparison: New Federalism Commerce Clause Decisions (Lopez, Morrison, NFIB)

This table maps the three Rehnquist/Roberts Court Commerce Clause decisions that imposed federalism limits on congressional power against the New Deal baseline established by Wickard.

| Axis | [[Cases/Wickard v Filburn (1942)\|Wickard v Filburn]] (1942) | [[Cases/United States v Lopez (1995)\|United States v Lopez]] (1995) | [[Cases/United States v Morrison (2000)\|United States v Morrison]] (2000) | [[Cases/NFIB v Sebelius (2012)\|NFIB v Sebelius]] (2012) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Statute / conduct | Agricultural Adjustment Act quota; home-consumed wheat | Gun-Free School Zones Act; firearm possession near schools | Violence Against Women Act civil remedy; gender-motivated violence | Affordable Care Act individual mandate; failure to purchase health insurance |
| Category at issue | Substantial effects on interstate commerce (third Lopez category) | Substantial effects — but statute fails | Substantial effects — but statute fails | Commerce Clause (activity-inactivity limit) |
| Economic activity? | Yes — home-grown wheat competes with market wheat | No — possessing a gun near a school is not economic activity | No — gender-motivated violence is not economic activity | No majority on Commerce Clause; Roberts (controlling): inactivity cannot be regulated as commerce |
| Aggregation permitted? | Yes — individual contribution trivial, but class-wide effect is substantial | No — non-economic local activity cannot be aggregated into substantial effects on commerce | No — extensive congressional economic findings cannot substitute for the economic-activity predicate | Not reached — mandate fails on activity-inactivity ground before aggregation analysis applies |
| Congressional findings | Implicit in statutory framework; Court defers | Court finds inadequate jurisdictional nexus; findings that gun violence affects learning do not convert non-economic activity to economic | Extensive findings (four years of hearings, $3B+ economic cost estimate) — Court holds findings irrelevant when activity is non-economic | ACA contains extensive health-market findings; Roberts holds they cannot overcome the inactivity problem |
| Vote | 9-0 (Jackson, unanimous) | 5-4 (Rehnquist; Thomas concurrence for broader originalist critique) | 5-4 (Rehnquist; same five-Justice majority as Lopez) | 5-4 on Commerce Clause limit; 5-4 separately on taxing power save |
| Federalism rationale | Post-New Deal settlement: political safeguards of federalism; broad judicial deference to congressional economic judgments | "Truly national" vs. "truly local" distinction; education and local crime are "areas of traditional state concern" | Gender-motivated violence is "within the province of the States"; federal police power rejected; Section 5 also fails for lack of state action | Activity-inactivity line preserves space where individuals are simply left alone; extends but does not contradict Lopez/Morrison |
| Surviving authority | Reaffirmed by Gonzales v Raich (2005) for economic activity in a comprehensive regulatory scheme | Controlling authority for three-category framework; Lopez-category-one and -two (channels and instrumentalities) are undiminished | Consolidates Lopez; adds Section 5 state-action constraint on enforcement power | Roberts majority distinguishes (does not overrule) Lopez/Morrison; the activity-inactivity line supplements rather than replaces the economic-activity limit |

The table's through-line: Wickard's aggregation principle remains the baseline for economic activity in a comprehensive regulatory scheme (as Raich confirms). Lopez, Morrison, and NFIB each establish that the aggregation move fails outside that zone — for non-economic activity (Lopez, Morrison) and for economic inactivity (NFIB). The New Federalism cases do not overrule Wickard; they establish its limits.

## Comparison: Conditional-Spending Coercion Trajectory (Dole, NFIB)

This table maps how the Rehnquist four-part test from Dole evolved into an enforceable coercion ceiling in NFIB.

| Axis | [[Cases/South Dakota v Dole (1987)\|South Dakota v Dole]] (1987) | [[Cases/NFIB v Sebelius (2012)\|NFIB v Sebelius]] (2012) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Condition | 5 percent withholding of federal highway funds for States with a drinking age below 21 | Loss of all existing Medicaid funding for States that refuse ACA Medicaid expansion |
| Governing test | General welfare, unambiguous condition, germane to federal interest, no independent constitutional bar | Same four-part test is restated, but the coercion prong is finally applied |
| Coercion holding | Dictum only; 5 percent is described as mild encouragement rather than coercion | First time the Court strikes a spending condition as coercive; loss of all existing Medicaid is the "gun to the head" |
| Germaneness handling | Easily satisfied because drinking age correlates with safe interstate highway travel | Not reached; the case turns on the coercion prong before germaneness is argued |
| Twenty-first Amendment / federal floor | Twenty-first Amendment would bar Congress from setting a national drinking age directly; Spending Clause is the workaround | Tenth Amendment structural reservation; Congress cannot use withdrawal of existing entitlement funding to commandeer state legislative processes |
| Vote | 7-2 (Brennan and O'Connor dissenting) | 7-2 that the expansion as conditioned is coercive (Roberts with Breyer and Kagan, plus joint dissent of Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Alito) |
| Doctrinal increment | Establishes the four-part framework and names coercion as the outer boundary without finding it crossed | Operationalizes coercion for the first time; creates a judicially enforceable ceiling on conditional-spending leverage |

The through-line: Dole frames conditional spending as a permissible workaround to enumerated-powers federalism because a state's choice to forgo federal money remains meaningful at 5 percent. NFIB identifies the breaking point where a conditional-spending statute crosses from inducement to commandeering. The Twenty-first Amendment sets the direct-regulation floor in Dole; the Tenth Amendment's structural reservation of sovereign state budgetary autonomy supplies the functional analog in NFIB.
