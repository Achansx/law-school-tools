# Equal Protection

## Overview

The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment commands that no State shall "deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws." Because all legislation classifies -- treating different persons differently -- the clause cannot prohibit classification as such. It requires instead that some classifications receive more rigorous justification than others. The entire edifice of equal protection doctrine rests on this premise and on three threshold questions: which classifications trigger heightened scrutiny, what does "heightened" require, and who decides.

The clause was ratified in 1868 with what Justice Miller described as "one pervading purpose" -- protecting the newly freed slaves from state-imposed subordination through institutions like the Black Codes. That historical origin has not confined the clause: over 150 years the Court extended it to sex, national origin, alienage, and other classifications, developing a tiered framework in which the level of judicial scrutiny reflects the Court's judgment about how often a given type of classification is a good idea.

Bolling v Sharpe (1954) extended equal protection analysis to the federal government by reading it into the Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause, reasoning that it would be "unthinkable" for the same Constitution to prohibit states from maintaining segregated schools while imposing no analogous constraint on the federal government.

---

## Governing Rule

> **Rule (Strict Scrutiny -- Race, National Origin, Alienage):** A classification based on race, national origin, or alienage must be narrowly tailored to serve a compelling governmental interest. The government bears the burden. The standard is symmetrical: it applies regardless of which racial group bears the burden (Adarand Constructors Inc v Pena, 1995). The standard is "strict in theory, fatal in fact" -- nearly all laws subject to it are struck down.

> **Rule (Intermediate Scrutiny -- Sex, Legitimacy):** A classification based on sex must serve important governmental objectives and must be substantially related to achievement of those objectives (Craig v Boren, 1976). The government bears the burden. After United States v Virginia (1996), parties defending sex-based government action must demonstrate an "exceedingly persuasive justification" -- one that is genuine, not post-hoc, and does not rely on overbroad generalizations about the different talents, capacities, or preferences of males and females.

> **Rule (Rational Basis -- All Other Classifications):** All other classifications -- age, disability, wealth, and all remaining categories -- need only be rationally related to a legitimate governmental interest. The burden falls on the challenger, and nearly all laws survive. Exception: where a law rests on bare animus or irrational prejudice rather than any legitimate interest, it fails even rational basis review. This heightened rational basis standard, without forming a formal fourth tier, has struck down laws targeting persons with intellectual disabilities (City of Cleburne v Cleburne Living Center, 1985) and laws imposing broad disabilities based on sexual orientation (Romer v Evans, 1996).

## Doctrinal Development

### The Textual Problem and the Foundation of Tiers

The Equal Protection Clause's literal command -- no denial of "the equal protection of the laws" -- cannot mean no legislative classification, because every law classifies. The necessary inference is that some classifications require more justification than others. The entire three-tier structure flows from this inference, and four theoretical frameworks compete to explain which classifications are suspect and why.

Carolene Products' Footnote Four (1938) supplied one foundation: rational basis applies as the default for ordinary commercial legislation, but three categories of laws warrant more searching scrutiny -- laws restricting rights in the Bill of Rights, laws restricting the political process, and laws targeting "discrete and insular minorities" whose marginalization "tends seriously to curtail the operation of those political processes ordinarily to be relied upon to protect minorities." This process-theory rationale explains why the Court might intervene where democratic accountability fails, but it does not explain all of the doctrine.

### Racial Equal Protection: Plessy to Brown to SFFA

Plessy v Ferguson (1896) established that "separate but equal" public accommodations do not violate the Fourteenth Amendment. Justice Brown drew a sharp distinction between political equality (which the Amendment protects) and social equality (which it does not). The majority rejected the argument that separation stamped Black people with a badge of inferiority, reasoning that any such inference was purely self-imposed. Harlan's dissent declared that "our Constitution is color-blind, and neither knows nor tolerates classes among citizens" -- a principle later adopted as the foundation of the SFFA majority's anti-classification holding, though Harlan's dissent was itself built on explicitly anti-Chinese reasoning that revealed the instability of any principle that can only be articulated by reference to a disfavored comparison group.

Plessy stood for 58 years. Brown v Board of Education (1954) overturned its application to public education by holding that "separate educational facilities are inherently unequal," eliminating the institution-by-institution inequality burden that had made challenges practically impossible. Warren's opinion was deliberately short and narrow -- the price of a unanimous Court -- and it relied on social science evidence that critics later identified as methodologically contestable. Brown's briefness produced decades of enforcement ambiguity: "all deliberate speed" (Brown II, 1955) built delay into the remedy, and by 1964 only about 2% of Black children in the South attended integrated schools.

Hernandez v Texas (1954) rejected the "two-class theory" of the Equal Protection Clause -- the argument that the Amendment recognized only "white" and "Negro" as protected categories. Warren's opinion held that the clause protects against discrimination based on ancestry and national origin wherever a community's own practices (segregated schools, separate toilets, exclusion from jury service) have established that a group is treated as a distinct class. Community prejudices "are not static, and the law is not bound to fight the last generation's battles."

Washington v Davis (1976) clarified that equal protection requires discriminatory purpose, not merely disparate impact. A facially neutral law producing racially unequal outcomes does not trigger heightened scrutiny absent evidence that the legislature adopted it with discriminatory intent. Disparate impact alone -- however severe -- satisfies the Equal Protection Clause.

Loving v Virginia (1967) struck Virginia's anti-miscegenation statute on dual grounds: racial classifications must survive "the most rigid scrutiny" under the Equal Protection Clause, and the freedom to marry is a fundamental right whose denial violates the Due Process Clause. The trial court's rationale -- that "Almighty God created the races... and he placed them on separate continents" and therefore did not intend them to mix -- exemplified exactly the kind of justification that could not survive scrutiny.

The affirmative action arc runs from Bakke (1978) through SFFA (2023). Regents of the University of California v Bakke (1978) struck racial quotas as unconstitutional but produced a Powell controlling opinion (no other Justice joined) endorsing diversity in higher education as a compelling governmental interest permitting holistic consideration of race as one factor among many. Grutter v Bollinger (2003) sustained race-conscious holistic admissions under strict scrutiny -- finding diversity a compelling interest -- but included an expectation that race consciousness would no longer be necessary within 25 years. Gratz v Bollinger (2003), decided the same day, struck an undergraduate point-based racial bonus of 20 points as insufficiently individualized and therefore not narrowly tailored. Students for Fair Admissions v Harvard (2023) effectively ended race-conscious admissions: Roberts's majority held that the programs at Harvard and UNC failed strict scrutiny because their stated interests were too amorphous to be measurable, race unavoidably operated as a negative in zero-sum admissions, the programs relied on racial stereotyping, and there was no logical endpoint. Thomas's concurrence offered an originalist defense of the colorblind Constitution, arguing that the Fourteenth Amendment was designed from the start to eradicate all race-based legal distinctions regardless of direction.

### Sex Discrimination: From Divine Ordinance to Exceedingly Persuasive Justification

Bradwell v Illinois (1873) was the first sex discrimination case to reach the Court. The majority (Miller) ducked the sex question by treating the right to practice law as not a privilege of national citizenship. Bradley's concurrence is the opinion that matters for doctrine: he wrote that "nature itself" recognized a fundamental difference in the spheres of men and women and that a married woman's domestic role was the "law of the Creator." The language of protection was in practice a language of confinement -- the "pedestal or cage" that later doctrine would have to dismantle.

Reed v Reed (1971) was the first invalidation of a sex-based classification under equal protection. Idaho's mandatory preference for men over women as estate administrators fell because sex bore no rational relationship to estate-administration competence. Burger's unanimous opinion applied what looked like rational basis but reached a result that almost nothing reaches under that standard -- an early form of rational basis with bite rather than formal intermediate scrutiny.

Frontiero v Richardson (1973) produced four votes to treat sex as a suspect classification requiring strict scrutiny, but Justice Powell concurred separately. With the Equal Rights Amendment pending before the states, he thought it premature for the Court to resolve the constitutional question while the American people were deliberating through the amendment process. The ERA failed to ratify. Strict scrutiny for sex never arrived.

Craig v Boren (1976) established intermediate scrutiny for sex classifications. Oklahoma allowed females to buy 3.2% beer at 18 but required males to wait until 21. The case is notable for Ruth Bader Ginsburg's strategic choice of male plaintiffs -- framing sex discrimination as a system of role-assignment harmful to everyone, not only to women seeking entry into male-dominated spheres. Brennan announced the standard: classifications by gender must serve important governmental objectives and must be substantially related to achievement of those objectives. Oklahoma's statistical justification (2% of males versus 0.18% of females arrested for drunk driving) failed the substantially-related requirement.

United States v Virginia (1996) tightened intermediate scrutiny. Ginsburg's majority held that parties defending sex-based government action must demonstrate an "exceedingly persuasive justification" -- one that is genuine rather than hypothesized after litigation begins, that cannot rest on post-hoc rationalization, and that must not rely on overbroad generalizations about the different talents, capacities, or preferences of males and females. Virginia Military Institute's 157-year all-male tradition, defended with generalizations about the different educational needs of men and women, failed this standard.

[[Cases/Geduldig v Aiello (1974)|Geduldig v Aiello (1974)]] carved out a constitutional anomaly: pregnancy exclusions from disability insurance are not sex discrimination, because the classification runs between "pregnant women" and "nonpregnant persons" -- the second group includes men. Congress rejected this reasoning in the Pregnancy Discrimination Act (1978), but the constitutional holding stands.

San Antonio Independent School District v Rodriguez (1973) closed off equal protection as a path to remedying structural economic inequality: wealth is not a suspect class and education is not a fundamental right, so rational basis applies.

### Disability and Animus

City of Cleburne v Cleburne Living Center (1985) declined to elevate disability to quasi-suspect status even though persons with intellectual disabilities present as a Footnote Four textbook case -- small, identifiable, historically disadvantaged, with limited political power. Nevertheless, the ordinance fell because the city's stated justifications (neighbor hostility, flood-plain concerns, student harassment) were indistinguishable from the justifications one could offer for other permitted uses like fraternity houses and nursing homes. The law rested on "an irrational prejudice against the mentally retarded," and irrational prejudice is not a legitimate governmental interest even at the rational basis level.

Romer v Evans (1996) struck Colorado's Amendment 2, which barred any governmental entity from protecting persons based on sexual orientation. Kennedy's majority applied rational basis review and found that Amendment 2 imposed a "broad and undifferentiated disability" on one class of citizens -- precluding them from seeking protective legislation at any level of government -- without any legitimate justification other than a bare desire to harm a politically unpopular group. Animus, standing alone, is not a legitimate governmental interest.

### Pregnancy Classifications: Constitutional and Statutory Trajectory

The Court has kept pregnancy outside the sex-classification tier as a matter of constitutional law while Congress repeatedly intervenes on the statutory side. The resulting dual track is the reason pregnancy cases sit uneasily between equal protection doctrine and employment-discrimination statutes.

| Event | Year | Forum | Move | Effect on Pregnancy-as-Sex Question |
|-------|------|-------|------|-------------------------------------|
| [[Cases/Geduldig v Aiello (1974)\|Geduldig v Aiello]] | 1974 | SCOTUS (EP) | Pregnancy exclusion from state disability insurance framed as "pregnant / nonpregnant persons"; rational basis | Establishes the constitutional boundary: pregnancy is not sex as such unless shown to be a pretext for sex discrimination |
| General Electric Co v Gilbert | 1976 | SCOTUS (Title VII) | Extended Geduldig's reasoning to Title VII; pregnancy exclusion not sex discrimination under the Act | Doubled down on Geduldig in the statutory context, prompting congressional override (no brief yet in vault) |
| Pregnancy Discrimination Act | 1978 | Congress | Amended Title VII to define sex discrimination as including discrimination "on the basis of pregnancy, childbirth, or related medical conditions" | Statutory override of Geduldig and Gilbert for Title VII; does not disturb the constitutional holding |
| [[Cases/Bray v Alexandria Womens Health Clinic (1993)\|Bray v Alexandria Women's Health Clinic]] | 1993 | SCOTUS (§ 1985(3)) | Reaffirmed Geduldig's pregnant-versus-nonpregnant framing in the civil-rights-conspiracy context | Confirms that Geduldig's constitutional framing persists outside the EP doctrine proper |
| [[Cases/Dobbs v Jackson Women's Health Organization (2022)\|Dobbs v Jackson Women's Health Organization]] | 2022 | SCOTUS (DP) | Overruled Roe; in dicta invoked Geduldig for the proposition that abortion regulation is not sex-based on its face | Extends the life of Geduldig's framing into the post-Roe landscape; critics read Dobbs as ratifying the doctrinal anomaly |

**The underlying tension.** [[Cases/Geduldig v Aiello (1974)|Geduldig]] and Brennan's dissent mark the two poles of a question the Court has not resolved: whether classifications that burden only one sex biologically are sex classifications at all. The majority's formalism (pregnant-versus-nonpregnant) has survived because the Court has never needed to overrule it; Brennan's view (the classification burdens only women and therefore is sex-based under Reed and Frontiero) has survived as a dissenting tradition later marshaled in feminist-legal-theory critiques of [[Cases/Craig v Boren (1976)|Craig]] and VMI.

### Identifiable-Class Equal Protection (Beyond the White-Black Axis)

[[Cases/Hernandez v Texas (1954)|Hernandez v Texas]] rejected the "two-class theory" that had implicitly confined Fourteenth Amendment protection to discrimination between white and Black persons. The line of cases below traces the evolution of that universalist reading from Reconstruction-era jury-exclusion cases to modern extensions.

| Case | Year | Class Protected | Doctrinal Move | Relationship to Hernandez |
|------|------|-----------------|----------------|---------------------------|
| Strauder v West Virginia | 1880 | Black men excluded from jury service | Fourteenth Amendment prohibits racial exclusion from juries; established the core template | Foundational precedent Hernandez extends beyond race (no brief yet in vault) |
| Yick Wo v Hopkins | 1886 | Chinese launderers in San Francisco | Facially neutral ordinance applied with discriminatory purpose against a national-origin group violates EP | Early universalist reading of "person"; prefigures Hernandez's identifiable-class framework (no brief yet in vault) |
| Norris v Alabama | 1935 | Black jurors in Alabama | Established the "rule of exclusion": prima facie case from proof of substantial population + qualified members + long absence from juries | Evidentiary engine Hernandez imports and applies to Mexican-American exclusion (no brief yet in vault) |
| Cassell v Texas | 1950 | Black grand jurors in Dallas County | Tokenism does not defeat a prima facie rule-of-exclusion showing | Immediate predecessor; Hernandez decided four years later on the same framework (no brief yet in vault) |
| [[Cases/Hernandez v Texas (1954)\|Hernandez v Texas]] | 1954 | Mexican-Americans in Jackson County, Texas | Fourteenth Amendment protects any "distinct class" shown by community treatment; rejects two-class theory explicitly | The pivot case: universalizes the jury-exclusion framework beyond racial categories |
| [[Cases/Brown v Board of Education (1954)\|Brown v Board of Education]] | 1954 | Black schoolchildren | Separate educational facilities are inherently unequal | Decided two weeks after Hernandez by the same unanimous Warren Court; together the two decisions establish the scope of EP in the Warren era |
| Castaneda v Partida | 1977 | Mexican-Americans on Texas grand juries | Formalized the statistical-disparity test for systematic exclusion | Directly descends from Hernandez-Norris; makes rule of exclusion a structured doctrine (no brief yet in vault) |

**What the lineage establishes.** The universalist reading of the Fourteenth Amendment is older than Hernandez (Yick Wo, Strauder) and broader than any single classification. Hernandez's contribution is the formal statement that the Amendment's reach is not confined to Black/white and that any class the community treats as distinct may invoke the Clause. That rule has proved durable in national-origin, alienage, and undocumented-status contexts, but it has not been extended to suspect-class status for any group beyond race, national origin, and alienage.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side

1. **Hypo:** A state law requires that all public-school students be assigned to schools on the basis of their race, intending to maintain proportional racial balance in each building. **Why:** The law is a facial racial classification and triggers strict scrutiny under Adarand and SFFA. Maintaining a proportional racial balance is not a compelling governmental interest -- SFFA explicitly held that race cannot be used to achieve racial balance -- and race-based student assignment is not narrowly tailored. The law fails.

2. **Hypo:** A federal agency requires that only male agents be assigned to combat-zone embassy postings, relying on a legislative history noting that female agents have historically not been posted abroad. **Why:** Sex classification by the federal government triggers intermediate scrutiny (Bolling applies equal protection to the federal government through the Fifth Amendment). Post-hoc generalization about past assignments cannot constitute an "exceedingly persuasive justification" under VMI. The government cannot defend a sex classification with a rationale manufactured or relied on after the classification was challenged.

### Opposite-Side

3. **Hypo:** A state imposes higher auto-registration fees on vehicles more than 15 years old, reasoning that older vehicles are more likely to fail emissions standards. **Why:** Age of a vehicle is not a suspect or quasi-suspect classification; rational basis applies. The legislature's reasoning (older vehicles, higher environmental risk) is rationally related to the legitimate interest in reducing emissions. The challenger bears the burden, and rational basis is easily satisfied. The law survives.

4. **Hypo:** A city requires voters to provide photo identification to vote in local elections, citing concerns about fraud. **Why:** Absent evidence that the photo-ID requirement targets a racial group with discriminatory intent (Washington v Davis), the law is reviewed as a neutral voting regulation. No suspect classification is on the face of the law; the equal protection challenge alone (without a fundamental-rights analysis) requires showing discriminatory purpose, not merely disparate impact.

### Fence-Sitter

5. **Hypo:** A state university adopts an admissions policy that gives application "process points" to first-generation college students, veterans, and recipients of federal Pell Grants -- knowing that these factors correlate positively with racial and socioeconomic diversity. The university does not mention race on the application form. **Why:** This is the question SFFA left open. Roberts's majority carved out room for programs that consider "an applicant's own discussion of how race affected that applicant's life" tied to individual character. A facially race-neutral policy with a disparate impact in favor of underrepresented groups does not trigger strict scrutiny under Washington v Davis (absent discriminatory purpose against another group). But if the design is a transparent proxy for race, opponents will argue the policy is facially neutral but functionally identical to a racial preference -- and the Court's appetite for that argument after SFFA is unknown.

## How to Spot on an Exam

**Trigger facts:** any classification based on race, sex, national origin, alienage, age, disability, or wealth; government denial of a benefit to one group that another group receives; a law that produces unequal outcomes for different groups; claims of "discrimination" or "unequal treatment."

**Analytical framework:**

1. Identify the classification. Is the law facially discriminatory (classifying expressly by race, sex, etc.) or facially neutral with disparate impact? If facially neutral, equal protection requires a showing of discriminatory purpose (Washington v Davis), not just impact.

2. Assign the tier. Race, national origin, alienage: strict scrutiny (symmetrical). Sex, legitimacy: intermediate scrutiny (exceedingly persuasive justification). Everything else: rational basis. Check for animus (Cleburne, Romer) -- animus disqualifies the law even at rational basis.

3. Apply the standard. Strict: compelling interest + narrowly tailored; government bears burden; rarely survived. Intermediate: important interest + substantially related; genuine, not post-hoc; government bears burden. Rational basis: legitimate interest + rational relation; challenger bears burden; almost always survived unless animus present.

4. Check special issues: federal action (Bolling reverse-incorporation via Fifth Amendment Due Process); pregnancy ([[Cases/Geduldig v Aiello (1974)|Geduldig]] rational basis, not sex discrimination); wealth or education (Rodriguez rational basis); affirmative action (SFFA bars race-conscious admissions programs; race-neutral proxies remain contested).

The Equal Protection Theory lecture's central diagnostic claim bears repeating: tier assignment drives outcomes. All the real judicial work migrates to the threshold classification question. On an exam, determine the tier first -- the answer almost always follows from that assignment.

## Critique

Four theories compete to explain equal protection doctrine, and each illuminates a different slice of it while failing to account for the whole.

The **anti-subordination** view holds that the clause targets classifications that create or maintain social hierarchy -- the Slaughter-House "one pervading purpose." It best explains why racial discrimination against minorities is worse than "reverse discrimination" and it is historically grounded. But it cannot explain SFFA (2023), which applied strict scrutiny to programs that benefit racial minorities, or Adarand's symmetry principle. Its reliance on judicial sociology about which groups are subordinated imports contestable value judgments into constitutional law.

The **anti-classification** view holds that certain traits -- race, sex, national origin -- are so rarely legitimate bases for law that their use is presumptively suspect, regardless of direction. Roberts's SFFA majority and Thomas's concurrence embody this view. It produces symmetry and predictability, but the political-process paradox is explicit: anti-classification provides its strongest protection to the politically powerful (men, whites) who least need judicial protection from democratic outcomes.

**Process theory** (Footnote Four) provides the most structural account: heightened scrutiny is warranted where the political process cannot be trusted to protect the minority. But Craig v Boren is its refutation: young men buying beer are the paradigmatic political insiders, not a discrete and insular minority; yet they receive intermediate scrutiny. Anti-stereotyping -- not process failure -- is the engine of sex discrimination doctrine.

**Displaced Lochnerism** is the most candid critique: the tier system does not eliminate judicial value judgments about which laws are probably bad ideas -- it relocates them from the individual statute to the category. The comparison of race and age illustrates this: both characteristics are immutable and often irrelevant to legislative purposes, yet one receives strict scrutiny and the other rational basis. The Court has made a fundamental value judgment at the tier-assignment stage -- racial classifications are almost certainly bad ideas, age classifications are usually fine -- that is a form of Lochnerism wholesale rather than retail, and it remains vulnerable to the same critique the Court deployed against Lochner in 1937.
