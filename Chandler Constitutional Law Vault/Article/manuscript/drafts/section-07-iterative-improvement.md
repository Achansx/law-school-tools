# Section VII. Iterative Improvement Under Professorial Control

## 7.1 The Karpathy loop, domesticated

On March 7, 2026, Andrej Karpathy released `autoresearch`, a Python script that delegates the modify, run, evaluate, keep-or-discard loop to a language-model agent and that ran fifty experiments overnight to surface a learning-rate improvement.[^1] The structural insight the script makes legible is four-piece: a per-phase rubric that scores each pass on criteria the phase owns, a lessons file that carries durable memory between passes, a rolling scorecard that records what each pass produced, and a forced-finding adversarial pass that has to return findings rather than confirm everything looks fine. The vault adopts the architecture and not the autonomous-agent setting that runs it. Karpathy’s loop runs unsupervised against a measurable scientific objective; the vault’s loop runs against a non-measurable pedagogical objective, with the professor available to gate, defer, redirect, or reverse any phase, and with reviewer comments preferred over the rotation’s own next-in-queue work. The structural insight imports because it makes generation reviewable; the autonomous-agent execution does not, because the loop serves professorial judgment rather than replacing it.

## 7.2 One phase per tick: the rotation as the loop

The vault’s maintenance rotation runs through six phases (Ingest, Lint, Enrich, Expand, Synthesize, Verify) and then repeats.[^2] Each scheduled-task tick selects one active phase from that sequence, executes the procedure for that phase once, writes the resulting state, and advances the next-phase pointer for the following tick. An autonomous Karpathy-style loop touches every phase in a single overnight pass; the vault commits one phase per tick and lets the rotation itself be the loop. The choice keeps each pass small enough for the professor to inspect on its own terms, lets each phase’s quality differ instead of being averaged into a single per-pass number, and gives each phase a separate scope-selection step that can prefer professorial input over the rotation’s own next-in-queue work.

## 7.3 The forced-finding Verify pass: catching what other phases let pass

The Verify phase runs three hostile personas (Staleness Auditor, Contradiction Hunter, Template Enforcer); each must return at least one concrete finding per run, and a persona that genuinely finds nothing records *persona-produced-nothing* as its own finding so the absence of findings is auditable.[^3] A lead-rotation table and a per-persona focus-mutation table both keyed off the Verify run count reshape the lead-and-focus pairing every pass. The forced-finding rule converts Verify from a phase that confirms things look fine into a phase that catches what prior phases missed.

The *Prize Cases* (1863) is the canonical iteration story, and it rewards telling slowly, because it is the loop working under pressure rather than in theory.[^4] The Ingest phase pulled a Justice Grier quotation from the professor’s modernized teaching PDF, a copy that had quietly smoothed the opinion’s archaic phrasing into contemporary words. The quotation read clean on its face, so Ingest carried it into the War Powers brief. When a later pass asked Midpage’s `findInOpinion` to confirm the language, the keyword search returned zero matches. A zero should have stopped the line; instead it was logged as an OCR artifact and left alone, and the quotation propagated from the brief onto the War Powers topic page. The unsettling part is what came next. Lint, two separate Enrich passes, and Expand each read the same drifted source, and each reproduced the wrong words faithfully, because none of them re-checked the quotation against the indexed opinion. The error survived five phases not through carelessness but because every phase was consulting the same corrupted copy. The break came when a Verify persona, operating under a standing instruction not to accept a zero result as an answer, abandoned keyword search and called `analyzeOpinion`, which reads the opinion text rather than matching a string. Midpage returned Grier’s actual language. The vault had written “never formally declared…by its character”; Grier wrote “never solemnly declared…by its accidents.” The correction was applied to the brief and then grep-driven back across the vault to catch the stale copy on the topic page, and the loop encoded a durable rule: verify a quotation against the indexed opinion, never against a paraphrase or a modernized PDF.

A second story shows the loop catching a different kind of error. The Judicial Votes section of the *Youngstown* brief first reported that Frankfurter, Douglas, Jackson, and Burton all “joined” Black’s majority, a defensible shorthand but a misleading one for a student, because the six-justice majority produced six separate opinions: two justices joined the opinion and also wrote separately, and three concurred in the judgment only.[^5] The brief sat partially wrong through several cycles, because no earlier phase was looking for the mischaracterization, until the Contradiction Hunter persona, on a pass focused on concurrence-versus-majority errors, ran an `analyzeOpinion` re-check of Frankfurter’s language and produced the rewrite. The pair broadens the loop’s demonstrated coverage from quotation-text errors to doctrinal-characterization errors. The forced-finding architecture instantiates the AI-in-higher-education field’s call for increased ethics, collaboration, and rigour; the meta-review naming that call surveys chatbot-tutor and adaptive-system interventions rather than reviewed static publications, but the field-level evidentiary standard it sets applies here too.[^11]

## 7.4 Loop memory: LESSONS as durable record, scorecard and narrative as audit trail

The vault’s `LESSONS.md` is the loop’s running memory.[^6] Every run that catches an error or notices a pattern appends a fixed-schema entry (Rule, Why, How to apply, an optional dated Seen footer). The file is capped at thirty-five entries, and an out-of-rotation Consolidate sweep merges duplicates and prunes superseded entries so the cap stays honest rather than aspirational. Loading is tiered: Ingest, Enrich, Expand, and Synthesize read the file because they generate content; Lint and Verify skip it because the corrections that govern structural checks differ from the corrections that govern content. The lessons file is what answers a reader who asks whether the loop is improving or just running.

The token-problem story shows the loop reshaping its own architecture rather than catching a content error.[^7] Early runs loaded one large `RUBRIC.md` covering all six phases, the full `LESSONS.md`, and the entire build narrative every pass, so a Lint run that only cared about structural formatting was burning context on Enrich and Verify criteria it would never use. The fix was tiered loading (per-phase rubric files, LESSONS skipped by Lint and Verify, build narrative rotated monthly, no-op phases stopped being scored); a secondary fix fell out of the same change, because the shared rubric had let one phase game a criterion in a way that made its own score look good and another phase’s job harder. The per-phase-rubric-only principle is the article’s own L-007.

The run-by-run scorecard at `.run-scores.jsonl` and the rolling `BUILD_NARRATIVE_YYYY-MM.md` are the loop’s audit trail.[^8] The scorecard holds one JSON line per run containing the active phase, the weighted criteria scores, the red-flag list if any, and the timestamp. The narrative holds a two-to-four-sentence prose entry per run describing what the phase did and what was learned. The two artifacts together let a reviewer reconstruct the loop’s state at any past moment: the scorecard for measurable phase quality, the narrative for the structural context the score alone cannot carry.

## 7.5 Professorial control: the page-feedback intake and the staging triplet

The vault’s answer to professorial control is the page-feedback intake at Step 0 of every scheduled-task run.[^9] Before the phase dispatcher selects the active phase, Step 0 pulls inbound comments from the deployed-site page-feedback form, triages each submission into one of six routes (trivial fix, structural, analytical, cross-reference, topic-level, verification), and logs a typed *professor-feedback* pending issue routed to the phase that owns the fix. Trivial fixes land on the same run; structural and substantive feedback waits for the phase that owns the fix to rotate around, and that phase’s scope-selection step must prefer pages carrying open *professor-feedback* issues routed to it. The intake is the architectural feature that distinguishes the vault’s loop from a Karpathy-style autonomous loop. What the architecture establishes is that professorial review stays possible and inspectable at every phase; it does not, on its own, record how often the professor intervened or what the professor changed, a limit Section XII addresses directly. The staging triplet is its observable counterpart: *Trump v. United States* sits at the skeleton stage, with the factual sections filled and the rest marked by `<!-- ENRICH: -->` stubs; the Rehnquist Court Commerce Clause lecture covering *Lopez* and *Morrison* is mid-cycle, mostly clean with a handful of stubs marking places to go deeper; *Gonzales v. Raich* is a case after a full Enrich cycle, with majority analysis, Scalia’s concurrence, O’Connor’s and Thomas’s dissents, five hypotheticals, critique from both directions, and every Key Quotation pin-cited against Midpage.[^10] The system did not replace professorial judgment; it made that judgment reusable, inspectable, and publishable.
