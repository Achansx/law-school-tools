# XIII. Institutional Implications

## A. The open-educational-resource landscape and complementary positioning

Legal education already supports open educational resources. Harvard Library and the MIT Press publish H2O Open Casebooks, and CALI’s eLangdell Press distributes openly licensed casebooks and course materials.[CITE: Harvard Library & MIT Press, H2O Open Casebooks, https://opencasebook.org; CALI eLangdell Press, https://www.cali.org/the-elangdell-bookstore — workplan §XIII; dedicated evidence-13-oer-positioning owed per PI-091; URL liveness deferred to Cite per L-019] A course knowledge system is complementary to that infrastructure rather than a competing channel: H2O and eLangdell publish the texts, while this method publishes the structure around the texts, namely the typed schema, the doctrinal map, the verified-source links, and the navigable course graph. A school that adopts the method adds a structural layer the existing projects do not provide rather than displacing them. What carries across is the open-resource commitment to reusable, openly licensed teaching materials; what does not carry across is the object of publication, because H2O and eLangdell publish finished casebook texts under license, while a course knowledge system publishes structure and links over a professor’s own slides, public opinions, and original hypotheticals.

## B. Who builds and who sustains: faculty labor and institutional roles

The method’s labor is professor-led but institutionally supported, and naming that split is the institutional implication. The cost-and-labor accounting shows that the build is tractable for a single professor working with a scheduled assistant and that deployment runs on near-zero-cost static hosting; the figures live in Section IX rather than here.[CITE: see supra Section IX (Cost and Labor: The Honest Accounting)] Sustaining the work across a faculty and across course offerings is a different matter, because it implicates roles the individual professor cannot carry alone: the library as steward of an open-resource catalog, instructional design as a partner in the schema and its pedagogy, and information technology as host and version-control custodian of the versioned build pipeline.[CITE: see supra Section VIII (From Vault to Website)] A course knowledge system is therefore best read as faculty scholarship of teaching coupled with institutional stewardship, not as an unfunded individual burden.

## C. Governance, control, and recognition

At institutional scale the professor’s role as the gating actor becomes an explicit governance question: who reviews each page, who maintains the vault, and who is accountable for the public artifact.[CITE: see supra Section VII (Iterative Improvement Under Professorial Control)] The reviewed-static-website architecture is what makes the governance posture inspectable, because every page ships from a versioned commit and is accountable to a named professor who reviewed it before publication, so the chain of responsibility is auditable in a way a runtime system’s generated responses are not.[CITE: see supra Section XII (Risks and Limits)] The recognition question follows: a versioned, citable, publicly served course artifact is a scholarly output, which raises the question of whether an institution counts it as teaching infrastructure or as scholarship for review and promotion. The article names that question without resolving it, because the inspectable, versioned artifact is precisely what makes either form of recognition assessable: a reviewer can read what was published and trace who reviewed it.

## D. What the institution must decide

Adoption is opt-in and method-first. An institution adopts the method, the typed schema, the verified-source discipline, the professorial gating, and the phase rotation, rather than the constitutional-law artifact, consistent with the claim that the structure transfers while its constitutional-law content does not.[CITE: see supra Section X (Generalization Beyond Constitutional Law)] The institutional implication is a modest, replicable infrastructure investment offered as an invitation rather than a mandate. What an institution must decide is whether to extend that invitation, a choice the conclusion takes up.[CITE: see infra Section XIV (Conclusion)]
