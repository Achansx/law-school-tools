# Section XI. Evaluation: What Would Count as Success

Spine for the draft phase. Each H2 has a word budget; subtotal is 900. Each bullet states the claim, names the evidence card, and signals the inferential move it carries for the rest of the article. Section XI sits at drafting order 8, after the IV-V-VIII concrete trio, Section III's user-experience walkthrough, Sections VI-VII on prompting and iteration, Section IX's cost-and-labor accounting, and Section II's higher-altitude framing have all reached draft or beyond. The section's job is to state what the article would count as success and what it explicitly does not claim, then describe the modest, honest light-data plan the workplan §5 recommends and the field-level frame (Bond et al. 2024) plus the JLE-house precedent (Choi & Schwarcz 2025) that the posture explicitly anticipates. Section XII inherits the risk side; Section XI carries the evaluation side. Per L-002 and L-026 the recurring sentence does not land in Section XI even though Section XI's what-counts-as-success question is the closest paraphrase of the recurring-sentence claim of any section after Section II, and the paraphrase trap here is the highest in the article because the section explicitly asks the question the sentence answers. Per L-003 and L-031 the voice-unity sweep keeps Section XI's evaluation vocabulary aligned with what the article's actual artifact is, a reviewed static website, rather than drifting into chatbot-tutor evaluation framings the cited literature is built on. Per L-024 every external citation lands a transfer-of-inference clause in body prose naming what generalizes from the cited intervention to the article's setting and what does not. Per L-027 every cost or throughput figure routes to Section IX; Netlify analytics, Likert frequencies, and think-aloud session counts are Section XI's own evaluation data and stay here. Per L-040 the live-pedagogy-versus-static-artifact slip check stays in scope across all five subsections because the section's vocabulary is the easiest place in the article to slip into a substitution claim.

## 11.A The disclaimer that organizes the section (180 words)

- Claim: the article does not claim that students learned more from the case study, that the case study produced a treatment effect on student performance, or that the system caused any measurable change in exam scores; the article's thesis is a method contribution, and Section XI's first move is to state the disclaimer explicitly as the load-bearing claim the rest of the section builds on. Evidence: the PROJECT_PRIMER thesis paragraph paired with the workplan §1 not-a-claim-students-learned-more bullet, both of which carry the disclaimer at the article level and make Section XI the section in which the disclaimer becomes operational. Inferential move: the explicit disclaimer is the rhetorical anchor that gives the Section XII risk argument its integrity and that lets Section XI offer modest usability data without that data being read as a learning-outcomes claim by a skeptical reader; without the explicit disclaimer at the section opening, every Bond et al. methodological objection becomes a fatal objection rather than an anticipated one, and the four-instrument plan at 11.B reads as ambition rather than as honest scope. [evidence: evidence-11-not-a-claim-students-learned-more]
- Claim: Section XI's positive claim is the workplan §5 method-plus-light-usability-data recommendation: a method contribution paired with modest, honest evidence proportional to the claim, neither method-only (which is publishable but markedly weaker) nor a full pilot study (which is a separate paper that would either underdeliver as research or overshadow the method). Evidence: the workplan §5 recommendation paragraph as the section's load-bearing posture card, with the three workplan reasons (JLE-audience-expects-evidence-proportional-to-claims, structured-pilot-is-expensive-and-distorting, light-data-is-cheap-and-credible) named as the section's structural backbone. Inferential move: 11.A states the disclaimer and the affirmative posture together so that 11.B can describe the instruments operationally and 11.C can land the field-level frame without re-litigating the posture decision; the workplan reasons are not enumerated as a list in body prose but condensed into a single sentence so the section opens on argumentative thrust rather than on a bulleted policy memo. [evidence: evidence-11-workplan-recommendation-method-plus-light-data]

## 11.B The four-instrument light-data plan (240 words)

- Claim: the light-data plan operationalizes the workplan §5 posture through four instruments, each measuring use patterns or self-report on the reviewed static artifact and none measuring learning outcomes. The first instrument is a short anonymous end-of-semester Qualtrics or Google Forms survey to the current Constitutional Law I cohort, with five Likert items on perceived usefulness, navigation, trust, and overreliance, plus two open-ended items. Evidence: the four-instrument-plan card carries the survey description from the workplan §5 practical-version paragraph. Inferential move: Section XI describes the Likert survey as a perceived-overreliance and perceived-usefulness measure on a reviewed static artifact, not as a self-reported learning measure, which keeps the L-024 transfer-of-inference framing clean for any later citation of survey-based AI-in-education work. [evidence: evidence-11-light-data-plan-instruments]
- Claim: the second instrument is Netlify analytics for page-view counts by page type (Case versus Topic versus Lecture), search-term distribution, and time on page, all anonymized. Evidence: the same workplan §5 paragraph carries the analytics description. Inferential move: Section XI names the analytics as a navigation-pattern measure on the deployed site that produces real numbers the section can report at draft time if data collection has run, and the section's prose hedges the data-collection status as an open question rather than asserting numbers that may not exist yet. [evidence: evidence-11-light-data-plan-instruments]
- Claim: the third instrument is three to five thirty-minute think-aloud sessions with volunteer students preparing for the exam, in which a student is asked to find an answer to a doctrinal question using the site and the session records what the student clicks and verbalizes. Evidence: the same card's think-aloud subsection. Inferential move: the think-aloud is a use-strategy measure rather than a comprehension measure, and 11.B names what the sessions would observe (click sequences, search terms, perceived navigation choices, verbalized confusion points) rather than what they would prove. [evidence: evidence-11-light-data-plan-instruments]
- Claim: the fourth element of the plan is the methodological-disciplinary commitment that the section frames the entire data slice as usability and use-pattern data, never as learning-outcomes data; the fourth element is not a fifth instrument but the commitment that organizes the other three. Evidence: the workplan §5 line 160 framing directive. Inferential move: the disciplinary commitment is the section's anchor against the substitution slip in which a navigation measure or a Likert-perceived-overreliance score gets read as a comprehension or performance measure; per L-003 and L-031 Section XI's evaluation vocabulary stays anchored on navigation, page-view distribution, search-term frequency, time on page, perceived usefulness, perceived trust, perceived overreliance, click sequences, and verbalized use strategies, and never imports response-quality, conversational-coherence, tutor-accuracy, engagement-turns, or dialogue-state-tracking from the chatbot-tutor evaluation literature. [evidence: evidence-11-static-not-chatbot-evaluation-framing]

Evidence: the Bond meta-review citation card with the L-019 deferral noted, the L-022 WebSearch-first resolution path named for the next Cite tick, the L-043 transcription-flag noted against the workplan-transcribed DOI prefix that does not obviously match the 2024 publication year, and the L-024 transfer-of-inference clause requirement named (the abstract field-level call for proportional rigor generalizes; the chatbot-tutor and LMS-integration implementation surface predominantly covered by Bond's corpus does not).
