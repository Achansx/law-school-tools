## 13.A The OER landscape and complementary positioning (140 words)

- Claim: legal education already has open-educational-resource infrastructure that publishes open texts: H2O Open Casebooks (Harvard Library and the MIT Press) and CALI's eLangdell Press both distribute openly licensed casebooks and course materials, and the institutional implication is that a course knowledge system is complementary OER infrastructure rather than a competing publication channel: H2O and eLangdell publish the texts, while this method publishes the structure around the texts (the typed schema, the doctrinal map, the verified-source links, the navigable course graph). A school adopting the method is adding a structural layer the existing OER projects do not provide, not displacing them. Evidence: the workplan §XIII bibliography (H2O at opencasebook.org, CALI eLangdell at cali.org/the-elangdell-bookstore); no dedicated card yet, so the Draft tick leans on the workplan entry and PI-091 flags the owed `evidence-13-oer-positioning` card; URL liveness for both OER sites is deferred to the Cite tick per L-019. Inferential move: positioning the method inside the OER movement lets the institutional reader file it as shared infrastructure investment rather than a single professor's experiment, and 13.A hands the "this is infrastructure" frame forward to 13.B's question of who builds and who sustains it. Per L-024 the transfer clause names what generalizes (the OER commitment to openly licensed, reusable course materials) and what does not (H2O and eLangdell publish finished texts under license, while the course knowledge system publishes structure and links over a professor's own slides, public opinions, and original hypotheticals, not third-party casebook text). [evidence: workplan-section-xiii-oer-bibliography]

## 13.B Who builds and who sustains: faculty labor and institutional roles (140 words)

- Claim: the method's labor is professor-led but institutionally supported, and naming that split is the institutional implication. The cost-and-labor accounting (Section IX) shows the build is tractable for a single professor working with a scheduled assistant over a manageable budget, and the deployment is near-zero-cost infrastructure (direct-upload static hosting, no build minutes), but sustaining the work across a faculty and across course offerings implicates institutional roles the individual professor cannot carry alone: the library as steward of an OER-style catalog, instructional design as a partner in the schema and pedagogy, and IT as host and version-control custodian. The institutional implication is that a course knowledge system is best read as faculty scholarship-of-teaching coupled with institutional stewardship, not as an unfunded individual burden. Evidence: the labor-categories card and the zero-build-minutes deployment card establish the qualitative tractability; the build-pipeline card establishes the version-control and hosting surface that the library and IT would steward. Per L-027 the actual dollar figures, person-hour totals, and infrastructure numbers route to Section IX's master cost table and are NOT quoted here; 13.B names the qualitative property (tractable to build, cheap to host, but institutionally distributed to sustain) and cross-references Section IX for the numbers. PI-091 flags the owed `evidence-13-institutional-roles` card. Inferential move: 13.B converts Section IX's cost story into an institutional-adoption story and hands the distributed-stewardship frame forward to 13.C's governance question (if many hands sustain the artifact, who is accountable for what it says). [evidence: evidence-09-labor-categories-workplan]

## 13.C Governance, control, and recognition (130 words)

- Claim: at institutional scale the professor-as-gating-actor (Section VII) becomes an explicit governance question (who reviews each page, who maintains the vault, who is accountable for the public artifact), and the reviewed-static-website architecture (Section XII) is what makes the governance posture inspectable: every page ships from a versioned commit and is accountable to a named professor who reviewed it before publication, so the chain of responsibility is auditable in a way a runtime chatbot's outputs are not. The recognition implication follows: a versioned, citable, publicly served course artifact is a scholarly output, which raises the institutional question of whether a course knowledge system is counted as teaching infrastructure or as scholarship for review and promotion, a question the article names but does not resolve. Evidence: the professorial-control-mechanism card (governance as professorial gating) and the static-vs-chatbot risk-architecture card (the named-professor accountability surface and versioned-commit update mechanism); the live-deployed-site and build-pipeline cards establish the artifact as a versioned, citable public object. Per L-044 the recognition hedge ("counted as infrastructure or as scholarship") is paired with the architectural-implication sentence: the inspectable, versioned static artifact is precisely what makes either form of recognition assessable, because a reviewer can read what was published and trace who reviewed it. PI-091 flags the owed `evidence-13-governance-and-recognition` card. Inferential move: 13.C names the institutional decision (infrastructure versus scholarship) without resolving it and hands the opt-in framing forward to 13.D. [evidence: evidence-07-professorial-control-mechanism]
