The article's risk argument turns on an architectural contrast: a chatbot-tutor produces output at runtime by generating text against a corpus, and the user sees that output without prior professorial review; a reviewed static website produces output at build time by professorial authoring and review against a corpus, and the user sees only content that was inspected before publication. The Magesh et al. finding that leading commercial legal retrieval-augmented-generation systems hallucinate seventeen to thirty-three percent of the time describes the chatbot pipeline's runtime risk surface. A reviewed static website has no runtime generation surface for that hallucination to occur on, because the generation work happened upstream and was professorially reviewed before any page reached a reader.

The contrast is structural, not absolute: the static-website architecture has its own risk surface. The risks live in different parts of the production pipeline, but they exist. Section XII's task is to enumerate them honestly. The drafting risks of the reviewed-static-website architecture include: (1) a professor or author who reviews many pages quickly may miss errors that a more deliberate review would catch, so the review process scales differently from generation; (2) the static artifact, once published, is not adaptive to a specific student's misunderstanding the way a conversational tutor could be in principle, so the static architecture loses an interactivity affordance the chatbot offers; (3) the static artifact's structure tells the reader what to think is doctrinally important, and the structure itself carries pedagogical commitments the professor must own; (4) the publication step bakes errors and stylistic choices into a public artifact, which raises the cost of correction relative to a chatbot whose answer is ephemeral.

The contrast holds at four points that Section XII's prose can render as a structured paragraph or as a literal table: review timing (build-time professorial inspection versus runtime generation), error surface (publication of an inspected artifact versus generation of an uninspected response), update mechanism (a re-deploy under the same review pipeline versus a re-generation under the same uninspected pipeline), and accountability surface (the professor and the author own each published page versus the system itself appearing to own each response). At each of the four points the static architecture concentrates the risk surface in a place the article can describe, audit, and review; the chatbot architecture distributes the risk surface across every user interaction in ways that are difficult to audit and impossible to review exhaustively.
