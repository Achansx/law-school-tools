Elizabeth Mertz's *The Language of Law School: Learning to "Think Like a Lawyer"* (Oxford University Press 2007) is an ethnographic study of first-year Contracts classrooms across multiple law schools. The relevant observation for Section II is that a great deal of what a law professor actually teaches happens in real-time classroom interaction — Socratic exchange, framing moves, hedges, the moment-by-moment shaping of how a case gets read — and never lands in any artifact a student or a successor instructor can return to later. The casebook holds the cases; the syllabus holds the reading order; the LMS holds announcements; the slide deck holds the bullets the professor projected. None of those captures the structural and doctrinal performance that constitutes the actual teaching. Section II deploys Mertz briefly (the workplan's directive: "with restraint") to support the observation that the course knowledge system is one way to make the performed component of teaching reusable, inspectable, and (in the article's framing) publishable.
