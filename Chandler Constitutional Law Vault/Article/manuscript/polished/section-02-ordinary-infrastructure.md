# II. Why Ordinary Course Infrastructure Is Not Enough

## A. The long-range frame: a static method on shifting ground

The casebook has been the spine of doctrinal teaching for more than a century and a half, and any proposal that takes itself seriously has to begin with that fact rather than wish it away. Edward Rubin’s account traces the persistence of the Langdellian method from the 1870s to the present and treats its survival across enormous changes in law, practice, theory, and pedagogy as a central long-range observation about how American law schools teach.[^1] The article that follows does not argue for retiring the casebook, the case method, or the assigned reading list. It argues that the inherited inventory of teaching forms is incomplete: a course knowledge system is the layer the casebook never carried and was never designed to be.

## B. What ordinary course infrastructure produces: an inventory

The starting state of the Constitutional Law I vault, before the synthesis pipeline ran, was a flat folder of more than two hundred sixty files exported from a law-school course management system, with the top level holding sixty-one PowerPoint lecture decks, sixty-six PDFs of case readings, a dozen merged reading packets, several practice exams, and a small set of teaching guides, all organized only by filename, and an Uploaded Media subfolder carrying the bulk of the individual case-opinion PDFs the top-level set indexed.[^2] No structural index, no doctrinal map, no cross-reference between a lecture and the cases it covered, and no synthesis layer above the inventory came with the export. The May 2026 progress snapshot of the case study records one hundred ninety-eight pages of structured prose alongside one hundred fifty-four still-unprocessed source materials, and the coexistence of the two corpora at one snapshot date is the operational fact: turning the inventory into the system is separate work, not a property the inventory has by itself.[^3] Both corpora presuppose Canvas-enrolled students with broadband access and devices capable of rendering the modernized PDFs and the deployed wiki at full fidelity; Section XII takes up the equity and access implications of the architectural choice.

## C. The performed teaching ordinary infrastructure does not capture

Elizabeth Mertz’s ethnographic study of first-year Contracts classrooms records that much of what a professor teaches happens in real-time Socratic exchange, framing moves, hedges, and the moment-by-moment shaping of how a case gets read, and that little of that performance lands in any artifact a student or successor instructor can return to later.[^4] The casebook holds the case the publisher prints; the syllabus assigns the reading; the LMS posts the link; the slide deck quotes the holding. None of those was built to capture the performed component of the teaching, which lives in the live event rather than in the artifacts the live event leaves behind. A second, sharper version of the same gap is verification: when a professor’s modernized PDF silently updated archaic phrasing in a Civil War-era opinion, no piece of the inherited inventory could compare the prose the student read against the indexed opinion that controlled, and Section VII tells that story in full.[^5]

## D. The synthesis and graph affordances ordinary infrastructure does not produce

A third affordance the inherited inventory does not produce is the doctrinal-synthesis view a student actually needs at exam time. The May 2026 stages email describes the Topic page as the synthesis surface of the vault: a page that gathers every case brief and lecture summary touching a doctrine into a single view carrying the governing rule, doctrinal development from the earliest controlling case to the most recent, a Key Cases comparison table, hypotheticals, an exam-spotting framework, and a critique block, with Commerce Clause as the running example.[^6] A casebook organizes the doctrine through chapter sequences and the case method, a syllabus orders that material by week, and an LMS posts the resulting artifacts by date and folder, but none of those forms produces the per-doctrine synthesis view the Topic page is. A fourth gap is the navigational graph. The deployed vault carries an explicit wiki-link grammar in which Topic pages link to their cases and lectures, cases link back to topics and forward to citing cases, and lectures link to the cases and topics they covered. That grammar produces a doctrinal map at the navigation surface, an affordance the casebook, syllabus, LMS, and slide deck were not built to deliver because none of those is a hypertext graph.[^7]

## E. Positioning within the JLE pedagogical-reform line

The course knowledge system extends the casebook, the syllabus, and the live classroom rather than displacing any of them. Jamie R. Abrams’s reframing of the Socratic method belongs to a Journal of Legal Education line of pedagogical-reform pieces that adapt inherited teaching forms instead of rejecting them, and the same move applies here: the inherited form is not the deficiency the proposal is built to remedy, and the contribution is the layer the inherited form was never designed to carry.[^8] The article also engages the AI-in-higher-education research literature, whose most recent meta-review of that field calls for greater ethics, collaboration, and rigour in how such research is conducted and reported.[^9] What carries over from that meta-review is its evidentiary standard, which this article meets by disclaiming learning-outcomes gains and cataloguing risks in Section XII; what does not carry over is the chatbot-tutor and adaptive-system intervention setting it surveys, because the case study here is a reviewed static publication that places no model in front of the student.
