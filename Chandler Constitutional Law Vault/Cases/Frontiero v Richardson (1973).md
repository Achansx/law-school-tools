# Frontiero v Richardson (1973)

## Memory Jogger

> An eight-to-one decision striking down sex-discriminatory military benefit rules, famous for Justice Brennan's plurality argument that sex is a suspect classification requiring strict scrutiny -- a position that drew four votes but never a majority.

---

## Facts

Sharron Frontiero, a lieutenant in the United States Air Force, sought to claim her civilian husband Joseph as a dependent to receive increased housing allowance and medical benefits. Federal statutes automatically granted dependent status to the wives of male servicemembers, but required female servicemembers to prove that their husbands were actually financially dependent (defined as receiving more than one-half of their support from the wife). Sharron could not satisfy the dependency-in-fact requirement because Joseph worked part-time and earned income of his own. The challenged statutes created an explicit sex-based asymmetry: husbands of male servicemembers received benefits automatically; wives of female servicemembers did not. The Government defended the scheme as administratively convenient -- wives were almost always financially dependent on their husbands, so requiring proof was unnecessary; husbands of female servicemembers were rarely dependent, so requiring proof served the system's accuracy interest.

---

## Procedural History

Sharron and Joseph Frontiero sued in federal district court in Alabama, challenging the federal statutes under the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment (which incorporates equal protection principles as applied to the federal government). The district court upheld the statute, finding the sex-based classification rationally related to Congress's interest in administrative economy. The Frontiero appealed directly to the Supreme Court, which reversed 8-1.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Plurality:** Brennan (joined by Douglas, White, Marshall) -- sex is a suspect class requiring strict scrutiny
- **Concurrence (Stewart):** Joins judgment under Reed rational-basis-with-bite; declines to reach suspect-class question
- **Concurrence (Powell, joined by Burger and Blackmun):** Would decide on Reed grounds; would defer declaration of sex as suspect class pending ratification of the Equal Rights Amendment
- **Dissent (Rehnquist):** Sole dissenter; would uphold the statute under rational basis review

---

## Holding

The federal statutes providing different dependency-benefit rules for male and female servicemembers violate the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment. A classification drawn solely on the basis of sex for the purpose of administrative convenience is unconstitutional. Eight Justices agreed on the outcome; only four (the Brennan plurality) agreed that sex is a suspect classification requiring strict scrutiny.

---

## Analysis

### Plurality Opinion

Justice Brennan's plurality, joined by Douglas, White, and Marshall, opened with a sweeping claim: sex classifications, like race, are "inherently suspect" and must be subjected to "strict judicial scrutiny." The plurality marshaled three arguments for this elevation. First, sex is an immutable, highly visible characteristic that bears no necessary relationship to ability or contribution to society -- the same rationale that made race suspect. Second, women have historically faced pervasive discrimination that continues to constrain their opportunities despite formal legal equality. Third, Congress itself seemed to recognize sex as suspect by proposing the Equal Rights Amendment, which was pending ratification at the time.

Applying strict scrutiny, the plurality easily found the statutes unconstitutional. Administrative convenience cannot be a compelling interest -- if it could, "the Government always could evade the strictures of the Constitution simply by administrative fiat." The sex-based distinction was not narrowly tailored to any legitimate end because it penalized women who actually provided their spouses' primary support.

The plurality opinion did not command a majority. Powell's concurrence explicitly declined to join the strict-scrutiny holding, arguing that declaring sex a suspect class should await the ERA's outcome. This left sex-as-suspect-classification with only four votes, a plurality position that was never adopted as the majority rule. Craig v Boren (1976) would instead establish intermediate scrutiny as the governing standard.

## Connections

- [[Cases/Geduldig v Aiello (1974)]]: Brennan's Geduldig dissent builds directly on the Frontiero plurality's suspect-class argument for sex classifications, arguing that pregnancy classifications must be treated as sex classifications under the same heightened tier Brennan urged here. The majority's refusal to treat pregnancy as sex-based confined Frontiero's reach and previewed the Court's eventual settlement on intermediate scrutiny in Craig.
- [[Cases/Craig v Boren (1976)|Craig v Boren (1976)]]: Reciprocal of Craig's Frontiero entry. Craig settles on intermediate scrutiny three years after Frontiero's failed strict-scrutiny plurality; Brennan moves from his Frontiero four-Justice strict-scrutiny coalition to a seven-Justice majority that takes the smaller jurisprudential bite Powell had refused to give him in 1973. The Reed-Frontiero-Craig sequence is the canonical teaching arc for how the Court arrived at the modern sex-classification tier.
- [[Cases/Reed v Reed (1971)|Reed v Reed (1971)]]: Doctrinal predecessor. Reed's "fair and substantial relation" formulation supplied the linguistic seed Frontiero tried to elevate to strict scrutiny and that Craig later canonized as intermediate scrutiny.
- [[Cases/United States v Virginia (1996)|United States v Virginia (1996)]]: Downstream completion of the arc Frontiero opened. Ginsburg's VMI majority tightens Craig's intermediate scrutiny into the "exceedingly persuasive justification" formulation, vindicating the heightened-review impulse that fell short of a majority in Frontiero.
