# Gonzales v Raich (2005)

## Memory Jogger

> Federal power to prohibit homegrown medical marijuana survives Lopez and Morrison because the Controlled Substances Act is a comprehensive regulation of an interstate market in fungible commodities, and Congress may rationally conclude that intrastate personal use aggregated across the country substantially affects that market.

---

## Facts

In 1996, California voters passed Proposition 215, codified as the Compassionate Use Act of 1996, which exempts seriously ill patients and their primary caregivers from state criminal prosecution for possessing or cultivating marijuana for medicinal purposes upon a physician's recommendation. Angel Raich and Diane Monson were California residents suffering from serious medical conditions who used marijuana on their doctors' recommendations. Raich's physician believed that forgoing cannabis would cause her excruciating pain and could prove fatal. Monson grew her own cannabis; Raich obtained it free from two caregivers (John Does 1 and 2) who cultivated it for her.

On August 15, 2002, county deputies and agents from the federal Drug Enforcement Administration came to Monson's home. County officials concluded her use was lawful under California law, but after a three-hour standoff the federal agents seized and destroyed all six of her cannabis plants.

Both women sued the Attorney General and the head of the DEA, seeking injunctive and declaratory relief against enforcement of the federal Controlled Substances Act, 84 Stat. 1242, 21 U.S.C. section 801 et seq., as applied to their wholly intrastate cultivation and use of marijuana. The CSA classifies marijuana as a Schedule I controlled substance and makes its manufacture, distribution, and possession a federal crime except in a manner authorized by the statute.

---

## Procedural History

The District Court denied a preliminary injunction. The Ninth Circuit reversed, finding that respondents had demonstrated a strong likelihood of success on the merits that the CSA was an unconstitutional exercise of Commerce Clause authority as applied to the intrastate, noncommercial cultivation and possession of marijuana for personal medical use. The Ninth Circuit relied heavily on United States v Lopez (1995) and United States v Morrison (2000) to conclude that the activity at issue was noneconomic and fell outside Congress's reach. The Supreme Court granted certiorari and reversed, 6-3.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority:** Stevens (joined by Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer) — 6-3

- **Dissent:** O'Connor (joined by Rehnquist and Thomas in part); Thomas

---

## Holding

Congress's Commerce Clause power, together with the Necessary and Proper Clause, authorizes the federal prohibition on the intrastate cultivation and possession of marijuana for personal medical use under the Controlled Substances Act. Where Congress enacts a comprehensive regulatory scheme reaching an interstate market in a fungible commodity, courts lack power to excise individual intrastate applications of that scheme, even if those applications are purely local and noncommercial, so long as Congress had a rational basis for concluding that failure to regulate the class of activity would undercut the regulation of the interstate market.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Justice Stevens wrote for the Court. The CSA is a comprehensive statute creating a closed regulatory system over five classes of controlled substances. Congress classified marijuana as Schedule I based on its judgment that the drug has a high potential for abuse and no accepted medical use. Respondents conceded the facial validity of the CSA and challenged only its application to their intrastate, noncommercial activity. That framing, Stevens explained, is fatal. When Congress regulates a class of activities within the reach of the commerce power, courts have no power to excise individual instances as trivial.

The decisive precedent is Wickard v Filburn (1942). Roscoe Filburn's wheat, grown for home consumption, was not itself commerce; yet Congress could regulate it because home-grown wheat aggregated across the country would substantially affect the interstate market Congress was attempting to manage. Raich and Monson are in the same posture. Marijuana is a fungible commodity for which a substantial, lucrative, and admittedly illegal interstate market exists. A nationwide exemption for locally cultivated marijuana, even for medicinal use, could plausibly swell supply, leak into recreational channels, and undermine the CSA's closed regulatory system. Congress had a rational basis for concluding that the aggregate impact of such an exemption would be substantial.

Stevens distinguished Lopez and Morrison on two grounds. First, those cases involved facial challenges to short, single-subject statutes regulating noneconomic activity, not as-applied challenges excising applications from a comprehensive economic regulatory scheme. Second, the activities at issue in Lopez (gun possession in school zones) and Morrison (gender-motivated violence) were noneconomic; the production, distribution, and consumption of commodities like marijuana is "quintessentially economic." Because the CSA directly regulates economic activity, Lopez and Morrison pose no obstacle.

The CSA's closed-regulatory-system structure was central to Stevens's analysis. Unlike the Gun-Free School Zones Act in Lopez (a targeted, single-subject prohibition) or the Violence Against Women Act in Morrison (a civil-remedy statute), the CSA establishes a comprehensive, self-contained regulatory system for all controlled substances across five schedules. Within that system, every exception creates an enforcement gap: locally cultivated marijuana can be indistinguishable from market marijuana, can be diverted into recreational channels, can be used to launder market supply, and can depress prices in the illegal interstate market Congress is attempting to suppress. The rational-basis review applied to the class of activity is permissive but not toothless: Stevens required that Congress have "a rational basis for concluding" that the failure to regulate the intrastate slice would "undercut" the broader interstate regulatory scheme. On those facts, the CSA's comprehensive structure satisfied the test.

On the Lopez/Morrison framework, Stevens emphasized two distinctions. First, Lopez and Morrison were facial challenges to stand-alone, single-subject statutes; Raich was an as-applied challenge asking the Court to carve out an individual application from a comprehensive regulatory scheme. The Court had never accepted such a carve-out and declined to do so here. Second, the activities in Lopez (gun possession) and Morrison (gender-motivated violence) were noneconomic; the production, distribution, and consumption of marijuana is "quintessentially economic" because it involves the market for a fungible commodity. This economic/noneconomic distinction became the doctrinal bridge between Wickard's aggregation principle and the post-Lopez regime: where the class of activity is economic, Wickard's rational-basis aggregation analysis applies; where it is noneconomic, Lopez and Morrison's more demanding review governs.

### Concurrence (Scalia, in the judgment)

Justice Scalia concurred in the judgment but disagreed with the majority's analytical route. Rather than extending the substantial-effects test to cover the intrastate activity directly, Scalia located the congressional power in the Necessary and Proper Clause. His premise: the Commerce Clause, read in light of Lopez and Morrison, reaches economic activity that substantially affects interstate commerce, but it does not reach all noneconomic activity simply because it is embedded within a broader regulatory scheme. That said, the Necessary and Proper Clause independently authorizes Congress to regulate noneconomic intrastate activity when regulation of that activity is reasonably adapted to making the broader interstate regulatory scheme effective. Scalia read Lopez and Morrison as preserving only a narrow residual space -- isolated noneconomic regulation that is wholly untethered from any broader interstate regime falls outside federal power. But once Congress has enacted a comprehensive interstate regulatory scheme, as it plainly did in the CSA, it may reach local activities that could otherwise be regulated only at the state level, provided that excluding them would impede the regulation of the interstate market. The power is not simply the Commerce Clause power itself; it is the power flowing from the Necessary and Proper Clause to effectuate a legitimate commerce regulation. Scalia's concurrence is therefore narrower than the majority on the Commerce Clause but arrives at the same result via the NPC, and it anticipates the analytical move Roberts made in NFIB v Sebelius (2012) when he located authority over noneconomic individual conduct within NPC analysis rather than the Commerce Clause standing alone.

### Dissent (O'Connor)

Justice O'Connor, joined in part by Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justice Thomas, argued that the majority had effectively drained Lopez and Morrison of all practical significance. The majority's rule, she contended, is that Congress may reach any local activity -- no matter how noncommercial, no matter how purely intrastate -- simply by incorporating it into a larger regulatory scheme. On that logic, Lopez and Morrison were aberrations: a legislature sufficiently creative in structuring its statutory scheme will always be able to bring any activity within federal reach. The limiting principle the Court thought it was announcing in Lopez has dissolved.

O'Connor grounded her dissent primarily in federalism. California's Compassionate Use Act was a textbook exercise of the states' historic police power over public health and medical regulation. One of the chief virtues of the federal system, she emphasized, is that states can serve as "laboratories of experimentation" -- a phrase traceable to Brandeis -- allowing different policy approaches to compete before one is settled on nationally. Federalism is not merely a structural abstraction; it secures concrete liberty by keeping many policy decisions close to the people most affected. The majority's holding eliminates that capacity in the drug policy domain by forcing federal supremacy even where a state has carefully carved out a noncommercial, medically supervised exception.

On the merits, O'Connor disputed the majority's characterization of the activity as economic. The medical marijuana that Raich and Monson grew and used was not traded or sold; it never entered any market, legal or illegal. To call it "economic" because marijuana generally has an interstate market, and to then aggregate all such local non-transactions, is to reason at a level of generality that will always sustain federal regulation regardless of the actual character of the activity. The Wickard analogy, she argued, proves too much: Filburn at least had produced a commodity for which there was an active market; Raich produced no commodity at all in any commercial sense. If the aggregation principle applies to every activity that might affect a market in the broadest possible sense, it provides no limit whatsoever to federal power.

### Dissent (Thomas)

Justice Thomas wrote separately to press an originalist objection that goes deeper than O'Connor's structural federalism argument. His starting point is text: "Commerce among the several States" meant, at the founding, the buying and selling of goods across state lines -- the trade and exchange that occurs between states, not the production or consumption of goods within a single state for one's own use. Raich and Monson grew marijuana locally, consumed it locally, and never placed it into any stream of commerce. That activity is simply not "commerce among the several States" by any honest reading of the constitutional text.

Thomas traced his argument through his concurrence in Lopez (1995), where he had already urged the Court to abandon the post-New Deal substantial-effects jurisprudence and return to a more restrained reading of the Commerce Clause. In his view, Wickard v Filburn itself was wrong when decided, and Raich compounds the error. The aggregation principle, as the majority applies it, has no logical stopping point: virtually any local activity can be said to aggregate into an effect on some national market if characterized at sufficient breadth. On that logic, the Commerce Clause is a general police power, which the Framers deliberately withheld from the federal government.

On the Necessary and Proper Clause, Thomas rejected Scalia's concurrence as bootstrapping. The NPC cannot serve as an autonomous source of power to regulate activity that the Commerce Clause does not itself reach; it is only a means-selection clause enabling Congress to implement an enumerated power it already possesses. Allowing the NPC to expand the range of activities Congress may regulate -- rather than merely the range of means by which it may pursue an authorized end -- effectively reads the enumeration of powers out of the Constitution. If the NPC can sweep in wholly local, noncommercial activity because it is "necessary and proper" to some broader scheme, then no meaningful limit on federal power remains. Thomas's dissent thus sets out the most ambitious doctrinal agenda of any opinion in Raich: a return to the pre-Wickard Commerce Clause, the abandonment of the aggregation principle, and a rigorous distinction between commerce (exchange) and production/consumption.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

1. Arizona enacts a medical exemption allowing terminally ill patients to grow limited quantities of opium poppies for personal consumption of homemade pain tinctures. The federal Controlled Substances Act prohibits all opium cultivation. A patient challenges CSA enforcement as applied to her intrastate, noncommercial, state-authorized cultivation. This case is structurally identical to Raich. Opium is a fungible commodity at the center of a massive, well-documented illegal interstate market. The CSA is the same comprehensive, closed regulatory scheme that provided the analytical predicate in Raich. Every intrastate exemption -- however supervised and noncommercial -- creates the enforcement gap Stevens identified: locally grown opium is indistinguishable from market opium, can be diverted, can undercut federal scheduling, and can depress prices in the illicit market Congress is suppressing. Congress had at least a rational basis for concluding that the aggregate effect of such personal-cultivation exemptions would undercut the CSA. This case comes out the same way as Raich.

2. Congress enacts a statute prohibiting the home cultivation of ephedra plants beyond a threshold quantity, classifying the plant as a Schedule V controlled precursor because it can be converted into methamphetamine. A Montana resident grows ephedra for personal use in herbal teas. No sale or transfer occurs. This hypo involves a fungible plant with a direct, established relationship to an illegal interstate drug market. Even though the resident's cultivation is entirely local and noncommercial, the Raich framework applies: ephedra is the economic input to a fungible controlled substance, and Congress rationally concluded that failure to regulate home cultivation would undercut the broader regulatory scheme suppressing methamphetamine production. The fact that the regulated activity is one step removed from the final controlled substance does not change the analysis, because the comprehensive regulatory scheme covers precursors as part of its enforcement logic. This comes out the same way as Raich.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

3. Congress enacts the Medical Marijuana Suppression Act, a targeted, single-subject statute that does nothing more than criminalize the intrastate cultivation of marijuana for personal medical use. There is no accompanying comprehensive controlled-substances scheme; the statute stands alone. On these facts Raich does not save the statute. Stevens's majority expressly grounded the result in the CSA's character as a comprehensive, closed regulatory system: the as-applied challenge failed because excising the local exemption would have undercut the whole integrated scheme. Where the statute is a stand-alone, single-subject prohibition on local activity -- exactly the profile Lopez and Morrison condemned -- the escape hatch is gone. The court must ask under Lopez's three-category framework whether this is (a) regulation of channels of interstate commerce, (b) regulation of instrumentalities of interstate commerce, or (c) regulation of activity having a substantial effect on interstate commerce. Since this is a targeted criminal prohibition of local activity, it must satisfy (c). Without the comprehensive-scheme context, the activity looks far more like the noncommercial possession in Lopez than the fungible-commodity market in Raich. This is a hard case that would likely turn on how the Court characterizes the relevant class of activity -- purely local medical use, or the marijuana market writ large -- and whether Lopez's economic/noneconomic distinction is applied at the activity level or the market level.

4. Congress enacts a statute criminalizing the personal, nontransferable home production of raw honey from backyard beehives beyond a household threshold, citing aggregate effects on the interstate commercial honey market. The honey is consumed by the producer's household only and is never sold or gifted. Unlike marijuana, honey is not a fungible controlled substance at the center of an illegal market; no comprehensive regulatory scheme exists for honey analogous to the CSA; and the activity is quintessentially local domestic production with a remote and attenuated connection to interstate commerce. Without a comprehensive scheme to anchor the as-applied analysis, and without the fungible-commodity / illegal-market features that drove Wickard and Raich, this statute likely fails Lopez's substantial-effects test. Congress cannot plausibly claim a rational basis for concluding that the household's own honey consumption substantially affects interstate commerce when the connection depends on several inferential steps and the product is wholly non-fungible with interstate commercial honey in any enforcement sense. This comes out differently from Raich.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

5. Colorado and a consortium of states enact coordinated medical marijuana statutes creating a "closed-loop" intrastate regulatory system: cultivation, dispensing, and consumption are all licensed and tracked by GPS-tagged plant identification numbers; product cannot leave the state; all inventory is audited monthly; and the system is technically capable of distinguishing licensed state-program marijuana from any diverted or illegal product. Participants challenge CSA enforcement as applied. This is the genuinely hard case Raich left open. Stevens's enforcement-gap rationale rested on fungibility and the practical impossibility of distinguishing local from market supply. If a state can actually implement rigorous traceability that destroys fungibility in enforcement terms, the empirical premise of the Raich majority weakens considerably. O'Connor's dissent implicitly invited exactly this experiment. However, Raich never held that Congress's rational basis requires an empirically verified enforcement gap -- only that Congress rationally could conclude one exists. A court applying rational-basis review would likely defer to Congress's judgment that even a technically closed state system creates residual diversion risk. Whether the case comes out the same way probably depends on whether the Court applies Raich's deferential rationality standard mechanically or scrutinizes the claim more carefully in light of concrete evidence that the traceability system eliminates the identified risk. Genuinely unclear.

## Critique

Federalist critique: The most common conservative objection to Raich is that the decision effectively nullified Lopez and Morrison by identifying a formula -- embedding prohibited local activity within a broader comprehensive regulatory scheme -- that Congress can always satisfy. If all Congress must do to reach any local activity is wrap it in a sufficiently comprehensive statutory scheme, then the Commerce Clause limits announced in Lopez are practically inert. Every federal criminal prohibition can be characterized as part of a larger regulatory regime. The comprehensive-scheme escape hatch is not a principled limit; it is an invitation to strategic drafting. Raich thus represents, on this view, the quiet restoration of a general police power that the Framers deliberately withheld from the federal government and that the New Federalism cases of the 1990s briefly seemed to constrain.

Libertarian and originalist critique: Justice Thomas's dissent reflects the most unsparing version of this critique. The aggregation principle itself -- the inheritance from Wickard -- is the original error. Commerce, in 1789, meant exchange, not production or consumption. By permitting Congress to regulate any local activity that, in aggregate across the entire country, could conceivably affect an interstate market, the Court has defined the Commerce Clause to cover virtually all human economic activity. Thomas's project is to reconstitute the pre-Wickard understanding that production, manufacturing, and agriculture were distinct from "commerce" and fell within state rather than federal regulatory power. Raich represents the furthest extension of the Wickard baseline and, on this critique, the furthest departure from the Constitution's original design. Randy Barnett, who argued for the respondents, pressed the as-applied framing as a constitutional innovation: carving out noncommercial, state-permitted personal use as categorically distinct from the market Congress was regulating. While Barnett ultimately lost, his argument influenced Scalia's separate NPC-based concurrence and helped establish the conceptual vocabulary that Roberts later used to distinguish regulatory mandates from compulsion in NFIB v Sebelius.

Progressive defense: From a progressive standpoint, Raich is correctly decided and the New Federalism cases of the 1990s were the anomaly. The federal government needs the capacity to manage large-scale market failures and coordinate regulatory regimes across state lines; fragmenting drug regulation by exempting state-authorized personal use would create precisely the enforcement gaps and cross-state inconsistencies that the Commerce Clause was designed to allow Congress to address. The aggregation principle from Wickard is not a bug but a feature of modern federalism: it allows Congress to address economic problems that operate at a national scale even when individual instances of the regulated behavior are local. From this perspective, the Raich result vindicates the Court's post-1937 recognition that a national economy requires national regulatory capacity, and Lopez and Morrison are the decisions that need explaining, not Raich.

## Key Points

- Locked Wickard's aggregation principle into the post-Lopez doctrinal map by rephrasing "substantially affects" as a rational-basis review of Congress's judgment about the aggregate effect of a class of intrastate activity.
- Created the comprehensive-scheme escape hatch: as-applied challenges to intrastate slices of a broader economic regulatory regime almost always fail.
- Scalia concurrence treats the Necessary and Proper Clause, not the Commerce Clause alone, as the operative grant of power over noneconomic intrastate activity integrated into a larger scheme. This framing anticipates the analytical move in NFIB v Sebelius (2012).
- Doctrinal opposite of Lopez (1995) and Morrison (2000): those cases were facial challenges to short, single-subject, noneconomic statutes; Raich was an as-applied challenge to an intrastate slice of a comprehensive economic scheme.
- Professor emphasis: Raich is the doctrinal bookend to Lopez and Morrison and the key case for understanding why the "New Federalism" revival remained bounded.
