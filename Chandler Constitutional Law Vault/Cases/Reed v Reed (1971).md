# Reed v Reed (1971)

## Memory Jogger

> The first case where the Supreme Court struck down a sex-based classification under the Equal Protection Clause, holding that preferring men over women as estate administrators to avoid hearings is an unconstitutional arbitrary legislative choice.

---

## Facts

Sally and Cecil Reed were the separated parents of Skip Reed, a minor who died in Idaho without a will. Both parents filed petitions in probate court to be appointed administrator of their son's estate. The Idaho Probate Code contained a mandatory preference provision: when two persons equally entitled to administer a decedent's estate were of different sexes, the male must be preferred. The probate court appointed Cecil (the father) over Sally (the mother) solely on the basis of this mandatory sex preference. The amount at issue was small, but Sally challenged the sex-based preference as a violation of the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. The Idaho statute's explicit purpose, as the state defended it, was to reduce the workload of probate courts by eliminating the need for hearings to choose between applicants of equal entitlement.

---

## Procedural History

The Idaho district court initially ruled for Sally Reed, finding the sex-preference provision unconstitutional. The Idaho Supreme Court reversed, holding that the statute was a reasonable exercise of the legislature's power to regulate the administration of estates and not a denial of equal protection. The Idaho Supreme Court reasoned that the legislature could reasonably conclude that men were more familiar with business affairs. Sally Reed sought certiorari, and the United States Supreme Court unanimously reversed.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority (9-0):** Burger (joined by all Justices) -- unanimous decision

---

## Holding

An Idaho statute providing that, as between persons equally entitled to administer an estate, men must be preferred over women violates the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. A mandatory sex-based preference for the purpose of avoiding hearings on the merits is an arbitrary legislative choice that the Equal Protection Clause forbids.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Chief Justice Burger wrote for a unanimous Court. The majority applied what reads like rational basis review -- asking whether the classification rests upon "some ground of difference having a fair and substantial relation to the object of the legislation." The Court assumed, without extensive analysis, that the legislature could treat different classes differently, but concluded that the classification here -- mandatory male preference -- had no fair and substantial relation to the statute's objective of efficient estate administration.

The majority's key analytical move was to hold that using sex to eliminate the need for hearings amounted to making "the very kind of arbitrary legislative choice forbidden by the Equal Protection Clause." Crucially, the Court did not declare sex a suspect classification requiring strict scrutiny. It did not say so, and it did not need to. The Idaho statute failed because its chosen proxy (sex) was wholly unrelated to any legitimate indicator of competence or fitness to administer estates. Administrative convenience -- avoiding a hearing -- cannot justify treating similarly situated men and women differently.

The decision is short (less than five pages) and does not articulate a clear tier of scrutiny. Scholars and later courts would read it as either applying a heightened version of rational basis or as a harbinger of a new middle tier. Justice Brennan would push for intermediate scrutiny two years later in Frontiero v Richardson (1973), but the Court would not formally adopt that standard until Craig v Boren (1976).

Burger's opinion leans on a formulation borrowed from Royster Guano Co. v Virginia (1920): a statutory classification must rest "upon some ground of difference having a fair and substantial relation to the object of the legislation." That phrasing is older than modern tiers of scrutiny, but on its face it demands a closer fit than bare rationality. By pulling it forward into a sex case, Burger did two things at once. He stayed within the formal boundary of the rational basis framework the Court had been using for non-racial classifications, and he also signaled that the tie between a government's stated purpose and the sex line it was drawing had to be more than hypothetical. Idaho's asserted interest was a real one, and Burger did not say otherwise. The objective of reducing probate court workloads by avoiding contested hearings was, in the Court's words, "not without some legitimacy." The fatal move was the state's choice of proxy. A categorical preference for men did not track any actual difference in fitness to administer estates. It chose one applicant over another on the basis of sex for no reason related to the functions of an administrator.

The Court's choice not to declare sex a suspect class was deliberate and consequential. Declaring sex suspect would have required the Court to walk through the usual suspect-class factors (history of discrimination, political powerlessness, immutability, relevance of the trait to legitimate government aims) at a moment when the lower courts were just beginning to take sex-discrimination claims seriously. It also would have exposed the Equal Rights Amendment debate to an argument that the ERA was unnecessary because the Fourteenth Amendment already reached sex classifications at the highest tier. Burger avoided that terrain. The opinion's silence on scrutiny tiers invited the next case and the case after that to fill the doctrinal gap, which Frontiero v Richardson (1973) and Craig v Boren (1976) then did.

As a matter of constitutional history, the significance of Reed is not what it said but what it did. For the first time in the Equal Protection Clause's century of existence, the Supreme Court struck down a statute because it drew a sex line. The opinion's modest length and tone concealed a doctrinal door that had been opened. The Equal Protection Clause, which before 1971 had been marshaled against race classifications and a narrow set of non-race categories, was now available as a lever for sex-discrimination claims. Everything that followed in the sex-equality arc, from Frontiero through Craig v Boren to United States v Virginia, rested on Reed as foundation.

### Separate Opinions

No Justice wrote a concurring or dissenting opinion. The decision is 9-0 on a short single-author opinion from the Chief Justice. The absence of separate writing is itself historically notable: on a question as politically charged as the constitutional status of sex classifications, the Court spoke with one voice without any Justice wanting to add qualifications on the record.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

*State Insurance Board Tiebreaker.* A state workers' compensation statute provides that when two surviving dependents are equally entitled to a lump-sum death benefit, the male dependent is to be paid first. The state defends the rule as a tool for saving the time and cost of hearings. Under Reed, the rule would be struck down. The state's proxy (sex) has no fair and substantial relation to any legitimate interest in the orderly administration of the fund, and the elimination of hearings is not a permissible basis for making one class of claimants categorically preferred over another similarly situated class.

*Municipal Housing Waitlist Preference.* A city's municipal housing authority uses sex as a tiebreaker when two applicants have identical scores on its needs-based selection criteria, preferring male-headed households on the stated ground that men are more likely to maintain a unit. Whatever administrative convenience the rule delivers, there is no fair and substantial relation between sex and tenancy performance, and Reed forbids a state from using sex to shortcut a case-by-case inquiry.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

*Military Physical Fitness Screening.* A state-run correctional officer academy applies different pull-up and run-time standards for male and female candidates, drawing on independently validated physiological studies showing different average capacities in ways relevant to the specific job tasks. The state has documented the job-relatedness of each differential standard. This would not fail Reed. The classification rests on a difference in actual physical capacity tied to the job's demands, and it uses sex only as a rough proxy for the physiological variable that matters. Reed tolerates a sex-based rule that actually corresponds to the state's legitimate objective.

*Prison Searches by Same-Sex Officers.* A state prison policy directs that strip searches of detainees be conducted by officers of the same sex. The policy is defended on privacy and dignity grounds rather than administrative convenience. Under Reed, this would survive because there is a fair and substantial relation between the sex of the searching officer and the privacy interest the policy aims to protect. The sex line tracks the objective rather than substituting for it.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

*Jury Service Exemption Preference.* A statute provides that when multiple family members are summoned to the same jury pool, the wife is to be exempted in favor of the husband if one spouse must be excused for childcare reasons. The state cites administrative convenience and a statistical correlation with which parent most often functions as the child's primary caregiver. The statistical tie is real but imperfect: perhaps two-thirds of primary-caregiver households in the state match the stereotype. Under Reed the outcome is unclear. The rule has more of a "fair and substantial relation" to the state's child-welfare objective than Idaho's bare preference for estate administrators did, but it still makes a categorical sex assignment where individualized inquiry would be available. A court could go either way, and the case might press the Court toward articulating a more demanding scrutiny standard, which Frontiero and Craig v Boren eventually did.

## Critique

*Feminist critique.* From a feminist legal theory perspective, Reed is a decision celebrated as first contact and criticized as a missed opportunity. Ruth Bader Ginsburg, then a litigator on Sally Reed's brief, laid out an ambitious argument that sex ought to be treated as a suspect classification on the model of race. The Court accepted the result but not the reasoning. The consequence was that the next generation of sex-equality litigation had to be re-fought under formulations still subject to judicial discretion. Had the Court taken the larger step in 1971, cases involving statutes treating women as dependents rather than earners, like Frontiero, might not have required a plurality fracturing the Court's approach two years later. The narrowness of Reed left the scrutiny question open for two decades and produced the unstable intermediate tier eventually arrived at in Craig v Boren.

*Originalist critique.* An originalist reader of the Fourteenth Amendment would note that the generation that ratified it had just approved the Fifteenth Amendment specifically to extend the franchise on race but conspicuously declined to extend it on sex. The First Congress to pass the Fourteenth Amendment rejected proposals to include "sex" in the equal protection language. On that record, it is at least debatable whether the original public meaning of the Equal Protection Clause reached state statutes that classified by sex, particularly in domestic-relations contexts like intestate estate administration traditionally left to state police power. Reed makes that objection largely academic by 1971, but the originalist critique persists in writings by Raoul Berger and others and anchors later objections from Justices Scalia and Thomas to the Court's sex-equality jurisprudence.

*Doctrinal critique of scrutiny levels.* There is an ongoing scholarly debate about what Reed actually did. On one reading, the Court applied nothing more than rational basis review and reached a rare result where the asserted reason failed even that forgiving test. On another reading, the opinion quietly imported a more rigorous fit requirement from Royster Guano and applied what Gerald Gunther famously called rational basis "with bite." If the first reading is correct, Reed is of narrow compass and its true sex-equality legacy had to wait for Frontiero and Craig. If the second reading is correct, the Court was already on the way to an intermediate tier in 1971 and simply did not name what it was doing. The opinion's cryptic presentation has allowed both readings to survive, and lower courts applying Reed in its own right between 1971 and 1976 produced inconsistent results in part because the doctrine itself was unclear.

## Key Quotations

> "To give a mandatory preference to members of either sex over members of the other, merely to accomplish the elimination of hearings on the merits, is to make the very kind of arbitrary legislative choice forbidden by the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment; and whatever may be said as to the positive values of avoiding intrafamily controversy, the choice in this context may not lawfully be mandated solely on the basis of sex." (Chief Justice Burger)

A classification 'must be reasonable, not arbitrary, and must rest upon some ground of difference having a fair and substantial relation to the object of the legislation, so that all persons similarly circumstanced shall be treated alike.' ## Key Points

- The first Supreme Court decision to strike a state statute under the Equal Protection Clause because it drew a sex line. Unanimous 9-0 opinion by Chief Justice Burger, under five pages, no concurrences or dissents.
- Applied a formulation from Royster Guano Co. v Virginia (1920): a classification must rest "upon some ground of difference having a fair and substantial relation to the object of the legislation." That phrasing predates modern tiers of scrutiny but demands a closer fit than bare rationality.
- Administrative convenience (avoiding probate-court hearings) cannot justify categorical sex preference among similarly situated applicants. The state's goal was legitimate; the proxy it chose (sex) bore no fair and substantial relation to fitness to administer estates.
- Deliberately did not declare sex a suspect classification. The tier question was left open, which Frontiero v Richardson (1973) tried and failed to resolve at strict scrutiny and which Craig v Boren (1976) finally settled at intermediate scrutiny.
- Doctrinal significance exceeded the opinion's modest scope. Every subsequent sex-equality decision (Frontiero, Craig, United States v Virginia) builds on Reed as foundation.
- Scholarly debate persists over whether Reed applied ordinary rational basis or Gerald Gunther's "rational basis with bite." The opinion's cryptic presentation has allowed both readings to survive.
- Ruth Bader Ginsburg, then a litigator for Sally Reed, urged suspect-class treatment. The Court took the result but not the reasoning, leaving future cases to relitigate the tier question.

## Connections

- [[Topics/Equal Protection]]: Reed is the first Supreme Court decision to strike a state statute for a sex classification under the Equal Protection Clause. The opinion anchors the sex-discrimination arc and reappears as a foundational citation in every subsequent sex-equality holding.
- [[Cases/Frontiero v Richardson (1973)]]: Two years later, a Brennan plurality pushed the Court toward declaring sex a suspect class using Reed as its jumping-off point. Reed's deliberate silence on scrutiny left the doctrinal door open; Frontiero tried and failed to walk through it at strict scrutiny.
- [[Cases/Craig v Boren (1976)]]: The Court formally established intermediate scrutiny for sex classifications, grounding the new tier in the Reed-Frontiero line and quoting Reed's "fair and substantial relation" language as doctrinal bridge.
- [[Cases/Bradwell v Illinois (1873)]]: The decision Reed quietly displaced. Bradwell's Fourteenth Amendment rejection of a sex-equality claim (about women's admission to the bar) stood largely unexamined until Reed broke the pattern.
- [[Cases/United States v Virginia (1996)]]: The modern sex-equality apex, which invokes Reed as its doctrinal point of origin for the proposition that courts must take a "hard look" at official generalizations about sex. Ginsburg, who litigated Reed, later authored VMI.
- [[Cases/Geduldig v Aiello (1974)]]: Brennan's dissent in Geduldig leans on Reed's fair-and-substantial-relation rationale to argue that pregnancy-based state classifications should be treated as sex classifications triggering heightened review. The majority sidestepped that argument and limited Reed to classifications drawn on sex as such.

**Sex-classification scrutiny tier comparison.** The doctrinal arc Reed opens, traced across the four canonical sex-classification decisions:

| Case | Year | Tier announced | Verbal formulation | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [[Cases/Reed v Reed (1971)\|Reed v Reed]] | 1971 | Rational basis (with bite) | "fair and substantial relation to the object of the legislation" (Royster Guano language imported) | Idaho administrator-preference statute struck |
| [[Cases/Frontiero v Richardson (1973)\|Frontiero v Richardson]] | 1973 | Strict scrutiny (plurality only; no majority) | sex is "inherently suspect" and classifications must withstand "close judicial scrutiny" | Federal military-benefits statute struck (4-1-3-1) |
| [[Cases/Craig v Boren (1976)\|Craig v Boren]] | 1976 | Intermediate scrutiny (formal tier) | "important governmental objectives" / "substantially related to achievement of those objectives" | Oklahoma 3.2% beer age-and-sex statute struck |
| [[Cases/United States v Virginia (1996)\|United States v Virginia (VMI)]] | 1996 | Intermediate scrutiny tightened | state must show "exceedingly persuasive justification" for the classification | Virginia Military Institute male-only admissions struck |

The progression is not strictly monotonic. Frontiero's plurality reach for strict scrutiny lost Powell and never bound a majority, leaving Reed's rational-basis-with-bite as the operative standard until Craig formalized intermediate scrutiny three years later. VMI did not announce a new tier; Ginsburg's "exceedingly persuasive justification" gloss tightened Craig's "substantially related" requirement without crossing into strict scrutiny. Reed's "fair and substantial relation" wording (drawn from Royster Guano Co v Virginia, 253 U.S. 412 (1920)) is the linguistic bridge that recurs across all three later opinions.
