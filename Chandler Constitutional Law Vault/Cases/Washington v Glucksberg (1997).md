# Washington v Glucksberg (1997)

## Memory Jogger

> The unanimous Court upheld Washington State's ban on assisted suicide, holding there is no fundamental right to physician-assisted suicide under the Due Process Clause, and formally crystallizing the two-part test for identifying fundamental rights: the asserted liberty interest must be (1) deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition and (2) implicit in the concept of ordered liberty, with a careful description of the right at the appropriate level of specificity.

---

## Facts

Washington State's Natural Death Act made it a felony to knowingly cause or aid another person to attempt suicide. Several Washington physicians who treated terminally ill patients, along with three gravely ill individuals, filed suit arguing that the statute as applied to competent, terminally ill adults wishing to hasten their own deaths with a physician's assistance violated the Due Process Clause. The plaintiffs argued that the Due Process Clause protected an individual's right to choose the time and manner of death, at least in the specific context of terminally ill, mentally competent adults acting with a willing physician. The State defended the statute on grounds that it had compelling interests in preserving human life, preventing suicide, protecting the integrity of the medical profession, protecting vulnerable groups, and avoiding a slide toward euthanasia.

---

## Procedural History

The plaintiffs filed suit in the Western District of Washington. The District Court granted summary judgment for plaintiffs, finding a liberty interest. A divided Ninth Circuit panel reversed, but the en banc Ninth Circuit, sitting as a circuit court of appeals, reversed the panel decision and struck down the statute, concluding that the Due Process Clause protected a liberty interest of competent, terminally ill adults to choose physician-assisted suicide. The Supreme Court granted certiorari and reversed unanimously, upholding the statute.

---

## Judicial Votes

The judgment was 9-0, but only 5 Justices joined Rehnquist's majority opinion. The other 4 concurred in the judgment only and rejected the methodology.

- **Majority opinion (5 Justices):** Rehnquist, C.J. (author), joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas
- **Concurrence (joining majority + writing separately):** O'Connor (narrower; reserved the question of palliative care; was the decisive fifth vote for the majority opinion)
- **Concurring in the judgment only (4 Justices; rejected Rehnquist's two-part test as the controlling methodology):**
  - Stevens (wrote separately; would not foreclose as-applied challenges)
  - Souter (prefers Harlan's "reasoned judgment" method over Rehnquist's rigid two-part test)
  - Ginsburg (concurs in the judgment substantially for the reasons stated by O'Connor)
  - Breyer (would reframe as "right to die with dignity" rather than assisted suicide; joined O'Connor's reasoning on palliative care)

---

## Holding

Washington's ban on assisted suicide does not violate the Due Process Clause. The Due Process Clause protects fundamental rights and liberties that are deeply rooted in the Nation's history and tradition and implicit in the concept of ordered liberty. The claimed right -- a right to commit suicide with another's assistance -- has no such tradition; suicide and assisted suicide have long been condemned. Because no fundamental right is at stake, the statute is subject only to rational basis review, which it easily satisfies given the State's substantial interests in preserving life, preventing abuse of vulnerable persons, and maintaining the integrity of the medical profession.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion (Rehnquist, C.J.)

Chief Justice Rehnquist's majority opinion is notable as much for its methodology as for its result. Drawing together the Court's substantive due process precedents, Rehnquist articulated the now-canonical two-part test: (1) the claimed liberty interest must be "objectively, deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition" and (2) it must be "implicit in the concept of ordered liberty," such that "neither liberty nor justice would exist if [it] were sacrificed." Critically, the majority insisted on a "careful description" of the asserted fundamental interest -- a requirement that courts define the claimed right with specificity rather than at a high level of abstraction. When so defined, the claimed right to physician-assisted suicide had no tradition of protection; indeed, assisted suicide had been consistently condemned under Anglo-American common law and by the laws of all 50 states for most of American history. With no fundamental right at stake, the majority applied rational basis review and easily upheld the statute.

### O'Connor Concurrence (joined by Ginsburg in relevant part; practically the decisive fifth vote)

O'Connor joined Rehnquist's opinion in full but wrote separately to narrow its reach. She emphasized that the plaintiffs had not challenged the State's ability to provide aggressive palliative care, including medication that might hasten death as an unintended consequence, and she explicitly reserved the question whether a terminally ill patient with no access to such care might have a constitutionally cognizable interest in hastening death. Her concurrence signals that Glucksberg does not close the door on every as-applied challenge and that the Court's two-part test operates within a broader ecosystem where adequate palliative care is assumed to be available. Because O'Connor was the fifth vote for Rehnquist's opinion, this carve-out is not dictum but a functional limit on the scope of what Glucksberg actually decided.

### Stevens Concurrence (concurs in judgment only)

Stevens voted to uphold the Washington statute on the face-of-the-statute challenge presented but declined to join the majority opinion because he refused to foreclose future as-applied challenges on behalf of individual patients. He treated the State's interest in preserving life as substantial but variable in force depending on the patient's condition: the interest weakens as a patient approaches death, while the patient's own liberty interest in avoiding unwanted suffering strengthens. Stevens is the most explicit that the case's 9-0 judgment says less about the constitutional question than the reporter tally suggests.

### Souter Concurrence (concurs in judgment only)

Souter wrote the longest and most methodologically freighted concurrence. He voted with the majority on outcome but rejected the two-part test as a governing framework for substantive due process. Drawing directly on Justice Harlan's dissent in Poe v Ullman, Souter argued that "reasoned judgment" rather than a mechanical history-and-tradition inquiry is the honest description of what the Court does in unenumerated-rights cases. His concurrence explains that Harlan's method is neither "formulaic" nor "a formula" but a common-law reconciliation of liberty claims with "well-accepted principles and criteria" that must "take its place in relation to what went before and further cut a channel for what is to come." For Souter, the Rehnquist test obscures rather than constrains the interpretive choice about how narrowly or broadly to describe the claimed right.

### Ginsburg Concurrence

Ginsburg filed a one-sentence concurrence stating that she concurred in the judgment substantially for the reasons stated by O'Connor.

### Breyer Concurrence (concurs in judgment only)

Breyer would have reframed the asserted right as a "right to die with dignity" that bundles avoidance of severe pain, patient autonomy, and professional medical assistance. Thus reframed, he thought some protected liberty interest plausibly existed. But the Washington statute as challenged did not bar adequate palliative care, so Breyer joined the judgment upholding it. His reframing is the clearest articulation in the case that the "careful description" requirement is load-bearing: describe the right one way and the history-and-tradition test kicks in; describe it another way and a different substantive due process analysis applies.

### Doctrinal Lineage: Glucksberg, Lawrence, Obergefell, Dobbs

Glucksberg's two-part test sits at the center of a quarter-century methodology war. Lawrence v Texas (2003) declined to apply Glucksberg to strike down the Texas sodomy statute, substituting a more abstract autonomy analysis under the liberty interest. Obergefell v Hodges (2015) explicitly rejected Glucksberg's "careful description" requirement in favor of a framework that reads liberty in light of "new insight" and "emerging awareness" about dignity. Dobbs v Jackson Women's Health (2022) reasserted Glucksberg as the governing framework and used it to conclude that abortion is not deeply rooted in Anglo-American legal tradition, overruling Roe and Casey. The pendulum swing between Glucksberg and its rivals is the operative methodology question in post-2022 unenumerated-rights litigation.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

1. A state criminalizes assisted dying even for mentally competent patients suffering from severe but non-terminal chronic pain. **Why:** The "careful description" of the asserted right would be "the right to obtain medical assistance in ending one's life in the absence of imminent death," a description under which no Anglo-American tradition of protection exists. Rational basis review applies. The State's interest in preventing suicide, protecting the vulnerable, and maintaining medical-professional integrity is even stronger when death is not imminent, because the line between chronic suffering and depressive illness susceptible to treatment is harder to draw.

2. A state criminalizes any form of actively hastening death by physicians, including terminal sedation administered with the primary intent of causing death rather than relieving pain. **Why:** The Glucksberg framework distinguishes the "double effect" palliative-care case O'Connor flagged from active killing with the primary intent to end life. A statute that reaches the latter is on the same constitutional footing as the Washington ban: the careful description yields "the right to have a physician cause one's death," and the tradition runs against protection.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

3. A state prohibits competent adults from refusing life-sustaining treatment, including mechanical ventilation and artificial nutrition, against their express prior wishes. **Why:** Cruzan (1990) assumed that a competent person has a constitutionally protected liberty interest in refusing unwanted medical treatment, rooted in the common-law doctrine of informed consent and the battery tradition. That interest has the historical pedigree Glucksberg demanded and is the paradigmatic example of a deeply-rooted negative liberty. A statute overriding it faces strict scrutiny, not rational basis.

4. A state law flatly prohibits physicians from discussing end-of-life options — including legally available palliative sedation — with their terminally ill patients. **Why:** This changes the constitutional clause in play from substantive due process to the First Amendment. Glucksberg forecloses a fundamental right to assistance in dying; it says nothing about professional speech. Content-based regulation of physician speech about lawful medical options triggers at minimum the professional-speech standards of NIFLA v Becerra (2018), not Glucksberg rational basis.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

5. A state legalizes physician-assisted dying for terminally ill patients with a prognosis of six months or less, then a later legislature repeals it. The repeal is challenged by a patient who would have qualified under the prior law. **Why:** Glucksberg seemingly forecloses the patient's fundamental-rights claim because it says no such right is deeply rooted. But O'Connor's concurrence preserves as-applied challenges where palliative alternatives are inadequate, and Breyer's "right to die with dignity" reframing suggests that a patient-specific record might distinguish an as-applied challenge from the facial challenge Glucksberg rejected. The question is whether Glucksberg forecloses all as-applied challenges or only the facial-only claim the respondents pressed. The case is a genuine coin-flip because the majority never answered the as-applied question O'Connor reserved.

---

## Critique

### Progressive / Liberty-Expansive Angle

The most substantial progressive critique is that the "careful description" requirement is where the decisive work gets done, not where it purports to be done. Cass Sunstein and others have argued that substantive due process requires courts to decide how narrowly or broadly to frame the asserted right, and Glucksberg resolves that meta-question by fiat in favor of the narrowest framing (assistance in committing suicide) rather than a broader one (control over the time and manner of one's own death; bodily autonomy in extremis). Souter's concurrence is the methodological smoking gun: a Justice who voted with the majority on outcome thought the two-part test was no more determinate than the Harlan "reasoned judgment" standard it replaced, because level-of-generality choices drive both. Obergefell's 2015 approach — reading liberty in light of "new insight" about dignity — is the competing methodology that Glucksberg's test formally displaces but cannot eliminate, because someone still has to pick the level of generality at which "the right" is described.

### Originalist / Textualist Angle

The originalist defense of Glucksberg, most fully articulated in Dobbs, is that without a deeply-rooted tradition test the Court has no principled constraint on substantive due process beyond the Justices' own policy preferences. The "careful description" requirement, on this view, is not a rhetorical sleight of hand but the necessary antidote to what originalists see as the Roe and Lochner problem: a too-general description of the claimed right lets the Court constitutionalize whichever liberty it happens to favor at a given moment. Scalia's separate writings and the Dobbs majority push this further: any articulation of substantive due process that is not tied to historical practice is not law but philosophical preference. Thomas's Dobbs concurrence goes a step further still, questioning the entire doctrine of substantive due process on textualist grounds independent of Glucksberg's test.

### Doctrinal Architecture Critique

A separate structural critique observes that Glucksberg and Lawrence cannot both be unqualifiedly correct as descriptions of SDP methodology, yet both are on the books. Kennedy's Lawrence majority did not apply Glucksberg's two-part test to the sodomy statute. Obergefell expressly rejected the "careful description" step. Dobbs reinstated the full Glucksberg apparatus. The operative question after 2022 is whether every prior substantive-due-process precedent outside the strict history-and-tradition box — including Griswold, Loving, and Obergefell — now rests on doctrinal quicksand. Thomas's Dobbs concurrence invites exactly that reexamination; the majority disclaims it. The tension is unresolved, and this case is at the center of the unresolved tension.

## Key Points

- Formally crystallized the two-part Glucksberg test: (1) deeply rooted in history and tradition + (2) implicit in ordered liberty; with a "careful description" requirement demanding specificity in framing the claimed right
- Rational basis applied when no fundamental right found; statute upheld on multiple state interests
- Unanimous result but six separate opinions, with most concurrences resisting the majority's rigid methodology
- Glucksberg vs. Lawrence tension: Kennedy in Lawrence implicitly relaxed the Glucksberg test for intimate liberty; Dobbs later reasserted it as the governing standard
- The "careful description" requirement is the crux of the methodology wars -- it lets the framing of the question determine the answer
- Treatment: Caution; cited 2,810 times; governing standard for fundamental-rights identification reasserted in Dobbs

The Lawrence and Obergefell rows are the methodology-wars pendulum's "broad-framing" arc: both opinions decline to apply Glucksberg's deeply-rooted test to a specifically-defined historical right, recasting the asserted liberty at a level of generality that makes the absence of historical recognition non-dispositive. Dobbs is the pendulum's return: Alito's majority restores Glucksberg as "the established method" and uses the careful-description requirement to defeat the abortion claim. The bright line in the table is whether the case picks the narrow or broad framing under Glucksberg's "careful description" requirement; the framing choice predicts the outcome more reliably than any other variable.
