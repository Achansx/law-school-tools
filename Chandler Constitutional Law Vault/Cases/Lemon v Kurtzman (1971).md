# Lemon v Kurtzman (1971)

## Memory Jogger

> Announced the three-part Lemon Test for Establishment Clause challenges -- secular purpose, non-religion-advancing primary effect, and no excessive entanglement -- but the test itself was largely abandoned by Kennedy v. Bremerton (2022).

---

## Facts

Two companion cases were consolidated before the Court, each challenging state statutes providing direct financial assistance to parochial elementary and secondary schools.

Pennsylvania enacted the Nonpublic Elementary and Secondary Education Act of 1968, authorizing state reimbursement to nonpublic schools for the cost of teachers' salaries, textbooks, and instructional materials in specified secular subjects (mathematics, modern foreign languages, physical science, and physical education). The state had entered contracts with approximately 1,181 nonpublic schools serving roughly 535,000 students -- over 20 percent of the state's student population -- of whom over 96 percent attended church-related schools, the vast majority affiliated with the Roman Catholic Church.

Rhode Island enacted the Salary Supplement Act of 1969, authorizing the state to pay directly to teachers in nonpublic elementary schools a supplement equal to 15 percent of their annual salary, provided the supplemented salary did not exceed the maximum paid to public school teachers. Teachers had to be certified, teach only subjects offered in public schools using only approved secular materials, and agree in writing not to teach religion while receiving the supplement. At trial, approximately $5 million in annual expenditures had been generated under Rhode Island's program; about 25 percent of elementary students in Rhode Island attended nonpublic schools.

---

## Procedural History

Both statutes were challenged on Establishment Clause grounds in federal district court proceedings. The district court in the Pennsylvania case upheld the statute. The district court in the Rhode Island case struck it down. The Supreme Court consolidated the cases on certiorari and reversed Pennsylvania while affirming Rhode Island, holding both statutes unconstitutional.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority:** Chief Justice Burger (joined by Douglas, Harlan, Stewart, White, Marshall, Blackmun; Powell recused)
- **Concurrence:** Justice Douglas (joined by Black); Justice Brennan (separate concurrence)
- **Dissent:** None (8-0 judgment)

---

## Holding

Both statutes violate the Establishment Clause because, even assuming a secular legislative purpose and no direct primary effect of advancing religion, each necessarily fosters excessive entanglement between government and religion. The character of religious schools (pervasively sectarian institutions), the nature of the direct financial aid (teacher salaries that cannot be mechanically separated from the institution's religious mission), and the resulting need for comprehensive and continuing government surveillance of religious instruction together create the kind of church-state relationship the Establishment Clause forbids. Additionally, state programs creating large annual appropriations flowing to sectarian institutions generate divisive political activity along religious lines -- itself an entanglement the First Amendment was designed to prevent.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Chief Justice Burger's opinion synthesizes prior Establishment Clause decisions into a three-part test: the statute must (1) have a secular legislative purpose, (2) have a primary effect that neither advances nor inhibits religion, and (3) not foster an excessive government entanglement with religion. Pennsylvania and Rhode Island satisfy prong one -- both legislatures acted to improve secular education. Primary effect is more doubtful, because the aid flows directly to pervasively sectarian institutions rather than to students or parents as in Everson (bus transportation) or Allen (secular textbooks). But the decisive ground is excessive entanglement. Parochial schools are not merely religious in atmosphere; religious instruction is their core purpose. A teacher's professional conduct cannot be inspected once and certified the way a secular textbook can; ongoing surveillance is necessary to ensure state-funded teachers do not inculcate religion. That surveillance, together with the annual political battles over appropriations, creates precisely the church-state fusion the Establishment Clause forbids.

### Concurrence (Douglas, joined by Black)

Douglas (joined by Black) writes separately to press a harder line than Burger's entanglement test. For Douglas, the "raison d'etre of parochial schools is the propagation of a religious faith," and any state effort to fund the secular side of such an institution necessarily subsidizes the religious side because the two cannot be pried apart in a pervasively sectarian setting. The surveillance that might guarantee strict secular use is itself the problem: it "puts a public investigator into every classroom and entails a pervasive monitoring of these church agencies by the secular authorities." If surveillance is skipped, the "zeal of religious proselytizers promises to carry the day and make a shambles of the Establishment Clause"; if it happens, government becomes entangled in the daily operations of the church's educational mission. Douglas also sees a Free Exercise cost running alongside the Establishment Clause harm: "when taxpayers of many faiths are required to contribute money for the propagation of one faith, the Free Exercise Clause is infringed." The upshot is a categorical rule more sweeping than Burger's: direct aid to pervasively sectarian institutions is constitutionally suspect regardless of how carefully the state attempts to segregate religious and secular spending.

The Douglas concurrence also foreshadows the doctrinal path that Lemon's three-prong framework later takes. By the time of Agostini (1997) and Zelman (2002), the Court had relaxed the primary-effect prong for aid funneled through parents' private choices, but Douglas's point — that direct institutional subsidies are structurally different from indirect aid to students — survives as the dividing line between post-Lemon cases that uphold aid (vouchers, textbook loans, transportation reimbursements, remedial services under strict protocols) and those that strike it down (salary supplements, direct operational grants, tuition reimbursements paid to the institution).

### Concurrence (Brennan)

Brennan's concurrence reaches back to Madisonian first principles and argues that the three-prong test works only if it is understood as a structural rule about the proper relation of church and state, not a mechanical checklist of "purpose" and "effect." Relying on his prior concurrences in Schempp and Walz, Brennan identifies three categorical prohibitions the Religion Clauses enforce: the government may not (a) serve the "essentially religious activities of religious institutions," (b) employ "the organs of government for essentially religious purposes," or (c) use "essentially religious means to serve governmental ends, where secular means would suffice." In his words, "when the secular and religious institutions become involved in such a manner, there inhere in the relationship precisely those dangers — as much to church as to state — which the Framers feared would subvert religious liberty and the strength of a system of secular government."

For Brennan, Pennsylvania and Rhode Island fall on all three counts: direct institutional subsidies underwrite the church's educational mission, use state appropriations committees as arenas in which religious groups must compete for aid, and accomplish a secular goal (quality education) through a religious mechanism when state-run schools already exist. Brennan also flags the "political divisiveness" prong as constitutionally distinct from mere entanglement: "political division along religious lines was one of the principal evils against which the First Amendment was intended to protect," and annual appropriations battles structurally generate exactly that. Unlike Burger's opinion, Brennan is explicit that the structural harm is independently sufficient; without it, purpose-and-effect analysis would degenerate into ad hoc balancing.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

1. **Hypo:** A state program reimburses parochial high schools for 100 percent of the salary of any teacher who instructs a course in Latin, a subject taught at both religious and secular private schools. **Why:** Direct salary reimbursement to the religious institution itself, for a teacher whose conduct cannot be mechanically verified to exclude religious influence, triggers excessive entanglement under Lemon's third prong. Even under Agostini v Felton (1997) — which relaxed Lemon's primary-effect prong for neutral aid programs where public employees provide remedial services on parochial premises — direct salary subsidies to the parochial institution itself remain constitutionally problematic because the aid is not neutral on its face, flows to the institution rather than to students or parents, and still requires the same classroom surveillance that drove Lemon. Mitchell v Helms (2000) upheld lending secular educational materials directly to parochial schools, but only with a divided rationale and only because the materials themselves were neutral, supplementary, and not divertible to religious use. A teacher's professional conduct is none of those.

2. **Hypo:** A state comptroller refuses to reimburse a parochial school because state auditors found religious content in a state-funded teacher's secular-subject class, and the school sues claiming the refusal violates its own religious autonomy. **Why:** The very need for the audit demonstrates the excessive entanglement Lemon condemns; the government cannot win either way -- fund without oversight (Establishment risk) or audit with oversight (entanglement risk). This is the famous Lemon "Catch-22." Justice Scalia lampooned the test in Lamb's Chapel v Center Moriches Union Free School District (1993) as a "ghoul in a late-night horror movie" — "repeatedly killed and buried, Lemon stalks our Establishment Clause jurisprudence once again," and lower courts "can forget or ignore it in any year they like," producing capricious results that no litigant can predict. Scalia's critique sharpens precisely in audit-hypo fact patterns: the statute creates a structural no-win for everyone involved, which for Scalia demonstrates that Lemon's entanglement prong is not law-like enough to control results.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

3. **Hypo:** A state provides each student -- regardless of school -- a refundable tax credit for secular textbooks purchased for use in any accredited school. **Why:** The aid flows to individual students, not to religious institutions; the analogy to Everson (bus transportation) and Allen (textbooks to students) would likely survive even Lemon's primary-effect prong. Zelman v Simmons-Harris (2002) expressly upheld an Ohio school-voucher program using exactly this "private-choice" framework: when aid reaches religious schools "only as a result of the genuine and independent choices of private individuals," the state has not itself advanced religion, and the program is formally neutral with respect to religion, so Lemon's primary-effect prong is satisfied. The tax-credit hypo is Zelman redrawn: the state is not directly subsidizing any institution; individual families, not the state, direct the funds. Under Zelman-Agostini doctrine the program is constitutional even if most students spend the credit at religious schools, provided the religious option is not structurally preferred.

4. **Hypo:** A state university provides unrestricted grants to all registered student organizations, including a Christian group that uses some funds for devotional meetings. **Why:** Rosenberger v Rector (1995) holds that viewpoint-neutral funding to student groups does not violate the Establishment Clause; Lemon's entanglement concern is far weaker when the government is not directly subsidizing a pervasively sectarian institution. Rosenberger actually turns Lemon on its head in one direction: refusing to fund a religious student publication on the same terms as other publications violates the Free Speech Clause as viewpoint discrimination, and Establishment Clause concerns cannot justify that discrimination when the aid is neutral and flows through the students' own choice of organization. Under Kennedy v Bremerton's "historical practices and understandings" approach, the result is the same but reached differently: public universities have a long tradition of subsidizing student expressive activity, including religious student groups, and the relevant historical practice does not classify such subsidies as establishments. The fact that both doctrinal frameworks reach the same result in Rosenberger-type cases partly explains why the doctrinal shift in Kennedy v Bremerton did not upset the private-choice line of post-Lemon cases.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

5. **Hypo:** A state provides Title I remedial reading services using public school teachers who are stationed on-site at parochial schools but under strict protocols prohibiting any religious activity. **Why:** Agostini v Felton (1997) partially overruled Aguilar v Felton (1985) and allowed exactly this; the case is hard because it requires resolving whether on-site public teachers create excessive entanglement or whether the strict separation protocols are adequate — the precise issue on which the Court shifted between 1985 and 1997. Agostini collapsed the three Lemon prongs into a refined two-inquiry test: the Court continues to ask whether the government has a secular purpose and whether the aid has the primary effect of advancing religion, but the entanglement inquiry is folded into the effect prong (rather than standing as a separate bar) and is softened by a presumption that public employees working in religious-school buildings will not inculcate religion absent evidence to the contrary. The fence-sitter quality of the hypo is that under pre-Agostini Aguilar the aid would fail, under post-Agostini doctrine it survives, and under Kennedy v Bremerton's (2022) move to "historical practices and understandings" the inquiry would center on whether the Founding-era understanding of Establishment treated state-paid remedial services as an establishment — a question Bremerton itself did not resolve, so the correct answer depends on which historical frame the reviewing court adopts. For a professor, this is the richest illustration that Lemon's doctrinal trajectory was an arc of softening before Kennedy v Bremerton replaced the test with a tradition-based inquiry that still leaves the hard cases hard.

## Critique

**Progressive critique (incoherence and historical bias):** The progressive critique does not defend Lemon as elegant doctrine; it defends Lemon as the workable compromise that preserved robust church-state separation during the post-Everson era, and laments its abandonment. But even sympathetic progressive scholars (Kathleen Sullivan, Douglas Laycock) concede that the "excessive entanglement" prong is structurally circular: almost any state regulation of church-state interaction is itself an "entanglement," so the question of how much is "excessive" ends up being driven by unarticulated priors about acceptable levels of state-religion interaction. A deeper progressive line of critique, articulated by scholars like Steven Green and Leo Pfeffer, acknowledges that Lemon's aid-to-parochial-schools cases were driven in part by mid-twentieth-century anti-Catholic sentiment — the same sentiment that produced the Blaine Amendments in state constitutions — so that the doctrine's supposed neutrality quietly targeted Catholic educational institutions while leaving equivalent state aid to historically Protestant institutions (e.g., military chaplaincies, historic-preservation grants to colonial churches) comparatively undisturbed. This is an uncomfortable concession for progressives because it undercuts the appeal to Lemon's evenhandedness: the same test that produced Lemon and Aguilar also produced Meek v Pittenger (invalidating textbook loans to parochial-school students) and Wolman v Walter (invalidating field-trip funding), lines of decision that even defenders of strict separation now view as doctrinally difficult to reconcile.

**Originalist/textualist critique (law-like unpredictability and the ghoul):** Justice Scalia's attack on Lemon in Lamb's Chapel v Center Moriches Union Free School District (1993) remains the paradigm originalist/textualist critique: "Like some ghoul in a late-night horror movie that repeatedly sits up in its grave and shuffles abroad, after being repeatedly killed and buried, Lemon stalks our Establishment Clause jurisprudence once again." Scalia's objection is not merely that the test is often ignored; it is that a doctrinal rule whose prongs are regularly overridden by "context" and "purpose" judgments fails the basic requirement of law-like predictability. A lower court cannot apply Lemon consistently because each of its prongs turns on subjective characterizations — What is "the" primary effect of a statute that has multiple effects? How much entanglement is "excessive"? — and the Supreme Court itself has applied the prongs inconsistently across Agostini, Zelman, Mitchell, Van Orden, and McCreary County. Originalist critics also argue that Lemon was not grounded in the original public meaning of the Establishment Clause, which a historical inquiry (per Bremerton) would anchor to specific Founding-era practices such as established state churches, compulsory church attendance, and mandatory tithing to a designated denomination — practices far removed from incidental aid to secular education at religious institutions.

**Doctrinal trajectory:** Lemon's reach has been progressively narrowed since 1971. Agostini v Felton (1997) collapsed the three prongs into a reformulated two-step inquiry. Zelman v Simmons-Harris (2002) carved out a wide private-choice exception under which neutral voucher programs survive even when most aid flows to religious schools. Van Orden v Perry (2005) and McCreary County v ACLU (2005) splintered the Court on whether Lemon controlled passive-display cases. American Legion v American Humanist Association (2019) declined to apply Lemon to a long-standing cross memorial, signaling that the test was no longer controlling in monument cases. Kennedy v Bremerton School District (2022) formally abandoned Lemon, directing lower courts to interpret the Establishment Clause "by reference to historical practices and understandings." Lemon's three prongs nevertheless still influence lower courts by habit and by the weight of fifty years of citation; the pending doctrinal work is converting the new historical-practices framework from a slogan into a predictable rule of decision, which the Court has not yet done.

## Key Points

- The three-part Lemon Test (purpose, effect, entanglement) dominated Establishment Clause jurisprudence for 50 years.
- The "Negative" Midpage treatment reflects that Kennedy v. Bremerton (2022) explicitly abandoned Lemon in favor of a historical-practices approach.
- The "Catch-22" of the entanglement prong: to verify that state funds are used only for secular instruction requires exactly the kind of surveillance that creates entanglement; this structural tension exposed the test's instability.
- Professors typically teach Lemon alongside Kennedy v. Bremerton to show the Court's dramatic doctrinal shift in Establishment Clause methodology.
