# Grutter v Bollinger (2003)

## Memory Jogger

> O'Connor's majority upheld the University of Michigan Law School's race-conscious admissions program, holding that student body diversity is a compelling interest under strict scrutiny and that the Law School's holistic, individualized review process was narrowly tailored -- but coupled the holding with an expectation that race-conscious admissions would no longer be necessary in 25 years. The case was effectively overruled by Students for Fair Admissions v Harvard (2023).

---

## Facts

Barbara Grutter, a white Michigan resident, was denied admission to the University of Michigan Law School. The Law School's admissions policy sought to admit a diverse student body and considered race as one factor among many, aiming to achieve a "critical mass" of underrepresented minority students. Admissions officers conducted individualized, holistic reviews of each applicant's file; no applicant's race was the determinative factor, nor did the school use a quota. Grutter challenged the program under the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment, arguing that the consideration of race constituted unconstitutional racial discrimination. The Law School defended the program on the ground that student body diversity is a compelling governmental interest, relying primarily on Justice Powell's solo opinion in Regents of the University of California v Bakke (1978).

---

## Procedural History

The district court held the Law School's admissions program unconstitutional. The Sixth Circuit reversed en banc, upholding the program under strict scrutiny. The Supreme Court granted certiorari, deciding Grutter alongside its companion case Gratz v Bollinger (2003), and affirmed 5-4.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority:** O'Connor (joined by Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer) -- student body diversity compelling; holistic review narrowly tailored
- **Concurrence (Ginsburg, joined by Breyer):** Would not foreclose all future race-conscious admissions absent specific sunset date
- **Dissent (Rehnquist, joined by Kennedy, Scalia, and Thomas):** Argued the Law School's critical-mass rationale was a cover for a de facto quota; no evidence of genuine individualized review
- **Dissent (Kennedy):** Agreed with Rehnquist's critique that critical mass functioned as a numerical target
- **Dissent (Scalia, joined in part by Thomas):** Argued the Constitution forbids racial preferences in any context; Michigan could choose a diverse student body or an elite law school, not both at state expense
- **Dissent (Thomas, joined in part by Scalia):** Argued the law school had no compelling interest in maintaining its elite status on the backs of racial preferences; would apply strict scrutiny with full rigor

---

## Holding

Student body diversity is a compelling governmental interest that can justify race-conscious admissions in public higher education. The University of Michigan Law School's holistic, individualized review process -- in which race operated as a "plus" factor but was never the defining characteristic of an application, and no seat was reserved for minority students -- was narrowly tailored to achieve that compelling interest. Race-conscious admissions programs must, however, be limited in time; the Court expressed the expectation that they would no longer be necessary in 25 years.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Justice O'Connor's majority opinion formally endorsed Justice Powell's solo Bakke opinion -- the only basis on which a Court majority had ever addressed the constitutionality of affirmative action -- and declared student body diversity a compelling interest. The majority grounded this holding in the documented educational benefits of diversity: cross-racial understanding, reduced stereotyping, better preparation for a diverse workforce, and the cultivation of a diverse set of leaders for public life. These benefits were substantiated by extensive social science evidence and the amicus briefs of major corporations and military leaders.

On narrow tailoring, the majority distinguished the Law School's holistic review from the medical school quota invalidated in Bakke. The Law School gave individualized consideration to every applicant, treating race as one of many "soft" variables rather than a fixed numeric advantage. The majority stressed that the Constitution required programs to treat applicants as individuals, and the Law School's approach satisfied this requirement because race was never the decisive factor for any particular applicant.

The majority also inserted the sunset expectation -- an observation that race-conscious admissions should become unnecessary within approximately 25 years -- which would later inform the Court's decision in Students for Fair Admissions v Harvard (2023) to hold that such programs had outlived their constitutional justification.
