# Green v County School Board (1968)

## Memory Jogger

> Brown II imposed an affirmative duty to desegregate "root and branch" -- a freedom-of-choice plan that produces no real integration is constitutionally inadequate and must give way to more effective alternatives such as geographic zoning.

---

## Facts

New Kent County, Virginia, is a rural county of approximately 4,500 residents, roughly half of whom are Black. The county operated only two schools: the New Kent school (serving the east side) and the George W. Watkins school (serving the west side), together enrolling about 1,300 students -- 740 Black and 550 white. Before the Brown decisions, the county maintained a fully segregated dual system: New Kent school for white students only, Watkins school for Black students only. The segregation extended beyond student enrollment to faculty, staff, transportation routes, extracurricular activities, and every facility. This dual system was no accident; it was the deliberate product of state action through local school officials. Despite the absence of residential segregation in the county (both races lived throughout the area), the district maintained racial separation by running twenty-one overlapping bus routes that deliberately transported students to their racially assigned schools.

After Brown, the county continued operating its segregated system under state resistance-to-desegregation statutes. The Pupil Placement Act, in effect until 1966, placed assignment authority in a state board that required students to affirmatively apply for reassignment, effectively keeping the dual system intact. By September 1964, not one Black student had applied to attend New Kent, and no white student had applied to attend Watkins. In 1965 -- fourteen years after Brown I and thirteen years after Brown II -- the school board finally adopted a "freedom-of-choice" plan allowing students to apply annually for their school of choice. After three years of operation, the plan failed: not one white child had chosen to attend Watkins; only 115 of 740 Black students had chosen to attend New Kent; and 85 percent of Black children remained in the all-Black Watkins school.

---

## Procedural History

Parents of Black schoolchildren brought suit in federal district court challenging the school board's desegregation plan as inadequate. The district court accepted the freedom-of-choice plan as constitutionally sufficient. The Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit affirmed but required certain modifications. The Supreme Court granted certiorari and reversed, holding the plan constitutionally inadequate on its face given the results.

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority:** Justice Brennan (for a unanimous Court; all nine Justices joined)
- **Concurrence:** None
- **Dissent:** None

## Holding

School boards that previously operated state-compelled dual systems have an affirmative constitutional duty under Brown II to take whatever steps are necessary to convert to a unitary, nonracial system in which racial discrimination is eliminated "root and branch." That duty is not satisfied by mere adoption of a facially neutral plan; the burden is on the school board to come forward with a plan that "promises realistically to work, and promises realistically to work now." A freedom-of-choice plan is not a "sacred talisman" -- it is acceptable only if it effectively achieves a unitary system in practice. Where a plan has demonstrably failed, and where more effective alternatives (such as geographic zoning) are reasonably available, the freedom-of-choice plan is constitutionally unacceptable and must be replaced.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Justice Brennan's unanimous opinion interprets Brown II's "all deliberate speed" mandate as imposing a present, affirmative desegregation duty, not merely an obligation of good-faith gradualism. The opinion is notable for its results orientation: the Court does not hold freedom-of-choice plans unconstitutional per se, but it does require that any plan be measured by whether it achieves a unitary system in practice, not just in theory. After three years, the New Kent County plan had produced negligible integration -- 85 percent of Black students still attended the all-Black Watkins school. The Court holds that when results are this poor and when alternative mechanisms (like geographic attendance zones, which would automatically integrate a county with no residential segregation) are readily available, the board's choice of a demonstrably ineffective plan is constitutionally impermissible. The opinion identifies six specific factors by which to measure whether a dual system has been dismantled: student body, faculty, staff, transportation, extracurricular activities, and facilities -- later known as the "Green factors."

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

1. **Hypo:** A rural Southern county with no residential segregation adopts a voluntary transfer program that, after five years, has moved only 3 percent of Black students to formerly all-white schools, while the district could easily draw geographic attendance zones that would produce a fully integrated student body. **Why:** The fact pattern mirrors New Kent County exactly -- no residential segregation means geographic zoning would work immediately; the plan fails Green's "promises realistically to work now" standard.
<!-- ENRICH: Connect to Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg (1971), which authorized busing as a remedy when geographic zones were inadequate due to residential segregation. -->

2. **Hypo:** A school district previously under a desegregation decree proposes a magnet-school program that will leave 70 percent of Black students in schools that are 90 percent Black, when a geographic zoning alternative would produce 60/40 integration. **Why:** Green requires the board to choose the more effective alternative when a reasonably available option promises speedier and more effective conversion; the magnet program fails on its face.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

3. **Hypo:** A metropolitan school district with severe residential segregation adopts a freedom-of-choice plan that, due to concentrated poverty patterns, also fails to produce racial balance -- but no geographic attendance zone would achieve meaningful integration without cross-district busing. **Why:** Milliken v. Bradley holds that interdistrict remedies require interdistrict violations; where residential patterns are the cause, Green's demand for "more effective alternatives" cannot compel a remedy that exceeds the scope of the constitutional violation.
<!-- ENRICH: Trace the tension between Green's result-orientation and Milliken's constraint on remedial power. -->

4. **Hypo:** A school board that has achieved unitary status (court decree dissolved) voluntarily adopts a race-neutral geographic attendance plan; later demographic shifts produce racially identifiable schools. **Why:** Once a district achieves unitary status and is released from court supervision, Green's affirmative duty no longer applies to subsequent demographic imbalance that does not trace to state-imposed segregation; contrast with de jure vs. de facto distinction.
<!-- ENRICH: Connect to Parents Involved (2007), where the Court held that the remedial rationale does not justify race-conscious assignment after unitary status is achieved. -->

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

5. **Hypo:** A formerly segregated urban district with extensive residential segregation adopts a freedom-of-choice plan, and after five years only 15 percent of Black students have transferred to formerly all-white schools -- but the evidence is disputed about whether more effective race-neutral alternatives (redrawing zones, magnet programs) are actually feasible given housing patterns. **Why:** Green requires the board to use a more effective plan only if one is "reasonably available"; when feasibility is genuinely contested, the standard is fact-intensive and the court must weigh the board's explanation for preferring apparently less effective methods.

## Key Points

- Establishes the six "Green factors" (student body, faculty, staff, transportation, extracurricular activities, facilities) as the criteria for measuring whether a dual system has been dismantled.
- Converts Brown II's "all deliberate speed" standard into a demand for immediate, effective desegregation -- ending the era of token compliance.
- Freedom-of-choice plans are not per se unconstitutional but must be measured by results, not intent; if more effective alternatives exist, the board must adopt them.
- The "Caution" Midpage treatment reflects later cases (Milliken, Parents Involved) that limited Green's reach.
