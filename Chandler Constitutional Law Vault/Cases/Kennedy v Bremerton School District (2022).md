# Kennedy v Bremerton School District (2022)

## Memory Jogger

> A public school football coach's brief, personal, postgame prayer at midfield is protected by the Free Exercise and Free Speech Clauses, and the Establishment Clause does not require the school to suppress it; the Court formally abandons the Lemon test in favor of historical-practices analysis.

---

## Facts

Joseph Kennedy served as an assistant football coach at Bremerton High School, a public school in Washington State. After games, whether or not players joined him, Kennedy walked to midfield and briefly knelt in personal prayer. Over time some players and even opposing team members began to join. The school district initially accommodated his practice but later directed him to stop, concerned that his conduct could be perceived as state-endorsed religious exercise in violation of the Establishment Clause. Kennedy complied with directives to stop praying during team huddles and while delivering motivational talks, but continued to engage in quiet, personal prayer at midfield after the game clock expired and players had dispersed. The district placed him on administrative leave and declined to rehire him.

Kennedy sued under the Free Exercise, Free Speech, and (statutorily) Title VII provisions, seeking reinstatement and a declaration that his prayer was constitutionally protected.

---

## Procedural History

The district court granted summary judgment to the school district on free-exercise and free-speech claims, reasoning that the Establishment Clause justified the district's suppression of the prayer. The Ninth Circuit affirmed. The Supreme Court granted certiorari.

## Holding

The Free Exercise and Free Speech Clauses protect Coach Kennedy's postgame prayer, which was personal religious observance rather than government speech, and which did not cause coercion of students. The Establishment Clause does not require the school district to suppress the prayer and cannot be used as a defense for the adverse employment action. In reaching this conclusion, the Court rejects the Lemon v Kurtzman test, including its endorsement-test refinement, and directs lower courts to interpret the Establishment Clause by reference to historical practices and understandings. The school district's rule that Kennedy refrain from "demonstrative religious activity" at the conclusion of games did not satisfy strict scrutiny in light of a genuine religious exercise and personal speech claim.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Justice Gorsuch's majority opinion resolves three clauses in turn. On free exercise, the school's rule targeted religious conduct because Kennedy's prayer was distinctive, personal, and motivated by sincerely held beliefs, and the rule was not neutral or generally applicable in its operation. On free speech, Kennedy's postgame midfield prayer was private speech rather than government speech: it occurred when players had dispersed and teachers were briefly released, fell outside his official duties, and did not bear the school's imprimatur. On establishment, the Court formally abandons Lemon v Kurtzman and its endorsement-test formulation, holding that the clause must be interpreted by reference to "historical practices and understandings." There is no historical tradition of suppressing personal religious exercise by government employees in this type of setting. The majority distinguishes Lee v Weisman and related cases by emphasizing that Kennedy's prayer was neither coerced nor school-sponsored, and that a reasonable observer framework no longer governs Establishment Clause analysis after the Court's abandonment of Lemon.

## Key Points

- Formally abandons Lemon v Kurtzman and the endorsement test; substitutes historical-practices-and-understandings analysis for Establishment Clause questions.
- Distinguishes Lee v Weisman by emphasizing the absence of coercion and school sponsorship.
- Affirms that the Establishment Clause cannot be used as a sword to suppress private religious exercise by government employees.
- Continues the Court's post-2017 Free Exercise expansion alongside Trinity Lutheran, Espinoza, and Carson v Makin.
- Chandler emphasis: Kennedy is the pivotal decision in the modern religion-clauses trilogy and the clearest announcement that Lemon is no longer controlling.
