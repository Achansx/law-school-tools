# Marbury v Madison (1803)

## Memory Jogger

> The foundational judicial review case: Marshall struck down a provision of the Judiciary Act of 1789 while ruling against Federalist appointee Marbury, establishing that an act of Congress repugnant to the Constitution is void -- without giving Jefferson any occasion to defy the ruling.

---

## Facts

After losing the 1800 election to Thomas Jefferson, the lame-duck Federalist Congress enacted the Judiciary Act of 1801, creating sixteen new circuit court judgeships. President Adams rushed to fill these "midnight judge" positions before leaving office. The commissions were signed and sealed but -- through a logistical failure by outgoing Secretary of State John Marshall (who was simultaneously being appointed Chief Justice) -- several were never delivered. Jefferson, once inaugurated, directed his new Secretary of State James Madison not to deliver the remaining commissions.

William Marbury, one of the appointees, filed directly in the Supreme Court seeking a writ of mandamus ordering Madison to deliver his commission as Justice of the Peace for the District of Columbia. He invoked Section 13 of the Judiciary Act of 1789, which appeared to grant the Supreme Court original jurisdiction to issue writs of mandamus against federal officers.

The constitutional provisions at issue were Article III (which defines the Supreme Court's original and appellate jurisdiction) and the Supremacy Clause. The structural question was whether an Act of Congress could expand the Court's original jurisdiction beyond the categories enumerated in Article III, Section 2.

---

## Procedural History

Marbury filed the action directly in the Supreme Court as an original proceeding -- there was no lower court ruling or appeal below. He argued the Court had original jurisdiction under Section 13 of the Judiciary Act of 1789, which stated the Supreme Court could issue writs of mandamus "to any courts appointed, or persons holding office, under the authority of the United States."

The Jefferson administration refused to appear or respond. Marshall had to decide whether to issue the mandamus (inviting Jefferson's defiance and a potential constitutional crisis) or find another path. His solution was characteristically audacious: he held that Marbury was entitled to his commission and that a remedy existed in principle, but that the Supreme Court lacked jurisdiction to provide that remedy because Section 13 of the Judiciary Act was unconstitutional.

---

- **Concurrence:** None

## Holding

The Court held (1) that Marbury had a legal right to his commission, which was complete upon the President's signature and the Secretary of State's seal; (2) that withholding the commission was a legal injury for which the law provides a remedy; but (3) that the Supreme Court lacked jurisdiction to issue the writ because Section 13 of the Judiciary Act of 1789, insofar as it purported to grant the Court original jurisdiction to issue mandamus against federal officers, conflicted with Article III's enumeration of the Court's original jurisdiction and was therefore unconstitutional and void. The case established the power of federal courts to review acts of Congress and declare those that conflict with the Constitution void.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion (Marshall, C.J.)

Marshall organized the opinion around three questions, each answered in sequence.

**Question One: Does Marbury have a right to the commission?** Marshall concluded yes. An appointment is made when the President signs the commission; the commission is complete when the Secretary of State affixes the seal. Delivery is a ministerial act that cannot affect the vesting of the right. Madison's refusal to deliver was, in the Court's view, not "warranted by law" and "violative of a vested legal right."

**Question Two: If Marbury has a right, does the law afford him a remedy?** Marshall deployed the right-remedy alignment principle. "The very essence of civil liberty certainly consists in the right of every individual to claim the protection of the laws, whenever he receives an injury." Citing Blackstone, Marshall invoked the axiom: "where there is a legal right, there is also a legal remedy by suit or action at law, whenever that right is invaded." The United States is "a government of laws, and not of men" -- a phrase Marshall treated as the axiom of republican government. Without a judicial remedy for every legal wrong, this self-description would be hollow.

Marshall drew a distinction that planted the seed of the political question doctrine. Not every executive act is judicially reviewable. When executive officers exercise discretion -- choosing policy, exercising judgment committed by law to their exclusive discretion -- courts provide no review. These are acts "only politically examinable." But when a specific legal duty is assigned by statute, and individual rights depend on its performance, the injured party has a right to a judicial remedy. Madison's duty to deliver the commission was ministerial, not discretionary. This categorization enabled Marshall to lecture Jefferson and Madison on their lawlessness without actually ordering them to do anything.

**Question Three: Is mandamus from the Supreme Court the appropriate remedy?** This is where Marshall executed his tactical masterstroke. He held that Article III defines the Supreme Court's original jurisdiction exhaustively: the Court has original jurisdiction over cases affecting Ambassadors, other public Ministers and Consuls, and cases in which a State is a party. Congress cannot expand this list by legislation.

The argument proceeded from constitutional structure: "The constitution is either a superior, paramount law, unchangeable by ordinary means, or it is on a level with ordinary legislative acts, and like other acts, is alterable when the legislature shall please to alter it." There is no middle position. If the Constitution is supreme, an act of Congress that contradicts it must be void. "Certainly all those who have framed written constitutions contemplate them as forming the fundamental and paramount law of the nation, and consequently the theory of every such government must be, that an act of the legislature, repugnant to the constitution, is void."

**Judicial Review.** Courts must apply the law. When two rules conflict, courts must decide which governs. When a statute conflicts with the Constitution, the Constitution must control -- otherwise the Constitution could be overridden by ordinary legislation, which would reduce it to "a solemn mockery." The judicial oath to support the Constitution reinforces this: if courts can only apply constitutional provisions when the legislature chooses to honor them, the oath is meaningless. "It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is."

**The Tactical Genius.** Marshall chose a case with exquisite care. He could assert constitutional supremacy over Congress without ordering anything against Jefferson (the ruling went against the Federalist plaintiff). He delivered a stinging rebuke of Jefferson's lawlessness in the first two questions while stripping the Court of jurisdiction in the third, denying Jefferson any pretext to defy the judgment. As Professor Chandler emphasizes: no name given to Marshall's strategy by admirers or critics could change the fact that it was a political masterstroke as much as a legal argument.

**The Statutory Interpretation Subtext.** Marshall's reading of Section 13 is contestable. The provision may have been granting mandamus authority only in the context of appellate review of lower court cases, not as a freestanding grant of original jurisdiction. If so, there was no constitutional conflict to resolve, and Marbury's mandamus petition simply failed on statutory grounds. The suggestion that Marshall deliberately misread the statute to create the constitutional issue he wanted to resolve remains one of the most persistent critiques of the opinion.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

1. **Hypo:** Congress passes a statute granting the Supreme Court original jurisdiction to hear all civil rights claims against state officials. A plaintiff files directly in the Supreme Court invoking this statute. **Why:** Under Marbury, Congress cannot expand the Supreme Court's original jurisdiction beyond the Article III list. The statute is unconstitutional as applied to original jurisdiction (though appellate jurisdiction expansion would be permissible). The plaintiff must start in a lower federal court or state court.

2. **Hypo:** A lower federal court faces a statute that plainly requires it to dismiss a case against a federal agency, but the dismissal would deprive the plaintiff of any forum and violate a constitutional guarantee of access to courts. **Why:** Under Marbury's core logic, when a statute conflicts with the Constitution, the court must follow the Constitution. "It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is." The court applies the constitutional requirement and disregards the conflicting statute.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

3. **Hypo:** Congress passes a statute expanding the Supreme Court's appellate jurisdiction to hear appeals from state courts in all cases involving federal treaty rights. A state court litigant appeals a treaty issue to the Court under this statute. **Why:** Marbury's holding is specific to original jurisdiction, which Article III defines as a fixed list. Appellate jurisdiction is expressly subject to "such Exceptions, and under such Regulations as the Congress shall make" (Article III, Section 2). Congressional expansion of appellate jurisdiction raises no constitutional conflict and falls outside Marbury's rule.

4. **Hypo:** The President decides to pardon a convicted criminal. A victim's family challenges the pardon in court, arguing the President lacked power to pardon in this case. **Why:** Under Marbury's political question distinction, the pardon power is a discretionary executive power committed entirely to presidential judgment. There is no specific ministerial legal duty at stake, and no individual legal right that depends on the President exercising the pardon in a particular way. This is a "political" act "only politically examinable," and courts provide no review.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

5. **Hypo:** Congress passes a statute directing the Supreme Court to rehear any case in which the President certifies that the ruling impairs national security. A President invokes the statute after an adverse ruling in a detention case. **Why:** This conflicts with the judicial power vested in Article III courts (Congress can't make the executive a de facto appellate body over the judiciary), but the statute does not technically expand original jurisdiction or directly violate any specific constitutional provision Marshall identified. Marbury's logic supports striking it down (Congress can't override judicial judgments), but the Court would need to articulate a constitutional basis beyond Marbury's narrow original-jurisdiction holding. The result seems clear but the doctrinal path is genuinely uncertain.

---

## Critique

Marbury is simultaneously the most celebrated and most critiqued opinion in American constitutional history. The decision's flaws are structural, not incidental.

The core argument -- that a written constitution implies judicial supremacy in constitutional interpretation -- proves less than Marshall claimed. The argument from constitutional supremacy shows only that the Constitution is paramount law; it does not show that courts, rather than Congress or the President, must be the final arbiter of what the Constitution means. Jefferson and Lincoln both disputed judicial supremacy on precisely this ground: each argued that coordinate branches had their own obligation to interpret the Constitution independently, and that the Court's judgment was authoritative only on the parties before it, not on the constitutional question itself as a matter of general government conduct.

The opinion's use of the right-remedy principle is rhetorically powerful but analytically incomplete. Marshall announces that every legal right implies a judicial remedy, then quietly limits the principle to ministerial executive acts. He never fully explains why discretionary executive acts are exempt from this principle, or how courts are to determine which acts are ministerial and which discretionary. The political question doctrine planted in Marbury has been an enduring source of confusion precisely because Marshall never clearly delimited what makes a question "political."

The tactical elements of the decision raise their own concerns. If Marshall deliberately misread Section 13 (as the statutory interpretation critique suggests), then Marbury establishes judicial review through a manufactured constitutional conflict that may not have actually existed. A principle of constitutional law derived from a deliberate doctrinal detour is on shaky foundations.

From a democratic-legitimacy perspective, Marbury's critics have always questioned why nine (or in 1803, four) unelected judges should have final say over constitutional questions that directly affect the political branches' ability to govern. Other constitutional democracies -- the United Kingdom, Australia, Canada before 1982 -- have operated without strong judicial review, relying on legislative supremacy as the cornerstone of democratic governance.

## Key Points

- Established the foundational principle of judicial review: federal courts have the power to declare acts of Congress unconstitutional and void
- The argument from constitutional supremacy shows only that the Constitution is paramount law, not that courts are the exclusive interpreters -- this remains contested
- Distinguished ministerial executive acts (judicially reviewable) from discretionary executive acts (political questions, not reviewable) -- this distinction is the seed of the political question doctrine
- Marshall's tactical genius: he chose a case where he could establish judicial review at no institutional cost, because the ruling went against the Federalist plaintiff and ordered nothing against Jefferson
- The "right-remedy alignment" principle -- where there is a legal right, there is a legal remedy -- recurs throughout constitutional law as both an axiom and a contested claim
- Stuart v. Laird (decided the same week) upheld the Repeal Act of 1802 and circuit riding, immediately bounding Marbury's assertiveness through political accommodation
- Watch for Marbury "exceptions" throughout the course: courts that nominally follow Marbury while limiting justiciability through standing, ripeness, political question doctrine, or equitable remedies
- The decision does not address the power to strike down state laws (that question was resolved in Martin v. Hunter's Lessee, 1816) or the power of lower federal courts to review constitutional questions
