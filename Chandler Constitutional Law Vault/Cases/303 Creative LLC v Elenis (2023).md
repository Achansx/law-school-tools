# 303 Creative LLC v Elenis (2023)

## Memory Jogger

> The First Amendment forbids a State from using its public accommodations law to force a website designer to create custom expressive content that communicates a message she does not wish to convey.

---

## Facts

Lorie Smith owns 303 Creative LLC, a Colorado-based graphic and website design business. She sought to expand her services to include custom wedding websites but objected on religious grounds to designing sites celebrating same-sex weddings. She planned to post a statement on her business website explaining that, while she would gladly work with any client, she would not create custom sites that "contradict biblical truth" regardless of the client's sexual orientation. Colorado's Anti-Discrimination Act (CADA) prohibits public accommodations from denying the full and equal enjoyment of their goods or services on the basis of sexual orientation (the "Accommodation Clause") and from publishing any communication indicating that a person's patronage is unwelcome because of sexual orientation (the "Communication Clause").

Smith filed a pre-enforcement challenge, arguing that applying CADA to compel her to design same-sex wedding websites would violate the Free Speech and Free Exercise Clauses of the First Amendment. The parties stipulated to key facts before the Tenth Circuit: that Smith's wedding websites would be expressive, customized, tailored to each couple's story, and would celebrate and promote the couple's wedding and the message of marriage she wishes to convey.

---

## Procedural History

The District Court granted summary judgment for Colorado. The Tenth Circuit affirmed, holding that CADA survived strict scrutiny because Colorado had a compelling interest in ensuring equal access to publicly available goods and services and the law was narrowly tailored because Smith's services were unique (no one else could produce Smith's particular expressive products). The Tenth Circuit assumed the law compelled Smith's speech but held that compelling interest and tailoring justified the burden.

The Supreme Court granted certiorari on the Free Speech Clause question only (declining the Free Exercise question) and reversed.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority:** Gorsuch (joined by Roberts, C.J., Thomas, Alito, Kavanaugh, Barrett) — 6-3
- **Dissent:** Sotomayor (joined by Kagan, Jackson)

---

## Holding

The First Amendment prohibits Colorado from forcing a website designer to create expressive designs speaking messages with which the designer disagrees. Where a public accommodations law would compel a creator of custom expressive content to communicate a message she does not wish to convey, the law violates the Free Speech Clause and cannot be applied in that manner regardless of whether it survives strict scrutiny on other facts. Public accommodations statutes serve an important purpose but can sweep too broadly when deployed to compel speech; the Constitution must prevail in that collision.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion (Gorsuch)

Justice Gorsuch reasoned from a line of compelled-speech precedent (West Virginia State Bd. of Ed. v Barnette, Wooley v Maynard, Hurley v Irish-American GLB Group of Boston, Boy Scouts of America v Dale) treating the right to refrain from speaking as a core First Amendment principle. The stipulated facts that Smith's wedding websites are expressive, customized, and communicate messages placed her activity squarely within speech protected by the First Amendment, and that protection is not lost by virtue of selling the expression commercially or through the corporate form. Hurley controlled: just as Massachusetts could not compel parade organizers to include messages they did not wish to express, Colorado could not compel Smith to create messages celebrating marriages at odds with her beliefs. Gorsuch emphasized that the holding does not license businesses to refuse service based on a customer's protected status when the service itself is not expressive, and that Colorado remained free to apply CADA to non-expressive goods and services. The First Amendment's tolerance of misguided speech was central: even deeply disagreeable messages receive protection, and the remedy for bad speech is counter-speech rather than government compulsion.

## Key Points

- Extends the compelled-speech line (Barnette, Wooley, Hurley, Dale) into the public-accommodations setting in a way Masterpiece Cakeshop v Colorado Civil Rights Commission (2018) had declined to reach on the merits.
- Distinguishes expressive custom services from non-expressive commercial sales, a distinction likely to generate extensive lower-court litigation about where the line falls.
- Holding rests entirely on the Free Speech Clause; the Court did not decide the Free Exercise question.
- Sotomayor's dissent frames the case as part of a larger retreat from the equal-access principle that animated Heart of Atlanta Motel v United States and its progeny.
- Pre-enforcement procedural posture (stipulated facts, no actual refused customer) has drawn criticism on justiciability grounds.

- [[Cases/Fulton v City of Philadelphia (2021)|Fulton v City of Philadelphia (2021)]] (upstream anti-discrimination clash): 303 Creative is the Free Speech-compelled-speech sibling to Fulton's Free Exercise-individualized-exemption attack on public-accommodations enforcement. - [[Cases/Romer v Evans (1996)|Romer v Evans (1996)]] (doctrinal counterweight): Sotomayor's dissent invokes the equal-access tradition Romer protected. 303 Creative creates a speech-based carve-out that, according to the dissent, destabilizes the Romer premise that the state may withdraw same-class access only for impermissible reasons.
- [[Cases/Christian Legal Society v Martinez (2010)|Christian Legal Society v Martinez (2010)]] (in tension): Reciprocal of Martinez's 303 Creative entry. Martinez upheld a public university all-comers policy by treating viewpoint neutrality and benefit-conditioning as independent justifications; 303 Creative cuts the other way on similar facts by treating compelled speech as the controlling frame. The two cases mark opposite poles of the same compelled-association tension and are the canonical pairing for testing whether benefit-conditioning logic survives the post-2020 Free Speech turn.
