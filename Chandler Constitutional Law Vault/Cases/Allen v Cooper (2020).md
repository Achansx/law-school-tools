# Allen v Cooper (2020)

## Memory Jogger

> The Copyright Remedy Clarification Act fails just as the Patent Remedy Act did in Florida Prepaid — a thin legislative record of unconstitutional copyright infringement by states cannot justify a blanket abrogation of state sovereign immunity, even twenty years later.

---

## Facts

Frederick Allen, a videographer, was commissioned by the state of North Carolina to document the ongoing archaeological salvage of a shipwreck identified as Blackbeard's pirate vessel, the Queen Anne's Revenge, which sank in 1718 off the North Carolina coast. Allen, operating through his company Nautilus Productions, created extensive copyrighted videos and photographs of the underwater salvage project. North Carolina subsequently posted many of these works online without Allen's permission on multiple occasions, despite his repeated objections.

Allen sued North Carolina and the North Carolina Department of Natural and Cultural Resources under the Copyright Remedy Clarification Act of 1990 (CRCA). Like the Patent Remedy Act struck down in Florida Prepaid (1999), the CRCA expressly abrogated state sovereign immunity for copyright infringement, purporting to act under Congress's Article I copyright power and its Section 5 enforcement authority. Allen sought money damages for North Carolina's ongoing and willful infringement of his copyrighted works.

---

## Procedural History

The district court dismissed Allen's CRCA claims on Eleventh Amendment grounds, following Florida Prepaid's reasoning that an analogous intellectual property abrogation statute was invalid. The Fourth Circuit affirmed. Allen petitioned for certiorari, arguing that the two decades since Florida Prepaid had produced a more robust legislative record and different circumstances that might support the CRCA's constitutionality. The Supreme Court granted certiorari.

- **Majority:** Kagan (for a unanimous Court on the constitutional holding)
- **Concurrence:** Thomas (joined by Ginsburg, joined in part by Breyer and Sotomayor) — writing separately to question whether Florida Prepaid was correctly decided
- **Concurrence:** Breyer (joined by Ginsburg) — suggesting Florida Prepaid may warrant reconsideration

---

## Holding

The Copyright Remedy Clarification Act does not validly abrogate state sovereign immunity under Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment. The legislative record supporting the CRCA is as thin as the record that doomed the Patent Remedy Act in Florida Prepaid, and the statute's nationwide abrogation of state immunity for all copyright infringement fails the congruence-and-proportionality test.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Justice Kagan's majority opinion applied Florida Prepaid directly. The threshold constitutional analysis was the same: state copyright infringement, like state patent infringement, does not necessarily violate the Fourteenth Amendment. If a state infringes a copyright and provides an adequate remedy — through its own courts or a takings proceeding — no constitutional violation has occurred. Only states that infringe and then deny all remedy potentially violate due process.

The majority examined the CRCA's legislative record and found it materially equivalent to the PRA's deficient record in Florida Prepaid. Congress had held hearings and received testimony about state copyright infringement, but the documented instances were sparse and almost none demonstrated that states had denied constitutionally adequate remedies. Given that the CRCA and PRA were essentially identical in scope (nationwide abrogation regardless of whether adequate remedies were available), the CRCA could survive only if its record was materially stronger than the PRA's. It was not. The majority expressly declined Allen's invitation to reconsider Florida Prepaid, but several concurring justices signaled openness to doing so in a future case with a richer record.

## Key Quotations

> "Given the identical scope of the CRCA and Patent Remedy Act, this case could be decided differently only if the CRCA responded to materially stronger evidence of unconstitutional infringement. But as in Florida Prepaid, the legislative record contains thin evidence of infringement. Because this record cannot support Congress's choice to strip the States of their sovereign immunity in all copyright infringement cases, the CRCA fails the 'congruence and proportionality' test." (Kagan, J.)
