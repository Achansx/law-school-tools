# Lawrence v Texas (2003)

## Memory Jogger

> A State cannot criminalize private, consensual sexual conduct between adults; Bowers v Hardwick is overruled.

---

## Facts

Houston police, responding to a false report of a weapons disturbance, entered the apartment of John Geddes Lawrence and observed him and another adult man, Tyron Garner, engaged in a consensual sexual act. The officers arrested both men under a Texas statute (Texas Penal Code section 21.06(a)) that criminalized "deviate sexual intercourse" between persons of the same sex, defined to include anal and oral sex. The statute did not criminalize the same conduct between persons of opposite sexes. Each defendant was held overnight, arraigned before a Justice of the Peace, entered a plea of no contest, and was fined.

Lawrence and Garner challenged the statute on Equal Protection and Due Process grounds, arguing both that it drew an impermissible classification based on sexual orientation and that it invaded a protected liberty interest in private consensual intimacy. The Texas statute carried a fine up to $500 and subjected those convicted to the collateral consequences of a criminal record, including registration requirements in some States that treated same-sex conduct as a sexual offense.

---

## Procedural History

The Harris County Criminal Court rejected the constitutional challenges and affirmed the convictions. A three-judge panel of the Texas Court of Appeals initially reversed on Equal Protection grounds, but on rehearing en banc the Court of Appeals affirmed the convictions, relying on Bowers v Hardwick (1986), which had upheld a Georgia sodomy statute against a Due Process challenge. The Texas Court of Criminal Appeals denied discretionary review.

The Supreme Court granted certiorari to consider three questions: whether the Texas statute violated Equal Protection by criminalizing same-sex conduct while permitting opposite-sex conduct; whether it violated Due Process by invading a protected liberty interest; and whether Bowers should be overruled. The Court reversed.

## Holding

The Texas statute criminalizing private, consensual sexual conduct between persons of the same sex violates the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. Adults are entitled to respect for their private lives, and the State cannot demean their existence or control their destiny by making their private sexual conduct a crime. The statute furthers no legitimate state interest that can justify its intrusion into the personal and private life of the individual. Bowers v Hardwick was not correct when it was decided and is not correct today; it is overruled.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion (Kennedy)

Justice Kennedy reframed the liberty interest from Bowers's narrow question ("whether the Federal Constitution confers a fundamental right upon homosexuals to engage in sodomy") to a broader one focused on adult autonomy in intimate relationships. He surveyed the historical claim that anti-sodomy laws targeted same-sex conduct, concluding that sweeping pre-modern prohibitions on non-procreative sex were not the same as a longstanding national tradition of criminalizing homosexual conduct specifically, and that the modern pattern of state decriminalization (twenty-four States as of 2003) eroded Bowers's foundation. On the doctrinal question he applied what reads as rational-basis review but with real bite: the Texas statute furthered no legitimate state interest that could justify criminalizing private, consensual intimacy, and majoritarian disapproval of conduct on moral grounds is not alone a constitutionally sufficient basis for prohibiting it. Kennedy relied heavily on Planned Parenthood v Casey and Romer v Evans, invoking the former's autonomy reasoning and the latter's rejection of legislation born of "animus."

### Concurrence in the Judgment (O'Connor)

Justice O'Connor concurred in the result but declined to overrule Bowers, reasoning that the case could be resolved more narrowly on Equal Protection grounds without reopening the substantive due process question. Her interpretive move was analytically surgical: because the Texas statute drew an explicit line between same-sex and opposite-sex conduct, it classified persons based on sexual orientation and therefore required rational-basis justification for that classification. Moral disapproval of a disfavored group, she concluded, is not a legitimate state interest capable of satisfying even rational basis review under the Equal Protection Clause — citing Romer and the animus-doctrine line from Department of Agriculture v Moreno. O'Connor acknowledged that her Equal Protection approach would not automatically doom a statute that prohibited all sodomy regardless of the sex of the participants, and she flagged marriage as an area where the State's interests might provide rational justification for differentiation — a caveat that Kennedy later declined to honor in Obergefell.

### Dissent (Scalia)

Justice Scalia, joined by Rehnquist and Thomas, dissented on three interlocking grounds. First and most doctrinally, he argued that the majority's application of "rational basis" review was a disguise for heightened scrutiny: if moral disapproval alone cannot justify legislation, then innumerable laws — against bigamy, adult incest, prostitution, adultery, fornication, bestiality, and obscenity — are called into question, because all of them rest on the same foundation that the Court now pronounces insufficient. Second, Scalia catalogued the "massive disruption of the current social order" that follows from overruling Bowers, noting that reliance interests had accumulated across seventeen years of litigation, legislation, and social practice — a contrast he drew against Casey's different handling of reliance in the abortion context. Third, Scalia issued what reads as a constitutional prediction: if the majority's autonomy reasoning means that the State cannot deem private intimate choices criminal on moral grounds, then the State cannot deny same-sex couples marriage licenses on the same grounds — a prediction the Court confirmed twelve years later in Obergefell. His "product of a Court, which is the product of a law-profession culture" passage was widely quoted as a criticism of the Court's relationship to the legal academy's evolving consensus on sexual orientation.

### Dissent (Thomas)

Justice Thomas wrote separately in a single short paragraph. Borrowing Justice Stewart's phrase from Griswold, he called the Texas statute "uncommonly silly" and said he would vote to repeal it if he were a member of the Texas Legislature. But Thomas maintained that silliness is not unconstitutionality: he could find no general right of privacy or liberty of the sort Kennedy described in the Bill of Rights or any other part of the Constitution, and his duty was to decide cases agreeably to the Constitution and laws, not to his personal policy preferences. The dissent is notable both for its brevity and for its internal honesty — Thomas declined to defend the Texas statute on the merits while still casting a vote to uphold it.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

1. **Private cohabitation statute.** A State enacts a law making it a misdemeanor for two unmarried adults to live together in the same dwelling, premised on the legislature's traditional moral disapproval of fornication. Under Kennedy's majority reasoning the statute fails: the conduct is private, consensual, engaged in by adults, and the only asserted justification is majoritarian moral disapproval — precisely the interest Lawrence held insufficient. The fact that the statute targets opposite-sex as well as same-sex pairs does not save it; the "no legitimate state interest" holding is not limited to sexual-orientation classifications.

2. **Municipal BDSM ordinance.** A city passes an ordinance making it a criminal offense for adults to engage in consensual sadomasochistic acts in a private residence, citing community standards of decency. This falls squarely within Lawrence's protected zone: private, consensual, adult, non-commercial, and non-public. The Court's enumeration of what Lawrence does *not* cover — minors, coercion, public conduct, prostitution — functions as an exclusionary list, and this hypo satisfies none of the exclusions. The ordinance would fail under the same "no legitimate state interest" test.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

3. **Prohibition of commercial sex work.** A State criminalizes prostitution and related commercial sexual services between consenting adults. Kennedy's majority opinion expressly disclaimed extending Lawrence to situations involving "public conduct or prostitution." Commercial exchange alters the autonomy calculus — the State's interests in preventing exploitation, trafficking, third-party harms, and public health provide legitimate justifications that survive the Lawrence test. The commercial-sex prohibition would be upheld.

4. **Adult-minor sexual conduct statute.** A State criminalizes sexual contact between an adult and a seventeen-year-old. The Lawrence majority equally expressly disclaimed application to situations involving minors: "The present case does not involve minors." The State's compelling interest in protecting children from predatory conduct provides more than a rational basis; such statutes survive even strict scrutiny. The adult-minor statute would be upheld.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

5. **Polygamy statute.** A State criminally prohibits cohabitation in a plural marriage arrangement among three or more consenting adults. Scalia's dissent explicitly predicted that Lawrence's reasoning extends to polygamy: if moral disapproval cannot justify criminalizing consensual same-sex sodomy, it cannot justify criminalizing consensual plural cohabitation. Kennedy's majority attempted to cabin the holding to bilateral adult intimate relationships, but the limiting principle is contested. Post-Lawrence courts (including the Tenth Circuit) have generally upheld anti-polygamy statutes by distinguishing them on grounds of state interests in marital administration and protecting potential victims in plural households — but the textual logic of the Kennedy opinion does not supply a clean doctrinal stop. This is a genuine fence-sitter: the holding's limiting language points one way, the underlying reasoning points the other.

## Critique

**Originalist/Scalia critique.** The most persistent internal critique of Lawrence is that Kennedy's "rational basis" framing is incoherent. If moral views — the traditional police-power basis for legislating about sexual conduct — cannot justify a law, then rational basis review has been gutted for this category of legislation. Scalia argued that the majority covertly applied heightened scrutiny without acknowledging it, thereby avoiding the hard question of whether sexual orientation is a suspect or quasi-suspect classification. This formalism critique has force: the Court cannot simultaneously say it is not creating a fundamental right and apply a test that behaves like one. The broader textualist concern is that Kennedy's "emerging awareness" methodology substitutes judicial assessment of cultural trends for the fixed historical meaning that should anchor liberty guarantees.

**Progressive critique.** From the left, the dominant scholarly critique — associated particularly with Nan Hunter, Suzanne Goldberg, and Jack Balkin — is that Kennedy chose the wrong doctrinal ground. By resting Lawrence on autonomy-dignity under the Due Process Clause rather than O'Connor's Equal Protection path, Kennedy avoided holding that sexual-orientation classifications trigger heightened scrutiny. The consequence was a decade of lower-court confusion about the level of scrutiny applicable to DOMA and state marriage bans — uncertainty that Obergefell ultimately resolved by blending the two doctrinal strands Kennedy had kept separate. Additionally, Lawrence's methodology became vulnerable once Dobbs (2022) revived the Glucksberg "deeply rooted in history and tradition" test as the exclusive mode of substantive due process analysis: Lawrence's "emerging awareness" historical approach cannot satisfy that standard, and Thomas's Dobbs concurrence explicitly identified Lawrence as a candidate for reconsideration on exactly that basis.

## Key Points

- Overrules Bowers v Hardwick (1986) after only seventeen years, demonstrating the functional rather than formal character of stare decisis in constitutional cases.
- Part of the sexual-orientation liberty line: Romer v Evans (1996), Lawrence (2003), United States v Windsor (2013), Obergefell v Hodges (2015), Bostock v Clayton County (2020).
- Kennedy's reasoning blends Due Process and Equal Protection without committing to either as the doctrinal ground, a pattern he repeats in later sexual-orientation cases.
- The "rational basis with bite" application treats majoritarian moral disapproval as insufficient state interest, a holding repeatedly invoked in later animus-doctrine cases.
- Post-Dobbs, Thomas's concurrence explicitly identified Lawrence as a candidate for reconsideration under a stricter substantive-due-process framework.

- [[Topics/Equal Protection|Equal Protection]] — O'Connor's concurrence develops the animus doctrine's application to sexual-orientation classifications under rational basis EP review, foreshadowing the EP strand Kennedy later fuses into Obergefell.
- [[Cases/Bowers v Hardwick (1986)|Bowers v Hardwick (1986)]] — overruled by Lawrence; Kennedy's reframing of the liberty question from "fundamental right to sodomy" to "autonomy in intimate relationships" is the operative jurisprudential move.
- [[Cases/Romer v Evans (1996)|Romer v Evans (1996)]] — prior Kennedy opinion establishing that animus toward a disfavored group is not a legitimate state interest under rational basis; Lawrence extends that reasoning from legislation disabling civil rights protections to legislation criminalizing conduct.
- [[Cases/Planned Parenthood v Casey (1992)|Planned Parenthood v Casey (1992)]] — Kennedy's autonomy language in Lawrence draws directly on Casey's liberty passage; Lawrence explicitly cites Casey for the proposition that the State cannot control personal decisions about intimate relationships.
- [[Cases/Griswold v Connecticut (1965)|Griswold v Connecticut (1965)]] — foundational precedent for the zone of protected private conduct in intimate decisions; Lawrence extends Griswold's protection to same-sex relationships and removes the marital/procreative limit Griswold implied.
- [[Cases/Obergefell v Hodges (2015)|Obergefell v Hodges (2015)]] — Kennedy's Obergefell majority fuses Lawrence's autonomy reasoning with equal dignity to establish a fundamental right to same-sex marriage; Lawrence is one of the four principles on which Obergefell rests.
- [[Cases/Dobbs v Jackson Women's Health Organization (2022)|Dobbs v Jackson Women's Health Organization (2022)]] — Dobbs's revival of the Glucksberg history-and-tradition test undermines Lawrence's "emerging awareness" methodology; Thomas's concurrence identifies Lawrence as a candidate for future reconsideration.
- [[Cases/Washington v Glucksberg (1997)|Washington v Glucksberg (1997)]] — the Glucksberg two-step (deeply rooted in history and tradition plus careful description) is the methodology Lawrence implicitly displaces by reframing the asserted liberty interest at a higher level of generality (autonomy in intimate relationships rather than a specific historically-recognized "right to sodomy"); the Lawrence-vs-Glucksberg methodological tension is the doctrinal pivot Dobbs later resolves in Glucksberg's favor.
