# Tandon v Newsom (2021)

## Memory Jogger

> California limited in-home religious gatherings to three households while allowing hair salons, retail stores, and restaurants to operate with higher occupancy; the Court held that whenever any comparable secular activity is treated more favorably than religious exercise, strict scrutiny is triggered -- comparability is measured by risk posed to the government's interest, not the reason people gather.

---

## Facts

During the COVID-19 pandemic, California issued tiered restriction orders that, at relevant times, prohibited in-home gatherings of more than three households. The restrictions applied to all "gatherings" occurring in private residences, including religious gatherings. However, California permitted various secular businesses -- including hair salons, retail stores, personal care services, movie theaters, private suites at sporting events and concerts, and indoor restaurants -- to operate with greater occupancy, subject to masking and distancing protocols.

Appellants Ernestine Meriweather and others sought to hold in-home Bible study and prayer meetings that would include more than three households. They argued that their religious gatherings were comparable to the secular activities California permitted, and that California's differential treatment violated the Free Exercise Clause. They sought an emergency injunction pending appeal.

---

## Procedural History

Appellants moved for an emergency injunction in the Ninth Circuit. The Ninth Circuit denied the injunction. The Supreme Court granted the application for injunctive relief, reversed the Ninth Circuit, and issued a per curiam opinion explaining its reasoning. The matter was decided on the emergency injunction standard.

## Holding

Government regulations are not neutral and generally applicable, and therefore trigger strict scrutiny under the Free Exercise Clause, whenever they treat any comparable secular activity more favorably than religious exercise. Comparability is judged by the risk various activities pose to the government's asserted interest -- not by the reasons why people gather. California's restrictions, which allowed numerous secular settings to operate at higher occupancy while restricting in-home religious gatherings, failed strict scrutiny because the government did not show that in-home religious gatherings were more dangerous than the permitted secular activities, even when the same precautions were applied.

---

## Analysis

### Per Curiam Opinion

The per curiam opinion built on the Court's earlier COVID-religion decisions in South Bay United Pentecostal Church v. Newsom and Roman Catholic Diocese of Brooklyn v. Cuomo. It articulated three rules that together form the current Free Exercise framework for differential treatment of religious gatherings.

First, the comparable-secular-activity trigger: strict scrutiny applies whenever the government treats any comparable secular activity more favorably than religious exercise. The state need not treat all secular activities more favorably -- even one comparable secular exemption is sufficient to defeat the claim that the restriction is neutral and generally applicable. Second, comparability is assessed against the government's own asserted interest, not the subjective purpose of the gathering. California's asserted interest was reducing COVID transmission; the Court measured whether hair salons, retail stores, and restaurants posed similar transmission risks -- and found they did -- while permitting their operation. Third, the government bears the burden of demonstrating that less restrictive alternatives could not serve its interest. Where the government permits comparable secular activities with precautions (masking, distancing), it must show that the same precautions would be inadequate for the religious exercise at issue.

## Key Points

- **Tandon's comparable-secular-activity rule** is a significant expansion of Free Exercise protection: any single secular exemption from an otherwise general restriction is sufficient to trigger strict scrutiny for a religious claimant seeking similar treatment.
- The **comparability standard** is anchored to the government's own asserted interest: two activities are comparable if they pose similar risks to the interest the regulation is designed to serve, regardless of why people are gathering.
- The decision represents a continuation of the Roberts Court's trend, visible in **South Bay United Pentecostal** and **Roman Catholic Diocese**, of applying strict scrutiny rather than rational basis to COVID restrictions that burden religious gatherings.
- Tandon sits in tension with **Employment Division v Smith** (1990), which held that neutral, generally applicable laws survive Free Exercise challenges without strict scrutiny. Tandon narrows Smith's application by making it very easy to show that a law is not generally applicable.
- The opinion was **per curiam** and decided on the emergency injunction standard, but its three doctrinal rules have been widely treated as binding Free Exercise doctrine.
