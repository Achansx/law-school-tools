# Sessions v Morales-Santana (2017)

## Memory Jogger

> The INA's differential physical-presence requirements for unwed citizen mothers versus fathers to transmit citizenship to children born abroad failed heightened scrutiny -- but the remedy was to "level down" by applying the longer requirement to both sexes, not extend the shorter one.

---

## Facts

The Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) sets out the requirements for a child born abroad to transmit U.S. citizenship when one parent is a U.S. citizen and the other is a foreign national. For a child born out of wedlock, the statute drew a gender line: an unwed U.S.-citizen father had to have been physically present in the United States for at least ten years (five of them after age fourteen) before the child's birth, while an unwed U.S.-citizen mother needed only one continuous year of physical presence. Congress's rationale rested on the generalization that unwed mothers would bear and raise their children and maintain a relationship with them, while unwed fathers were less likely to do so without legal formalities compelling paternity acknowledgment.

Luis Ramon Morales-Santana was born in the Dominican Republic in 1962 to a Dominican mother and Jose Morales, a U.S. citizen who had been physically present in the United States from birth until approximately 20 days before his nineteenth birthday -- failing the statutory five-years-after-age-fourteen requirement by just 20 days. Had Morales-Santana's father been an unwed U.S.-citizen mother, the one-year requirement would have been easily satisfied. Morales-Santana, whose father died in 1976, sought derivative citizenship through his father's status.

---

## Procedural History

When immigration authorities sought to remove Morales-Santana from the United States based on a criminal conviction, he claimed U.S. citizenship through his father. An immigration judge rejected the claim. The Board of Immigration Appeals affirmed. The Second Circuit reversed, holding that the INA's gender-based differential violated equal protection and that the appropriate remedy was to extend the one-year rule to unwed fathers. The Supreme Court affirmed on the constitutional question but reversed on the remedy.

## Holding

The gender-based differential in 8 U.S.C. § 1409(c) -- which allows unwed U.S.-citizen mothers to transmit citizenship after only one year of physical presence while requiring unwed U.S.-citizen fathers to satisfy a longer residency requirement -- violates the equal protection guarantee of the Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause. Laws granting or denying benefits based on the sex of a qualifying parent constitute sex-based classifications subject to heightened scrutiny, requiring an exceedingly persuasive justification that the government cannot supply by relying on gender stereotypes about parental bonding. As to remedy, because extending the favorable one-year rule would effectively make the exception the general rule, the Court holds that Congress would have preferred to apply the longer requirement uniformly to both sexes; the appropriate interim remedy is to require five years of physical presence (the now-current general rule) for all unwed parents.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Justice Ginsburg's opinion situates Sessions in the line of sex-discrimination decisions from Reed v. Reed (1971) through United States v. Virginia (1996): laws that grant or deny benefits based on the sex of a qualifying parent are subject to heightened scrutiny and require an exceedingly persuasive justification grounded in genuine distinctions, not gender stereotypes. The government argued that the shorter maternal presence requirement was justified by the greater assurance that an unwed mother would maintain a tie between the child and the United States, given the practical realities of childbirth and custody. Ginsburg rejected this rationale as precisely the kind of stereotype -- that mothers bond and fathers do not -- that the Court's modern equal protection decisions have condemned. The statute thus falls on the constitutional question. On remedy, Ginsburg reasons that extending the one-year exception to fathers would transform the general five-year rule into an exception, which Congress clearly did not intend; leveling down is more consistent with legislative purpose.

## Key Points

- Confirms that heightened scrutiny applies to federal statutes that discriminate on the basis of sex, via the Fifth Amendment's due process clause (reverse-incorporated from Fourteenth Amendment equal protection).
- The "exceedingly persuasive justification" standard (from United States v. Virginia) requires a genuine justification grounded in real differences, not gender stereotypes.
- The leveling-down remedy is unusual: rather than extending the more favorable rule, the Court imposes the more demanding standard universally, resulting in the plaintiff losing citizenship despite winning on the constitutional merits.
- Compare to Sessions' holding with Nguyen v. INS (2001), which upheld a paternity-acknowledgment requirement on a narrower ground (biological certainty of parentage); Sessions and Nguyen now sit in some tension.
