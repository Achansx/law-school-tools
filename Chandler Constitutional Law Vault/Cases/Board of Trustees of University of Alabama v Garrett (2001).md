# Board of Trustees of University of Alabama v Garrett (2001)

## Memory Jogger

> ADA Title I cannot subject states to private suits for money damages because Congress documented no sufficient pattern of unconstitutional disability discrimination by states — disability classifications need only survive rational basis, so the remedial bar is low and the legislative record fell short.

---

## Facts

Patricia Garrett was a registered nurse employed as director of nursing at the University of Alabama at Birmingham Hospital, a state institution. After being diagnosed with breast cancer and undergoing surgery and chemotherapy, she was demoted to a lower-paying staff nurse position. Milton Ash was employed as a security officer by the Alabama Department of Youth Services. He suffered from severe asthma and a sleep disorder and requested that his employer modify his duties to reduce his exposure to cigarette smoke. His requests were largely denied, and he was disciplined for limiting his physical exertion.

Both Garrett and Ash sued their state employers under Title I of the Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990 (ADA), which prohibits disability discrimination in employment and was expressly stated to abrogate state sovereign immunity. States have no constitutional obligation to accommodate employees with disabilities beyond what rational basis requires — the Equal Protection Clause demands only that disability classifications be rationally related to a legitimate government interest.

---

## Procedural History

Garrett's and Ash's cases were consolidated in the Northern District of Alabama, which dismissed their ADA Title I claims for money damages on Eleventh Amendment grounds. The Eleventh Circuit reversed, holding that Congress had validly abrogated state sovereign immunity under Section 5. The Supreme Court granted certiorari to resolve a circuit split over whether ADA Title I constituted valid Section 5 legislation.

## Holding

Title I of the ADA does not validly abrogate state sovereign immunity under Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment. Because disability classifications are subject only to rational basis review, Congress may not use Section 5 to impose disability accommodation requirements on states that exceed what the Constitution independently requires, unless Congress documents a pattern of unconstitutional state conduct — and the legislative record here failed to establish such a pattern.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Chief Justice Rehnquist applied the City of Boerne congruence-and-proportionality test. The critical preliminary question was what constitutional standard applies to state disability classifications: the Court reaffirmed that disability, unlike race or sex, is not a suspect or quasi-suspect class. States may treat disabled and non-disabled employees differently so long as there is any rational basis. This means the constitutional floor that Congress can enforce under Section 5 is very low.

Against this low floor, the majority scrutinized Congress's legislative record. Testimony and anecdotes in the record described discrimination against disabled individuals, but the majority found that most of the examples reflected private conduct, actions by local governments (not covered by the Eleventh Amendment), or conduct that would survive rational basis review. The handful of instances attributable to states and plausibly unconstitutional was insufficient to justify a nationwide, prophylactic abrogation requiring states to provide reasonable accommodations — a standard far exceeding constitutional requirements. The statute's reach was therefore not congruent and proportional to documented constitutional violations. The majority distinguished cases like Fitzpatrick v Bitzer and Katzenbach v Morgan, where Congress had acted against more clearly established constitutional violations.
