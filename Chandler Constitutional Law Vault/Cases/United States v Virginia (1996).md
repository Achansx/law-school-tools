# United States v Virginia (1996)

## Memory Jogger

> A state that excludes women from a unique public educational opportunity must show an "exceedingly persuasive justification," and a parallel women's program that does not offer the same tangible and intangible benefits is not an adequate remedy.

---

## Facts

The Virginia Military Institute (VMI), a state-funded military college founded in 1839, was the only single-sex public institution of higher education in Virginia. VMI used an "adversative" method of training characterized by physical rigor, mental stress, absolute equality of treatment, and a spartan "rat line" designed to strip down cadets and rebuild them. VMI's stated mission was to produce "citizen-soldiers" prepared for leadership in civilian life and military service. Women were categorically excluded from admission.

In response to a federal equal protection challenge, Virginia proposed a remedial plan: establishing the Virginia Women's Institute for Leadership (VWIL) at Mary Baldwin College, a private women's liberal arts school. VWIL would offer a degree, military training, and leadership development, but without the adversative method, without VMI's faculty, endowment, and reputation, and without comparable resources or prestige.

---

## Procedural History

The United States sued Virginia under the Equal Protection Clause. The district court initially ruled for Virginia. The Fourth Circuit reversed, ordering Virginia to remedy the constitutional violation. After Virginia proposed the VWIL parallel program, the district court approved it and the Fourth Circuit affirmed. The Supreme Court granted certiorari to decide whether VMI's exclusion of women violated equal protection and, if so, whether the VWIL remedy cured the violation.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority:** Ginsburg (joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, and Breyer)
- **Concurrence:** Rehnquist (in the judgment)
- **Dissent:** Scalia
- Thomas did not participate.

---

## Holding

Virginia's categorical exclusion of women from VMI violates the Equal Protection Clause. To justify a sex-based classification, the state must show an "exceedingly persuasive justification" that the classification serves important governmental objectives and that the discriminatory means are substantially related to those objectives. The justification must be genuine, not invented post hoc, and must not rely on overbroad generalizations about the talents, capacities, or preferences of males and females. The VWIL program is not an adequate remedy because it does not offer the same tangible (resources, faculty, curriculum) or intangible (prestige, reputation, alumni network) benefits as VMI.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Justice Ginsburg's majority opinion reframes intermediate scrutiny for sex classifications by elevating the state's burden to an "exceedingly persuasive justification." The state must show the challenged classification serves important governmental objectives and the means are substantially related to those objectives, and the justification cannot rely on overbroad generalizations about males and females. Virginia's proffered interests (educational diversity and the unsuitability of the adversative method for women) failed. Diversity was a post hoc rationalization belied by the male-only admissions history, and the unsuitability claim rested on the very stereotyped generalization the doctrine forbids. Some women can meet VMI's demands, and categorical exclusion impermissibly treats all women as incapable. The VWIL remedy fails because equal protection requires equal access to the unique benefits VMI provides, not a separate program lacking comparable resources, faculty, prestige, and alumni network.

### Concurrence (Rehnquist)

Chief Justice Rehnquist concurred in the judgment but declined to join the majority's "exceedingly persuasive justification" formulation. He would have adhered to the Craig v Boren standard requiring that a gender classification "bear a close and substantial relationship to important governmental objectives." In his view the majority's new phrasing, while not intended to announce a different tier of review, was imprecise enough to destabilize settled doctrine. Rehnquist nonetheless agreed that Virginia's exclusion violated equal protection on two narrower grounds. First, Virginia's diversity rationale was unpersuasive because for most of VMI's history the Commonwealth provided no comparable single-sex institution for women. Diversity may be a legitimate aim of a public system of higher education, but a state cannot claim it as justification when only one sex has access to the diverse offering. Second, the adversative method rationale failed because the record contained no evidence that an adversative approach was pedagogically superior or produced character traits unavailable through other methods. Rehnquist also noted that the VWIL remedy was inadequate because its tangible and intangible benefits did not approach VMI's. His concurrence thus endorsed the result while signaling unease that the majority's language would be read as heightened scrutiny in intermediate clothing.

### Dissent (Scalia)

Justice Scalia dissented alone and organized his objections around four themes. First, the Court abandoned intermediate scrutiny in practice by demanding that Virginia disprove every imagined generalization about women through expert testimony. That burden, he argued, is indistinguishable from strict scrutiny and inverts the presumption of legislative validity intermediate scrutiny preserves. Second, single-sex public education has a long and respectable pedigree in American history, and the Fourteenth Amendment as ratified in 1868 was not understood to forbid it. Tradition of that duration should not be swept aside by judicial fiat. Third, the district court made detailed factual findings that VMI's adversative method would be materially altered by coeducation and that single-sex education produced distinct pedagogical benefits. The majority dismissed those findings as "overbroad generalizations" without identifying any evidentiary defect. Fourth, the practical consequence is that single-sex public education is "functionally dead" because any state defending such a classification must now prove by expert testimony an "exceedingly persuasive justification," a bar no state can reliably clear. Scalia feared even remedial programs for at-risk boys or girls would be vulnerable. He separately chided the concurrence for engaging the majority's reasoning while pretending to apply a narrower test, observing that the concurrence reached the same destination on a more moderate path but without greater analytical candor.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

1. A state maintains a male-only public engineering academy with unique laboratories, faculty, and industry partnerships, and defends exclusion of women by pointing to diversity among its public colleges and an internal culture said to suit men better. Under VMI the state must supply an exceedingly persuasive justification. The diversity argument fails because no comparable women's engineering program exists, and the cultural-suitability claim fails as an overbroad generalization about women's talents and preferences. The conviction of the exclusion falls on the same rationale that doomed VMI.

2. A state police academy runs a tactical training program open only to men, asserting that admitting women would require modifying the physical regimen and compromise the program's institutional identity. Under VMI categorical exclusion cannot rest on what most women can handle. The state must admit any qualified woman who meets neutral performance standards. The outcome tracks VMI directly.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

1. A state funds a boys' public high school and a girls' public high school in the same district, each offering equivalent curricula, faculty compensation, facilities, counseling resources, and college placement outcomes. A male student challenges his exclusion from the girls' school. Because the tangible and intangible benefits are substantively comparable, the remedial deficiency that doomed VWIL is absent. The state can defend the arrangement under intermediate scrutiny by pointing to documented benefits of single-sex secondary education, a justification VMI left undisturbed. The classification survives.

2. A state university maintains a women's rowing team under Title IX's athletic-equity framework. A male swimmer challenges exclusion. VMI's doctrine permits classifications tethered to "real differences" and to the remedial aim of redressing historic exclusion of women from athletics. The classification survives.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

A state establishes a public single-sex high school for at-risk adolescent girls, with empirical support showing the environment reduces dropout and pregnancy rates. The state creates a parallel school for at-risk boys with comparable funding, faculty, and programming. A girl denied admission to the boys' school sues. The state argues real empirical differences justify the programmatic split and that the parallel offering satisfies the VMI remedy. Plaintiffs argue the "separate but equal" logic VMI rejected applies with equal force, and that the record reveals stereotyped generalizations masquerading as empirical findings. The "exceedingly persuasive justification" inquiry and the adequacy of the parallel remedy are genuinely contested.

---

## Critique

VMI occupies an ambiguous position in sex-classification doctrine. It is widely read as the apex of heightened intermediate scrutiny, yet the majority refuses to announce a new tier and Justice Ginsburg explicitly preserves classifications rooted in "real differences."

Progressive and substantive-equality readings treat VMI as the long-overdue application of robust scrutiny to de jure sex discrimination. On this view the "exceedingly persuasive justification" language captures a truth intermediate scrutiny had always implied but courts had not enforced: categorical exclusions based on sex perpetuate historical subordination and deserve the same skepticism as race classifications. Commentators such as Cary Franklin read VMI as dismantling the compatibility of the anti-stereotyping principle with categorical exclusion, forbidding the state from treating any particular woman as the average woman.

Originalist and textualist critiques, led by Scalia's dissent, argue the Fourteenth Amendment as understood in 1868 tolerated single-sex public education (state-supported universities in that era were overwhelmingly single-sex), and the majority's standard has no textual or historical grounding. By requiring the state to disprove stereotypes through expert testimony, the majority inverts the presumption of legislative validity intermediate scrutiny nominally preserves.

A centrist doctrinal critique asks whether VMI creates a fourth tier of scrutiny between intermediate and strict or merely a rhetorical intensification within Craig v Boren. The answer matters for affirmative action: if VMI announces heightened review, then benign sex classifications favoring women in historically excluded fields face the same hurdle. Rehnquist's concurrence reflects precisely this concern.

A disparate-impact critique notes that VMI still permits classifications based on "real differences," leaving unresolved the tension Geduldig v Aiello exposed: whether biologically tied or pregnancy-based classifications count as sex discrimination. Post-VMI cases like Nguyen v INS (2001) suggest "real differences" reasoning remains potent when the state invokes biological parenthood, so VMI's scrutiny does not extend symmetrically to all sex-correlated classifications.

Chandler-exam framing: VMI is the bridge between Craig v Boren's intermediate scrutiny and any future recognition of sex as a suspect class. Students should flag both what the majority does (elevates the state's burden) and what it preserves (real-differences carve-outs), and be prepared to argue either way on whether the case announces a new standard or reinforces an old one.

## Key Points

- Establishes "exceedingly persuasive justification" as the burden the state must meet for sex classifications.
- Reaffirms intermediate scrutiny for sex while elevating the rigor of its application.
- The remedial analysis (VWIL inadequate) parallels Sweatt v Painter's rejection of separate-but-not-equal facilities in the race context.
- Permits sex classifications that reflect "real differences" but forbids those resting on "overbroad generalizations."
- Chandler emphasis: VMI sits at the apex of sex-classification doctrine and frames subsequent cases on single-sex education and women in the military.
