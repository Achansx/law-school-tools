# Barron v Baltimore (1833)

## Memory Jogger

> Before the Fourteenth Amendment, the Bill of Rights applied only to the federal government and did not constrain state or municipal action.

---

## Facts

John Barron co-owned a commercially valuable wharf in the Baltimore harbor. In the course of extensive street-grading and drainage projects, the City of Baltimore diverted streams and caused large quantities of sand and earth to accumulate around Barron's wharf. The deposits made the water too shallow for ships, destroying the wharf's commercial value. The damage was not a byproduct of incidental municipal activity but the direct consequence of deliberate municipal public-works decisions.

Barron sued the Mayor and City Council of Baltimore in Maryland state court, alleging that the City had effectively taken his private property for public use without paying just compensation. He invoked the Fifth Amendment's Takings Clause, which provides that "private property [shall not] be taken for public use, without just compensation." The case ultimately presented the question whether that federal constitutional guarantee restrained a state municipality.

---

## Procedural History

A Baltimore County court awarded Barron $4,500 in damages. The City appealed to Maryland's court of appeals, which reversed and held the Takings Clause inapplicable to the states. Barron sought review in the Supreme Court of the United States, which took jurisdiction under Section 25 of the Judiciary Act of 1789 on the theory that the case presented a federal constitutional question.

The Supreme Court heard argument in February 1833 and, shortly thereafter, Chief Justice John Marshall delivered the opinion of a unanimous Court. The Court did not reach the merits of the alleged taking; it dismissed for lack of jurisdiction, holding the Takings Clause inapplicable to state and municipal action.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority:** Marshall, C.J. (joined by Johnson, Duvall, Story, Thompson, McLean, Baldwin) — unanimous
- **Concurrence:** None
- **Dissent:** None

---

## Holding

The Fifth Amendment's Takings Clause, and by extension the Bill of Rights more generally, restrains only the federal government. It does not apply of its own force to the states or their political subdivisions. Because Barron's claim rested on a federal constitutional provision that did not reach the City of Baltimore, the Supreme Court lacked jurisdiction to review the Maryland court's judgment.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Chief Justice Marshall's structural argument proceeded in three steps. First, the Constitution was "ordained and established by the people of the United States for themselves, for their own government, and not for the government of the individual states." Second, general limitations of power in the Constitution therefore naturally constrain only the government the Constitution creates, absent an express textual indication otherwise. Third, Article I § 10 demonstrates that the Framers knew how to impose limitations specifically on the states and did so explicitly when they intended, while the Bill of Rights contains no comparable language directed at state governments. Marshall treated the inference as nearly self-evident, observing that the first ten amendments "contain no expression indicating an intention to apply them to the state governments. This court cannot so apply them." The ruling would be overridden, piece by piece, through the Fourteenth Amendment's incorporation doctrine more than a century later.

### Concurrence

No separate concurrence was filed. Historical context bears noting: Marshall authored this opinion late in his career, only two years before his death in 1835, and the restrained, structural cast of the opinion reflects a Chief Justice more interested in foreclosing a misreading of the Constitution than in building new doctrine. Where McCulloch (1819) had exercised the full creative energy of Marshall's federalism, Barron performs the mirror-image task: it confirms that federal constitutional limitations do not by their own force reach state action, and it does so by pointing to the text and structure rather than to any theory of national power.

### Dissent

No dissent was filed. The Court's unanimity is striking given the deep Jacksonian-era divisions over the Second Bank of the United States and the Cherokee Nation cases, both of which generated public controversy and intra-Court friction during the same term. Marshall's opinion succeeded in producing a consensus because it rested on a structural reading of the Constitution's text that neither Jacksonian states' rights advocates nor nationalists had substantial reason to contest. Jacksonians welcomed the outcome because it preserved state autonomy from a federal Bill of Rights overlay. Nationalists conceded the holding because nothing in it limited Congress's enumerated powers or the Marshall Court's prior federalism precedents.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

1. **1835 Massachusetts free exercise claim.** Massachusetts maintains a compulsory church tax supporting Congregationalist ministers; a Baptist dissenter challenges the tax as a violation of the First Amendment's free exercise clause. Under Barron, the challenge fails: the First Amendment, like the Fifth, binds only Congress. The dissenter's remedy lies in the Massachusetts state constitution, not the federal one. The Barron logic controls because the First Amendment's text contains no language directed at state governments and Massachusetts had not yet ratified any amendment altering that baseline.

2. **1845 state criminal procedure denying counsel.** A Virginia defendant charged with a capital felony is denied appointed counsel at trial and convicted; he claims a Sixth Amendment right to counsel on appeal to the Supreme Court. Under Barron, the claim fails. Although Powell v Alabama (1932) and Gideon v Wainwright (1963) later incorporate the counsel right against the states through the Fourteenth Amendment, those decisions are nearly a century away. In 1845, the Bill of Rights is an inert document as against state criminal process, and the defendant's only federal avenue is habeas relief from a federal court, which will deny jurisdiction on the same Barron ground.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

3. **Identical wharf damage today.** A modern city commits the same deliberate street-grading that diverts streams and destroys the wharf's utility. The property owner sues in federal court alleging a Fifth Amendment Takings Clause violation. The claim now succeeds. Chicago, Burlington and Quincy Railroad Co v Chicago (1897) incorporated the Just Compensation principle against the states through the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause, and modern takings doctrine treats this deliberate destruction of economic value as a regulatory or physical taking requiring just compensation. Barron survives as the historical starting point but not as controlling law against a modern municipality.

4. **Federal harbor project causing identical damage.** If the Army Corps of Engineers carried out the same grading and silting project under a federal public-works authorization, the Fifth Amendment would apply directly without any incorporation question. Barron itself concedes this: its holding is not that the Takings Clause is unenforceable but that it binds only the federal government. Because the defendant here is the federal government, the Takings Clause applies of its own force, and Barron actually supports the plaintiff's jurisdictional theory rather than defeating it.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

5. **Pre-1868 territorial government.** In 1850, a territorial legislature in what will become Colorado enacts a criminal statute allegedly violating the Sixth Amendment. Is the territorial government a "federal actor" to which the Bill of Rights applies directly, or a "state" actor shielded by Barron? The question divides early commentators. The Supreme Court has treated territorial governments as creatures of Congress, which suggests direct application of the Bill of Rights. But Barron's structural logic cuts both ways: if the Constitution restrains only the government it creates, then Congress's delegatee (the territorial legislature) inherits that restraint, yet if the territorial legislature exercises locally delegated police powers that function like state authority, Barron's reasoning might shield it. Reynolds v United States (1878) eventually confirms the direct-application view for federal territories, but in 1850 the answer is genuinely unsettled.

## Critique

**Progressive reading.** Barron is the necessary predicate for the Fourteenth Amendment and for the selective incorporation doctrine that followed. On this view, the holding did not merely announce an interpretive rule but created a constitutional gap so large that remedying it required rewriting the relationship between the federal government and the states. Akhil Amar's work on the Bill of Rights argues that Reconstruction Republicans explicitly designed Section 1 of the Fourteenth Amendment to reverse Barron, with the Privileges or Immunities Clause intended as the primary incorporation vehicle. Slaughter-House Cases (1873) then narrowed that clause, forcing the Court to accomplish through the Due Process Clause what the Privileges or Immunities Clause had been drafted to do, producing the piecemeal and contested incorporation doctrine that still governs.

**Originalist debate.** The originalist literature splits sharply over whether Marshall's reading was correct on its own terms. Raoul Berger defended the Barron outcome, arguing that the historical record strongly supports Marshall's inference that the Bill of Rights was proposed and ratified as a limitation on federal power alone. William Crosskey took the opposite view, arguing that the Framing-era evidence, including the language of some of the earlier state proposals and the understanding of several ratifiers, supports an original public meaning applying the Bill of Rights to the states. McDonald v City of Chicago (2010) treats Barron as correctly decided on 1833 text and structure but simultaneously treats the Fourteenth Amendment as a deliberate reversal of its holding. Justice Thomas's concurrence in McDonald argues that the Privileges or Immunities Clause, not the Due Process Clause, was the textually correct incorporation vehicle and that the Court's modern doctrine has unnecessarily burdened the analysis by routing incorporation through substantive due process.

**Doctrinal consequence.** A persistent critique from both progressive and libertarian commentators: because the Court chose Due Process as the incorporation vehicle, every incorporation question is now entangled with substantive due process methodology, which in turn invites the Washington v Glucksberg / Dobbs style historical tests. A cleaner Privileges or Immunities incorporation would have separated the structural question (does this right bind the states) from the substantive question (what does this right protect).

## Key Points

- Establishes the pre-Fourteenth Amendment understanding: Bill of Rights binds only the federal government.
- Vote: unanimous. Written by Chief Justice Marshall.
- Superseded piecemeal through selective incorporation beginning with Chicago, Burlington and Quincy v Chicago (1897) for the Takings Clause.
- Cited in McDonald v City of Chicago (2010) as the historical starting point that the Fourteenth Amendment was designed to unwind.
