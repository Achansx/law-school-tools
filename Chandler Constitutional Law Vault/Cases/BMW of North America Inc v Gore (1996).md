# BMW of North America Inc v Gore (1996)

## Memory Jogger

> A $2 million punitive damages award for a $4,000 repainting nondisclosure is grossly excessive and violates due process; the Court's three guideposts -- reprehensibility, ratio, comparable sanctions -- invite substantive review of punitive awards and, per Professor Chandler, quietly resurrect Lochner-era SDP reasoning.

---

## Facts

BMW of North America followed a nationwide policy of repainting pre-delivery cars when repair costs were less than 3% of the vehicle's suggested retail price, without disclosing the repainting to dealers or customers. Dr. Ira Gore purchased a new BMW for approximately $40,750 in Alabama, and nine months later learned the car had been repainted before delivery. The repair cost had been approximately $601 -- less than 3% of the price. The diminution in market value caused by the nondisclosure was estimated at $4,000.

Gore sued BMW in Alabama state court. The jury awarded $4,000 in compensatory damages and $4 million in punitive damages, reflecting a ratio of 1,000:1. The Alabama Supreme Court reduced the punitive award to $2 million -- a ratio of 500:1 -- but still affirmed.

Alabama's nondisclosure statute imposed a maximum civil penalty of $2,000 for a violation. BMW's conduct was lawful in most other states at the time.

---

## Procedural History

A jury in Alabama state court awarded $4 million in punitive damages against BMW on a consumer-protection nondisclosure theory. The Alabama Supreme Court reduced the award to $2 million, finding the $4 million award excessive under state law, but affirmed. BMW petitioned for certiorari on the due process question.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority:** Stevens (joined by O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg -- 5-4)
- **Concurrence:** Breyer (joined by O'Connor and Souter) -- emphasized the need for appellate review of punitive awards and greater procedural guidance for juries
- **Dissent:** Scalia (joined by Thomas) -- argued the Due Process Clause imposes no substantive limits on punitive damages and the majority has invented a constitutional rule without textual basis
- **Dissent:** Ginsburg (joined by Rehnquist) -- argued the Court should not constitutionalize punitive damages review; state appellate review was sufficient to prevent abuse

---

## Holding

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment prohibits a state from imposing a "grossly excessive" punishment on a tortfeasor. Courts evaluate whether a punitive award is grossly excessive by reference to three guideposts: (1) the degree of reprehensibility of the defendant's conduct; (2) the disparity between the punitive award and the actual or potential harm suffered by the plaintiff; and (3) the difference between the punitive award and civil or criminal penalties authorized for comparable misconduct. A state may not impose punitive damages to deter or punish conduct that was lawful in other states. The $2 million award against BMW for a $4,000 loss was grossly excessive.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Justice Stevens grounded the majority's analysis in the due process requirement of fair notice: a person must be able to foresee not only that conduct will subject them to punishment but the approximate severity of that punishment. The three-guidepost framework gives content to this notice requirement.

On reprehensibility, Stevens found BMW's conduct relatively low on the scale: no physical harm, no deliberate false statements, no financial vulnerability in the victim. On ratio, the 500:1 ratio far exceeded anything the Court had previously approved; prior cases had found ratios of 4:1 borderline. On comparable sanctions, Alabama's civil penalty for nondisclosure was $2,000 -- the jury awarded one thousand times that amount.

Stevens also held that Alabama could not punish BMW for conduct in other states that was lawful where it occurred and caused no harm to Alabamans. This limits states to using punitive damages to protect their own citizens and economy, not to export their regulatory preferences nationwide.

Professor Chandler identifies BMW v Gore as "Lochner rising": the majority uses the same tool the Lochner-era Court used (substantive due process review of "arbitrary" state action) against punitive damages rather than maximum-hours laws. Williamson said the day was gone when courts struck down state laws as arbitrary; BMW v Gore shows that day may not have fully passed.

## Key Points

- Establishes three constitutional guideposts for reviewing punitive damages under the Due Process Clause: reprehensibility, ratio, and comparable sanctions.
- States may not use punitive damages to punish conduct that was lawful in other states and caused no harm to the imposing state's residents.
- Professor Chandler frames BMW as "Lochner rising" -- the return of substantive due process scrutiny of economic outcomes under the rubric of "arbitrary" state action.
- Scalia's dissent is the most doctrinally sharp critique: if Williamson says courts cannot police "arbitrary" economic regulation, BMW v Gore contradicts that holding.
- Closely paired with State Farm v Campbell (2003), which refined the ratio standard and suggested single-digit ratios are generally permissible.
- Tension with Williamson v Lee Optical: the same "arbitrary" language Williamson said courts should not apply to legislation re-enters through the punitive damages backdoor.
