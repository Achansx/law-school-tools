# Obergefell v Hodges (2015)

## Memory Jogger

> The Fourteenth Amendment requires every State to license same-sex marriages and to recognize same-sex marriages lawfully performed in other States.

---

## Facts

Fourteen same-sex couples and two men whose same-sex partners had died brought suit in federal district courts in Michigan, Ohio, Kentucky, and Tennessee, challenging state laws that defined marriage as a union between one man and one woman and refused to recognize same-sex marriages validly entered into in other jurisdictions. The named plaintiff, James Obergefell, married John Arthur in Maryland while Arthur was dying of ALS; Ohio refused to list Obergefell as the surviving spouse on Arthur's death certificate. Other plaintiffs sought to jointly adopt children, secure survivor benefits, obtain accurate birth certificates for children conceived together, and be listed as spouses on official records. The state laws at issue had been enacted through statute and, in most of the respondent States, also through state constitutional amendments adopted in the early 2000s in response to the prospect of judicial recognition of same-sex marriage.

The challenges rested on both the Due Process Clause and the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. Petitioners argued that the right to marry is a fundamental liberty that extends to same-sex couples on the same terms as opposite-sex couples, and that excluding them from civil marriage denied equal dignity and worked concrete legal harms in taxation, inheritance, parenting, medical decisionmaking, and dozens of other domains.

---

## Procedural History

In each of the four consolidated cases the federal district court ruled in favor of the same-sex couples. The Sixth Circuit consolidated the appeals and reversed, holding that neither the Due Process Clause nor the Equal Protection Clause required a State to license or recognize same-sex marriages, and that the question should be resolved through the democratic process rather than the courts. The Sixth Circuit decision created a circuit split with the Fourth, Seventh, Ninth, and Tenth Circuits, each of which had invalidated state same-sex marriage bans following United States v Windsor (2013).

The Supreme Court granted certiorari and limited review to two questions: whether the Fourteenth Amendment requires a State to license a marriage between two people of the same sex, and whether it requires a State to recognize a same-sex marriage lawfully licensed and performed in another State.

## Holding

The Fourteenth Amendment requires a State to license a marriage between two people of the same sex and to recognize a marriage between two people of the same sex when their marriage was lawfully licensed and performed out-of-State. The right to marry is a fundamental right inherent in the liberty of the person, protected by both the Due Process Clause and the Equal Protection Clause, and same-sex couples may not be deprived of that right. Baker v Nelson is overruled, and state laws excluding same-sex couples from civil marriage on the same terms as opposite-sex couples are invalid.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion (Kennedy)

Justice Kennedy grounded the holding in a synthesis of Due Process and Equal Protection reasoning. On liberty, he identified four principles and traditions demonstrating that the right to marry is fundamental under the Constitution and applies with equal force to same-sex couples: personal choice regarding marriage is inherent in the concept of individual autonomy; marriage is unlike any other union in its importance to the committed individuals; marriage safeguards children and families and draws meaning from related rights of childrearing, procreation, and education; and marriage is a keystone of the Nation's social order. On equality, he emphasized that the Due Process and Equal Protection Clauses are "connected in a profound way" and that denying same-sex couples access to marriage works a grave and continuing harm that serves to disrespect and subordinate them. Relying on Loving v Virginia, Zablocki v Redhail, and Turner v Safley, he rejected the Sixth Circuit's view that the question must be left to the political process, reasoning that fundamental rights may not be submitted to majority vote.

### Dissent (Roberts, C.J.)

Roberts's principal dissent (joined by Scalia and Thomas) rested on two arguments. First, the Constitution does not define marriage, and the Framers left that question to democratic self-governance. The five justices in the majority, he argued, substituted their own policy preferences for the considered judgments of millions of voters and dozens of state legislatures that had addressed the question through the democratic process. The Court has no warrant to resolve contested social questions of this kind; even if the majority's answer is correct as a matter of policy, the Constitution does not authorize the Court to impose it. Second, Roberts drew a sharp distinction between Obergefell and Loving v Virginia. The interracial marriage ban in Loving did not redefine the institution of marriage; it extended access to an institution that was already defined as the union of a man and a woman to couples of different races. Removing racial barriers "did not change what a marriage was any more than integrating schools changed what a school was." Obergefell, by contrast, redefines the core structure of the institution. Roberts concluded: "The fundamental right to marry does not include a right to make a State change its definition of marriage." The majority's reasoning would apply with equal logical force to polygamous unions, a consequence Roberts argued the majority could not coherently foreclose.

### Dissent (Scalia)

Scalia's brief dissent (joined by Thomas) focused on institutional and methodological objections rather than substantive marriage policy. He characterized the majority opinion as a "judicial Putsch" driven by raw power rather than law: "The five Justices who compose today's majority are entirely comfortable concluding that every State violated the Constitution for all of the 135 years between the Fourteenth Amendment's ratification and Massachusetts' permitting of same-sex marriages in 2003." Scalia's signature contribution was his attack on Kennedy's rhetorical style. He catalogued Kennedy's "mystical aphorisms of the fortune cookie" -- passages such as the "dignity of a union" and "constellation of benefits" -- as evidence of a decision disconnected from legal reasoning and insulated from scrutiny precisely because its premises could not be falsified. Scalia's deeper point was democratic: by removing the marriage question from the political arena, the majority foreclosed the kind of organic, state-by-state resolution that he viewed as constitutionally appropriate for contested social questions. Unlike Roberts, Scalia engaged little with the doctrinal substance; his dissent was a methodological protest against the Court's institutional role.

### Dissent (Thomas)

Thomas's dissent (joined by Scalia) challenged the majority's foundational conception of liberty. Drawing on Blackstone and the founding era, Thomas argued that "liberty" in the Due Process Clauses "most likely refers to 'the power of locomotion, of changing situation, or removing one's person to whatsoever place one's own inclination may direct; without imprisonment or restraint, unless by due course of law.'" On that understanding, liberty is freedom from physical government restraint, not an entitlement to positive government benefits or recognition. The state's refusal to license or recognize a same-sex marriage does not imprison anyone, seize property, or physically constrain movement; it simply declines to confer a legal status. Thomas thus argued the Due Process Clause was not implicated at all. He also criticized Kennedy's dignity analysis: dignity, in Thomas's view, is inherent in persons as created in the image of God and cannot be taken away by government non-recognition. The government's refusal to call a relationship a "marriage" does not strip the parties of their human dignity. Thomas expressed concern that the decision would be weaponized against religious dissenters, warning that the majority's analogy between traditional marriage laws and racial discrimination laws would be exploited to stigmatize anyone holding traditional beliefs about marriage.

### Dissent (Alito)

Alito's dissent (joined by Scalia and Thomas) argued primarily from democratic theory and warned of consequences for religious liberty. He contended the Constitution takes no position on the definition of marriage, which has historically been understood as the union of a man and a woman for the purpose of channeling reproduction into stable households. On Alito's account, the question whether to extend the marriage institution to same-sex couples is a legitimate policy question on which reasonable people disagree, and the democratic process -- not the courts -- is the appropriate forum for resolution. Alito's most pointed contribution concerned religious dissenters. He warned that the majority's comparison of traditional marriage laws to anti-miscegenation laws creates an equality frame that will be used to classify anyone holding traditional beliefs as morally equivalent to a racist bigot. Individuals, schools, and institutions with traditional views on marriage will face social pressure and potential legal liability. Alito closed by emphasizing the Court's institutional overreach: by imposing a nationwide constitutional rule, the majority had made it impossible for states to experiment with approaches that might protect religious conscience alongside expanded marriage rights -- a structural loss of pluralism that the democratic process could have preserved.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

1. Ohio refuses to list James Obergefell as the surviving spouse on his late husband's death certificate, relying on Ohio's state constitutional amendment defining marriage as opposite-sex only. Under Obergefell's recognition prong, Ohio must recognize a valid out-of-state same-sex marriage for all legal purposes, including vital records. The recognition holding is categorical: "there is no lawful basis for a State to refuse to recognize a lawful same-sex marriage performed in another State on the ground of its same-sex character." Ohio's refusal directly violates the holding and would be struck down.

2. A state statute restricts survivor benefits under state intestacy law to spouses of opposite-sex marriages, denying a same-sex widow inheritance rights her late spouse intended her to receive. The statute cannot stand under Obergefell. Kennedy's majority specifically identified "inheritance and property rights" as among the "constellation of benefits" that the state links to marriage and from which same-sex couples were unconstitutionally excluded. The equal-dignity holding extends to all legal incidents of the marital status, not merely the license itself.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

3. A county clerk who is an ordained minister refuses on religious grounds to perform civil same-sex weddings. Obergefell explicitly reserved this question: "The First Amendment ensures that religious organizations and persons are given proper protection as they seek to understand and live out their beliefs." A government official using her governmental authority to refuse licenses may be a separate issue, but a private officiant's refusal is squarely within First Amendment religious-exercise protection. Kennedy's explicit reservation means Obergefell does not compel religious officiants to participate in ceremonies that violate their beliefs.

4. A state refuses to license a polyamorous three-person marriage. Roberts in dissent pressed this hypothetical, and Kennedy declined to answer it directly. Applying Kennedy's four-principles analysis, the right to marry is grounded in its historical role as a two-person commitment with a specific legal and social structure; nothing in the majority's reasoning compels extension to three-person arrangements. The "in all States" holding is specifically limited to same-sex couples, and the structural equality argument (same-sex couples seek access to the same institution opposite-sex couples enjoy) does not translate to plural marriage (which seeks a different institutional form entirely).

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

5. A private religiously affiliated university refuses to provide married-student housing to a same-sex couple legally married in another state. Obergefell's religious-liberty reservation was written for religious organizations in their internal affairs, but the university is a place of public accommodation arguably subject to state anti-discrimination law. The outcome turns on whether the state's public-accommodation law applies to universities, whether a religious exemption is available under state law or the Free Exercise Clause, and whether the university's religious mission is sufficiently integrated into housing as to make the exemption coherent. Neither Obergefell nor subsequent cases (Masterpiece Cakeshop, 303 Creative) fully resolve this intersection of Obergefell's recognition holding with First Amendment limits on compelled service.

## Critique

Conservative and originalist critique: Roberts's democratic-process argument is among the most intellectually serious dissents in modern constitutional history. The Court does not have a principled basis, on this view, for selecting which unenumerated rights to recognize as fundamental. Glucksberg v Washington (1997) required that unenumerated rights be "deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition" and "implicit in the concept of ordered liberty" -- criteria same-sex marriage did not satisfy as of 2015 under any conventional historical test. Kennedy's level-of-generality move (defining the right as "the right to marry" rather than "the right to same-sex marriage") is exactly the move Scalia's Michael H v Gerald D Footnote 6 would have forbidden. If level of generality is infinitely manipulable, Glucksberg is meaningless as a restraint. From this perspective, Kennedy's opinion is methodologically indistinguishable from Roe v Wade in its analytical permissiveness, which is why Justice Thomas in Dobbs identified Obergefell as a candidate for reconsideration alongside Roe and Lawrence.

Progressive critique: Kennedy's dignity-based reasoning, while emotionally resonant, is doctrinally thin in ways that created instability. The "constellation of benefits" and "individual dignity" frameworks are not legal tests; they are exhortations. Scholars including William Eskridge and Kenji Yoshino observed that Kennedy could have grounded the holding more durably in the Equal Protection Clause -- same-sex couples are treated differently from opposite-sex couples with no legitimate government justification -- rather than in substantive due process, which relies on the contested fundamental-rights methodology. The equal-protection route would have produced the same outcome with a more stable doctrinal foundation, less vulnerable to the post-Dobbs challenge Thomas issued. Additionally, Obergefell's explicit religious-liberty reservation, without any doctrinal guidance on how to implement it, left an unresolved tension between marriage equality and religious organizations that the Court has struggled to address coherently in subsequent cases (Masterpiece, 303 Creative).

## Key Points

- Fifth Supreme Court decision in the sexual-orientation liberty/equality line, following Romer v Evans (1996), Lawrence v Texas (2003), United States v Windsor (2013), and preceding Pavan v Smith (2017) and Bostock v Clayton County (2020).
- Fuses Due Process and Equal Protection reasoning rather than treating them as alternative grounds.
- Overrules Baker v Nelson (1972), the one-line summary dismissal treating the same-sex marriage question as lacking a substantial federal question.
- Explicitly reserves First Amendment rights of religious organizations and individuals to continue teaching religious doctrines about marriage.
- Post-Dobbs, Obergefell's methodology (unenumerated fundamental rights grounded in dignity and history-and-tradition) has been identified by Justice Thomas's Dobbs concurrence as a candidate for reconsideration.
