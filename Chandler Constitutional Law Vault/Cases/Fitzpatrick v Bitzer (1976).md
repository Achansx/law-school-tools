# Fitzpatrick v Bitzer (1976)

## Memory Jogger

> The Court holds that Congress may abrogate the Eleventh Amendment immunity of states when exercising its Section 5 Fourteenth Amendment enforcement power, allowing private suits for money damages in employment discrimination cases under Title VII.

---

## Facts

A class of male state employees in Connecticut sued the state under Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, alleging that the state's pension and retirement plan discriminated against male employees on the basis of sex. Congress had extended Title VII's coverage to state employers through the Equal Employment Opportunity Act of 1972 (the 1972 Amendments), and expressly subjected states to liability for backpay and attorneys' fees.

Connecticut invoked Eleventh Amendment immunity, arguing that a state could not be sued in federal court by its own citizens without the state's consent. The plaintiffs argued that Congress, acting under Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment, had the power to abrogate that immunity when enforcing the Amendment's equal protection guarantees.

The constitutional question was whether Congress could authorize private suits for retroactive monetary relief against states -- a form of relief normally barred by Eleventh Amendment immunity -- when acting pursuant to Section 5.

---

## Procedural History

The district court upheld Title VII's application to the states and awarded the plaintiffs retroactive relief. The Second Circuit affirmed in part but limited retroactive relief to a period after the 1972 Amendments. The Supreme Court affirmed, holding that Congress had constitutional authority to abrogate state sovereign immunity under Section 5.

## Holding

Congress has the power under Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment to authorize federal courts to award retroactive money damages and attorneys' fees against a state government that has discriminated in employment on the basis of sex, notwithstanding the Eleventh Amendment. The Eleventh Amendment and the principle of state sovereignty it embodies are necessarily limited by the enforcement provisions of Section 5, because the Fourteenth Amendment itself embodies significant limitations on state authority that Congress is expressly empowered to enforce.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Justice Rehnquist's majority reasoned structurally: the Fourteenth Amendment was ratified after the Eleventh Amendment and, unlike Article I, it expressly limits state authority. When Congress acts pursuant to Section 5 -- an express plenary grant to enforce those limitations -- the constitutional balance shifts. The state sovereignty that the Eleventh Amendment was designed to protect has already been circumscribed by the Fourteenth Amendment's own terms. Congress's Section 5 power is therefore not constrained by the Eleventh Amendment in the same way that an Article I Commerce Clause power would be.

The majority distinguished this from ordinary Article I legislation: a Commerce Clause statute that purported to subject states to suit would face an Eleventh Amendment bar. But when the constitutional basis for the legislation is Section 5 itself -- a provision of the same Amendment that imposes obligations on states -- those two constitutional provisions operate in harmony rather than in conflict.

Applying this framework, the majority upheld the 1972 Amendments as a valid Section 5 exercise (Title VII extended to states) and approved the award of retroactive monetary relief.

Two structural moves do the work. First, the Fourteenth Amendment is later-in-time. The Eleventh Amendment (1795) responded to Chisholm v Georgia by pulling federal jurisdiction back from suits against states brought by out-of-state citizens; the Fourteenth Amendment (1868) was ratified with the Civil War settlement and expressly reorganized state-federal authority over the protection of persons. Because the later Amendment's substantive clauses are, as Rehnquist puts it, "expressly directed at the States," the earlier immunity shield cannot be read to nullify the later enforcement obligation. Second, Section 5 is a plenary grant of legislative authority to enforce those substantive clauses by "appropriate legislation," language the Court in Katzenbach v Morgan (1966) had already read as giving Congress McCulloch-style means-ends latitude. Putting the two together, Rehnquist concludes that abrogation of state sovereign immunity is an exercise "constitutionally impermissible in other contexts" (like Article I legislation) but permissible here because the enforcement power itself is calibrated against the same state authority that the Eleventh Amendment otherwise protects.

A second strand of the reasoning -- less discussed but doctrinally consequential -- is the clear-statement predicate. The opinion accepts as a given that Congress exercised Section 5 (legislative reports explicitly invoked it) and that Title VII as amended in 1972 authorizes private damages actions against state employers. The Court thus never has to confront whether a Section 5 statute silent on abrogation would suffice. Atascadero State Hospital v Scanlon (1985) and later Seminole Tribe of Florida v Florida (1996) would formalize the clear-statement rule on exactly this point, requiring that congressional intent to abrogate be "unmistakably clear in the language of the statute."

Downstream, Fitzpatrick becomes the floor for the Section 5 abrogation doctrine and the foil for Seminole Tribe. Seminole Tribe forecloses Article I abrogation (the Indian Commerce Clause cannot support suits against states), leaving Section 5 as the sole path. [[Cases/City of Boerne v Flores (1997)|Boerne]] then polices how far that path can be stretched: even a Section 5 statute must satisfy congruence-and-proportionality to count as "enforcement" rather than substantive redefinition of Fourteenth Amendment rights. The arc from Fitzpatrick through Boerne to Florida Prepaid, Garrett, and Allen v Cooper is the story of a broad enforcement authority being progressively tier-matched to the scrutiny of the underlying right.

### Concurrence in judgment (Brennan, joined by Marshall)

Brennan reached the same result by a different and narrower route. The Eleventh Amendment, on his reading, bars only federal-court suits brought against states by citizens of other states -- the literal text -- not suits by a state's own citizens, which Hans v Louisiana had (erroneously, in Brennan's view) folded into the immunity doctrine by extratextual inference. Because the Fitzpatrick plaintiffs were Connecticut employees suing Connecticut, the Eleventh Amendment "is not literally applicable to this situation," citing Marshall's dictum in Cohens v Virginia and Brennan's own dissent in Employees v Missouri Public Health Dept. That premise moots the need to reach the Section 5 abrogation question at all: Connecticut was left only with a common-law sovereign immunity defense, which Brennan thought Congress had the authority to override under either the Commerce Clause or Section 5.

The concurrence is doctrinally important because it flags the Hans critique that later resurfaces in Justice Stevens's dissent in Seminole Tribe and in academic literature (e.g., John Manning, William Fletcher) questioning whether sovereign immunity is properly constitutional or properly common-law. If Brennan's narrow reading had commanded a majority, Section 5 abrogation doctrine would have been largely unnecessary -- states would have no immunity against suits by their own citizens to begin with.

### Concurrence in judgment (Stevens)

Stevens concurred in the judgment but would have rested it on the Commerce Clause rather than Section 5. In his view the Commerce Clause is "broad enough to support federal legislation regulating the terms and conditions of state employment" and therefore supplies the necessary affirmative authority for the 1972 Title VII Amendments, even though Congress formally invoked Section 5. He separately expressed doubt whether the Amendments were in fact "needed to secure the guarantees of the Fourteenth Amendment" within the meaning of Katzenbach v Morgan. On the immunity question, Stevens relied on Ex parte Young: the Connecticut pension statute had been held invalid, so the suit was effectively against state officers enforcing an unconstitutional statute, which Young places outside the Eleventh Amendment's reach.

Two points of doctrinal friction follow. First, Stevens's Commerce Clause theory would be sharply curtailed two decades later by Seminole Tribe, which held that Article I powers cannot abrogate state sovereign immunity. Had Stevens's rationale carried the day, Seminole Tribe would have had to confront the question head-on rather than treat Fitzpatrick as an already-settled Section 5 exception. Second, Stevens's reliance on Ex parte Young for the retroactive-relief half of the case is in tension with Edelman v Jordan (1974), which had limited Young to prospective relief and barred retroactive monetary awards from the state treasury -- a tension the majority avoided by grounding abrogation in Section 5 rather than in the Young workaround.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

**(1) Section 5 statute abrogating immunity for race discrimination in state hiring.** Congress amends a civil-rights hiring statute to expressly authorize private damages actions against state employers that engage in racial discrimination, citing Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment and detailing a record of contemporary state discrimination.
*Why: Race discrimination is the core Fourteenth Amendment concern, triggering strict scrutiny; the statute targets a classification the Amendment itself prohibits; Congress makes an unmistakably clear statement of intent to abrogate; and the remedy (private damages) is directly responsive to the constitutional violation. Fitzpatrick's Section 5 rationale applies a fortiori, and Boerne's congruence-and-proportionality test is easily satisfied.*

**(2) Section 5 statute against state disenfranchisement of language minorities, pattern of evidence.** Congress passes a Voting Rights Act amendment authorizing private suits for money damages against states that impose English-only literacy requirements after a legislative record documenting systemic exclusion of Spanish-speaking voters.
*Why: Voting is a core Fourteenth Amendment interest per [[Cases/Katzenbach v Morgan (1966)|Katzenbach v Morgan]]; the legislative record establishes a pattern of Fourteenth Amendment violations; the remedy is proportionate to the documented harm. Section 5 abrogation is valid and Fitzpatrick controls.*

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

**(1) Pure Article I Commerce Clause abrogation.** Congress passes a federal wage-and-hour statute authorizing private damages actions against state employers, invoking only the Commerce Clause.
*Why: Seminole Tribe (1996) forecloses Article I abrogation of state sovereign immunity. Fitzpatrick's reasoning turns on Section 5's unique status as part of the same Fourteenth Amendment that itself limits state authority; no Article I power carries that structural pedigree. The statute would be invalid as to state defendants.*

**(2) Silent abrogation -- Section 5 rooted but no express immunity-waiver language.** Congress passes a Section 5 anti-discrimination statute that confers a private right of action but does not expressly state that states are liable for damages.
*Why: The Atascadero/Seminole Tribe clear-statement rule requires that congressional intent to abrogate be "unmistakably clear in the language of the statute." A statute rooted in Section 5 but silent on abrogation fails the clear-statement requirement, so state sovereign immunity is not abrogated even though the Section 5 predicate is present.*

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

**Mixed-authority statute with disability employment discrimination provisions.** Congress amends the ADA to expressly authorize private damages actions against state employers, citing both the Commerce Clause and Section 5 for disability discrimination in public employment.
*Why: The answer depends on how tightly Section 5 is read. Under Garrett (2001), disability discrimination receives only rational basis review, so Section 5 authority is available only to the extent Congress has documented unconstitutional state discrimination (congruence-and-proportionality). If the record supports Section 5, Fitzpatrick's abrogation rationale controls regardless of the supplemental Commerce Clause citation. If the record does not support Section 5 as to public-employment disability claims, the Article I portion cannot save abrogation under Seminole Tribe, and immunity stands. The outcome turns on the congruence-and-proportionality record, not on the bare existence of a Section 5 citation.*

## Critique

**Progressive critique (structural equality).** The Fitzpatrick/Seminole Tribe division produces a doctrinally coherent but normatively odd result: federal rights rooted in the Fourteenth Amendment can be enforced through private damages actions against states, but the same federal rights rooted in Article I (e.g., federal statutory rights protecting workers, patent holders, copyright holders, disability plaintiffs) cannot. As Erwin Chemerinsky and other structural-equality scholars argue, sovereign immunity doctrine leaves enforcement of federal statutory schemes hostage to the nature of the underlying constitutional predicate rather than the severity of the state's conduct. Allen v Cooper's holding that the CRCA cannot abrogate is the natural -- and troubling -- extension: a state can willfully pirate copyrighted works with no remedy in damages, because copyright is Article I rather than Fourteenth Amendment. The progressive critique is that Fitzpatrick's framework, far from limiting state sovereign immunity, has helped ossify it.

**Originalist/textualist critique (Hans-centered).** The textualist objection picks up Brennan's thread and cuts the other way. If the Eleventh Amendment's text bars only suits against a state by citizens of another state, then Hans v Louisiana (extending immunity to same-state citizen suits) and Fitzpatrick (which accepts the Hans extension and then works around it via Section 5) are both built on questionable foundations. John Manning and William Fletcher have argued that a textualist methodology would reject Hans, making Fitzpatrick's Section 5 route a sophisticated solution to a problem that textualism would not have created. The originalist reply (Clarence Thomas's separate-opinion trajectory from Seminole Tribe through Alden v Maine to Franchise Tax Board v Hyatt) takes sovereign immunity as a structural constitutional principle predating and independent of the Eleventh Amendment text, vindicating Hans and, with it, the need for a Section 5-based abrogation mechanism.

**Doctrinal open questions.** (1) The congruence-and-proportionality test from Boerne was not on the books in 1976. Fitzpatrick itself speaks of Section 5 as "plenary," and does not ask whether Title VII's application to sex-discriminatory state pension rules was a congruent response to documented Fourteenth Amendment violations. Modern doctrine imposes an after-the-fact evidentiary floor that Fitzpatrick did not. (2) The line between prospective relief (Ex parte Young) and retroactive damages (Edelman v Jordan) is preserved for Article I suits but collapsed for Section 5 suits -- why Fourteenth Amendment enforcement should uniquely permit retroactive payments from the state treasury is a structural question that Fitzpatrick answers by fiat rather than argument. (3) The clear-statement rule operates as a surprise-avoidance doctrine, but the underlying notice interest is satisfied as soon as Congress's intent is clear to the state. The formalism of Atascadero/Seminole Tribe requiring "unmistakably clear" language in the statute itself -- not legislative history, not structure -- is harder to justify on notice grounds than on federalism-protective grounds.

## Key Points

- Establishes that Congress may abrogate Eleventh Amendment state sovereign immunity when acting under Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment.
- The constitutional basis: Section 5 power is "plenary" and the Fourteenth Amendment itself limits state authority, making the Eleventh Amendment limitation inapplicable to Section 5 enforcement.
- Distinguished from Article I-based abrogation: Seminole Tribe of Florida v Florida (1996) later holds that Article I alone cannot abrogate state immunity.
- Congress must make its intent to abrogate "unmistakably clear" (clear statement rule; later formalized in Atascadero State Hospital v Scanlon and Seminole Tribe).
- The Fitzpatrick exception (Section 5 abrogation) is a key doctrinal distinction in sovereign immunity analysis.
