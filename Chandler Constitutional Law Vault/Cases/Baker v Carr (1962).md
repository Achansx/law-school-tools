# Baker v Carr (1962)

## Memory Jogger

> Malapportionment claims under the Equal Protection Clause are justiciable, and the political question doctrine is about federal separation of powers, not federalism.

---

## Facts

The Tennessee General Assembly had last apportioned its legislative districts in 1901. Over the next sixty years, urbanization and population shifts produced gross disparities. By 1960, one vote in rural Moore County was worth roughly nineteen times one vote in urban Shelby County for purposes of electing state senators. The Tennessee Constitution required decennial reapportionment based on population, but the legislature had not redrawn the lines since 1901, and prior state-court challenges had been dismissed.

Charles W. Baker and other Tennessee voters residing in underrepresented counties sued Secretary of State Joe Carr and other state officials in federal court. They alleged that the 1901 apportionment statute violated their Fourteenth Amendment right to equal protection by diluting their votes. They sought declaratory and injunctive relief ordering reapportionment or, alternatively, at-large elections.

---

## Procedural History

A three-judge panel of the U.S. District Court for the Middle District of Tennessee dismissed the suit, relying principally on Colegrove v Green (1946) and other Guaranty Clause cases, and reasoning that the complaint raised a nonjusticiable political question and that the federal court lacked jurisdiction over the subject matter.

The plaintiffs appealed directly to the Supreme Court under then-existing provisions for appeals from three-judge district courts. The Court heard argument in April 1961, ordered reargument in October 1961, and decided the case in March 1962. The Court reversed 6-2 and remanded.

## Holding

A challenge alleging that a state legislative apportionment statute deprives voters of equal protection of the laws in violation of the Fourteenth Amendment presents a justiciable cause of action. The federal district court has subject-matter jurisdiction over the claim, the plaintiffs have standing, and the matter does not raise a nonjusticiable political question.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Justice Brennan separated three jurisdictional questions that the district court had conflated: subject-matter jurisdiction, standing, and justiciability. On subject-matter jurisdiction, the complaint clearly "arises under" the Constitution and satisfies 28 U.S.C. § 1343. On standing, voters alleging dilution of their own votes have the requisite personal stake under Colegrove itself. The heart of the opinion was the political question doctrine. Brennan conducted an extended review of past cases, concluding that political questions are "primarily a function of the separation of powers" between federal branches, not a shield for state governments. He distilled six factors that characterize a nonjusticiable political question, including a textually demonstrable commitment to a coordinate branch and a lack of judicially discoverable and manageable standards. Equal protection challenges to state apportionment met none of them; the fact that earlier Guaranty Clause claims were nonjusticiable did not foreclose a separate Fourteenth Amendment claim.

### Concurrence (Douglas)

Justice Douglas argued that the Fourteenth Amendment itself supplies a judicially manageable standard for voting rights claims: the prohibition on "invidious discrimination." He reasoned that the right to vote is inherent in the republican form of government envisaged by Article IV, section 4, and that while states may fix voter qualifications within limits, any classification that disfavors voters in a constitutionally unjustifiable way violates Equal Protection. Douglas accepted that universal numerical equality is not required -- there is room for weighting -- but insisted that the floor against invidious distinctions is fully workable by courts. In his view, Brennan's six-factor political-question framework was not necessary to resolve this case; the Fourteenth Amendment's "invidious discrimination" test was clear enough standing alone.

### Concurrence (Clark)

Justice Clark went further than Brennan and would have decided the case on the merits for the plaintiffs. Examining the apportionment data, he concluded that Tennessee's scheme was "a crazy quilt without rational basis" -- a pattern so irrational and disproportionate that no defensible policy explanation could account for it. Clark rejected Harlan's rational-basis defense as "entirely fanciful," arguing the dissenters could not identify any actual policy the 1901 apportionment reflected. Because the evidence so overwhelmingly favored the challengers, Clark believed the district court had not merely jurisdiction but a clear answer: "Tennessee is guilty of a clear violation of the state constitution and of the rights of the plaintiffs." He would not have remanded for further fact-finding.

### Concurrence (Stewart)

Justice Stewart's brief concurrence was pointedly narrow. He emphasized that the majority decided only three things: that subject-matter jurisdiction existed, that the plaintiffs had standing, and that the controversy was justiciable. The Court said nothing about what Equal Protection required on the merits of apportionment challenges, what standards would govern any particular scheme, or what remedy a court could order. Stewart stressed that the Fourteenth Amendment affords states wide discretion in enacting laws affecting different groups, and that the burden of proving unconstitutionality rests on the challenger. His concurrence served as a caution against reading the majority opinion as resolving the substantive question of what apportionment standards the Constitution demands.

### Dissent (Frankfurter)

Justice Frankfurter's 60-page dissent -- his last major opinion before retirement -- argued that the majority had committed "a massive repudiation" of a dozen cases and plunged the Court into a "political thicket" it could not navigate by legal standards. His core objection was that courts possess no neutral, principled criterion for evaluating legislative apportionment. Any formula a court selected -- equal population, geographic units, hybrid weighting -- would itself be a policy choice, not a legal determination. Frankfurter warned that the Court's authority "ultimately rests on sustained public confidence in its moral sanction," which is undermined when the Court injects itself into "the clash of political forces." He read the historical record as uniformly treating apportionment as a matter for the political process alone, and characterized Brennan's separation of Guaranty Clause cases from Equal Protection claims as a formalistic maneuver that ignored the substantive identity of the two questions.

### Dissent (Harlan)

Justice Harlan's separate dissent addressed the merits that Frankfurter declined to reach. He argued that the Equal Protection Clause imposes no one-person-one-vote requirement and that Tennessee's 1901 scheme had a rational basis. Citing MacDougall v. Green (1948), Harlan reasoned that distributing electoral strength among geographic units rather than strictly by population census is a legitimate legislative policy choice -- no more irrational than levying a property tax rather than an income tax. States may rationally decide to give weight to geographic interests, regional communities, and minority rural populations. Harlan viewed Clark's "crazy quilt" characterization as a polemic that ignored the legislature's defensible judgment about rural representation, and argued that the Fourteenth Amendment "permits the States a wide scope of discretion in enacting laws which affect some groups of citizens differently than others."

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

1. A state legislature redraws congressional districts with population deviations of 40% between the largest and smallest district, consistently overrepresenting rural counties. Voters in underrepresented urban districts sue in federal court under the Fourteenth Amendment. Under Baker's holding, the federal court has subject-matter jurisdiction, the voters have standing as individuals disadvantaged by the disparity, and the challenge is justiciable. On the merits, Wesberry v. Sanders (1964) -- applying Baker to congressional districts -- governs, and the 40% deviation would almost certainly fail the strict-equality standard applicable to congressional apportionment.
2. A challenge to a state legislative apportionment scheme where the most rural county has 50 votes' worth of representation for every 1 vote in the most urban county -- a disparity even starker than Tennessee's 19:1 ratio. Baker controls: the federal court has jurisdiction, standing, and justiciability. On the merits, Reynolds v. Sims (1964) -- the direct follow-on to Baker -- held that the Equal Protection Clause requires "substantially equal" population across state legislative districts. A 50:1 disparity is constitutionally indefensible under Reynolds, and the case comes out the same way on strengthened facts.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

3. Plaintiffs challenge a state's ballot initiative process as violating their right to a "republican form of government" under Article IV, section 4, because direct-democracy initiatives allow simple majorities to override representative governance. This claim rests on the Guaranty Clause, not the Equal Protection Clause. Baker explicitly preserved the rule that Guaranty Clause challenges remain nonjusticiable political questions (citing Luther v. Borden and Pacific States Telephone). The majority's holding was premised on the distinction between Equal Protection claims (justiciable) and Guaranty Clause claims (not). The district court must dismiss.
4. Plaintiffs challenge a congressional map as a partisan gerrymander, arguing that it systematically dilutes the votes of supporters of one political party in violation of equal protection and the First Amendment. After Rucho v. Common Cause (2019), such claims present nonjusticiable political questions in federal court. Applying Baker's second factor, the Rucho Court found no judicially discoverable and manageable standard for deciding when partisan bias crosses the constitutional line -- unlike racial or population-based claims, courts have no neutral baseline from which to judge partisan intent. Baker opened apportionment litigation; Rucho closes it again for purely partisan claims by applying Baker's own framework.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

5. A state with a bicameral legislature deliberately models its upper chamber on the U.S. Senate, giving each county one seat regardless of population. The state argues the federal analogy insulates the scheme: if the U.S. Senate survives constitutional scrutiny, a state senate organized identically must be permissible. Baker's justiciability holding clearly controls, so the federal court has jurisdiction. Reynolds v. Sims (1964) squarely rejected this "federal analogy" defense -- the federal structure arises from unique historical compromises among sovereign states and cannot be replicated at the intrastate level. The state loses on the merits under Reynolds. The fence-sitter tension is that Harlan's rational-basis point (geographic representation as a distinct interest) retains theoretical force; the historical and structural arguments the state makes are not frivolous; and political theory offers genuine support for territorial representation as an alternative to purely numerical democracy -- but current doctrine forecloses the claim.

## Critique

Baker is the cornerstone of the democratic-legitimacy strand of constitutional theory. From a progressive perspective, malapportionment had for decades entrenched rural white minorities at the expense of urban and suburban voters -- often voters of color -- with no remedy through the political process, since the legislature that could redress the grievance was the very beneficiary of the distortion. Courts were the only institution capable of breaking the self-reinforcing cycle. Baker is therefore not counter-majoritarian but restorative: it forced a correction to a structural failure that the majoritarian process itself could not fix. Reynolds v. Sims and Wesberry v. Sanders, built directly on Baker, produced a nationwide redistribution of political power toward urban and suburban majorities.

The originalist critique, pressed most forcefully by Frankfurter and later by Alexander Bickel and Robert Bork, questions whether the Equal Protection Clause was ever understood to reach apportionment. The Framers of the Fourteenth Amendment operated within a political system that included the malapportioned U.S. Senate, and Reconstruction-era legislators never argued the amendment would mandate equal-population apportionment. The "one person, one vote" gloss on Equal Protection has no textual foundation -- it is a policy preference dressed in constitutional language. Frankfurter's institutional objection also carries independent weight: by mandating redistricting across all fifty states, the Court embroiled itself in a generation of structural supervision that Bickel warned would drain the Court's moral capital. Rucho v. Common Cause (2019), which closed the door on partisan gerrymandering claims for lack of judicially manageable standards, echoes Frankfurter's core insight that some apportionment judgments are beyond judicial competence.

## Key Points

- Supplies the six-factor test still cited today to identify political questions (e.g., Zivotofsky, Rucho).
- Vote: 6-2 (Whittaker not participating). Frankfurter's dissent is his last major opinion before retirement.
- Doctrinal bridge to Reynolds v Sims (1964) and Wesberry v Sanders (1964), which applied Baker on the merits.
- Distinguishes Guaranty Clause cases (Luther v Borden, Pacific States) as prudentially off-limits while leaving Equal Protection open.
