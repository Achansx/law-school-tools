# West Coast Hotel Co v Parrish (1937)

## Memory Jogger

> The 5-4 decision that ended the Lochner era by upholding Washington State's minimum wage for women, explicitly overruling Adkins v Children's Hospital and establishing that liberty under the Constitution is not absolute but is liberty within a social organization subject to reasonable regulation.

---

## Facts

Washington State enacted a minimum wage law for women and minors in 1913. The Industrial Welfare Committee set a minimum wage of $14.50 per 48-hour week for hotel workers. Elsie Parrish, a chambermaid at the Cascadian Hotel in Wenatchee operated by West Coast Hotel Company, was paid below that rate. She and her husband sued for the difference between her actual wages and the statutory minimum.

West Coast Hotel argued that the minimum wage law violated the liberty of contract protected by the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause, relying on *Adkins v Children's Hospital* (1923), in which the Supreme Court had struck down a similar District of Columbia minimum wage law for women as an unconstitutional interference with freedom of contract.

The case arrived at the Court in the political context of President Roosevelt's Court-packing plan, proposed in February 1937 after the Court had blocked major New Deal legislation. Justice Owen Roberts's vote switch in this case produced the famous "switch in time that saved nine."

---

## Procedural History

The trial court ruled for West Coast Hotel, but the Washington Supreme Court reversed, upholding the minimum wage law. West Coast Hotel appealed to the U.S. Supreme Court. The Court had previously granted certiorari in a case involving a similar New York law (*Tipaldo*, 1936), in which it had struck down the law — a result widely criticized as applying Adkins too rigidly. West Coast Hotel was argued on December 16 and 17, 1936; the decision was handed down on March 29, 1937, during the height of the Court-packing controversy.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority:** Hughes (joined by Brandeis, Stone, Roberts, Cardozo)
- **Dissent:** Sutherland (joined by Van Devanter, McReynolds, Butler) — argued that liberty of contract remained a constitutionally protected right and that Adkins was correctly decided

---

## Holding

Washington State's minimum wage law for women and minors does not violate the Fourteenth Amendment. *Adkins v Children's Hospital* is expressly overruled. Liberty under the Constitution is not absolute and uncontrollable but is liberty in a social organization that requires the protection of law against evils that menace health, safety, morals, and welfare. Regulation that is reasonable in relation to its subject and adopted in the interests of the community constitutes due process. The state's interest in preventing the exploitation of workers at substandard wages — exploitation that shifts the cost of inadequate wages onto the public through relief — justifies the minimum wage requirement.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Chief Justice Hughes framed the central question as whether the Constitution prohibits the state from fixing minimum wages. He began by rejecting the premise that the liberty of contract is unconstrained: the Constitution "does not recognize an absolute and uncontrollable liberty" and "liberty in each of its phases has its history and connotation." The liberty safeguarded, Hughes wrote, is liberty within a social organization requiring protection from evils that menace health, safety, morals, and welfare. He invoked the Lochner-era caveat that liberty of contract is subject to "reasonable" regulation in the community's interests, but gave that caveat far more force than the Lochner Court had. Hughes then overruled *Adkins* on the ground that its rejection of minimum wages for women was inconsistent with the Court's own prior reasoning. He noted that the bargaining inequality between a woman in need of employment and an employer gave the legislature a sufficient basis to intervene, and that the public bore the costs of substandard wages through relief expenditures — a rationale the Court accepted without requiring empirical proof. The decision effectively closed the Lochner era without explicitly renouncing the broader substantive due process methodology.

Three features of Hughes's opinion merit closer analysis. First, Hughes conspicuously did not overrule *Lochner* itself. He targeted *Adkins*, the 1923 minimum-wage case for women, and left the Lochner-line liberty-of-contract precedents formally on the books. The reason is partly prudential (a narrower ground commands wider internal assent) and partly doctrinal (Adkins had already been criticized as an outlier even among Lochner-era observers for treating women workers as bargaining equals despite the long-established protective-legislation tradition). The consequence is that the Lochner era ends in West Coast Hotel without the central precedent ever being directly interred; that work was left to *Williamson v Lee Optical* (1955), which applied rational-basis review so permissively that Lochner became dead letter without being formally overruled.

Second, the "switch in time that saved nine" historiography deserves scrutiny. The decision was argued December 16 to 17, 1936, conferenced December 19, 1936, and assigned to Hughes shortly after Roberts's vote was evident — all before Franklin Roosevelt announced the Court-packing plan on February 5, 1937. That chronology complicates the simple narrative that Roberts switched in response to political pressure. Scholars like Barry Cushman and Daniel Ho have argued that Roberts's vote was consistent with his Tipaldo vote if one notices that Tipaldo was resolved on the narrow ground that New York had not asked the Court to revisit Adkins, and Washington did. Other scholars (Burt Solomon, Jeff Shesol) read the record as consistent with genuine political responsiveness, pointing to the 1936 election results and Roosevelt's landslide as a structural signal that the Court could not hold indefinitely. The honest answer is that the "switch" was overdetermined: legal technicalities, intellectual dissatisfaction with Adkins, and political pressure all pointed the same direction.

Third, the public-subsidy rationale in Hughes's opinion is doctrinally significant beyond the minimum-wage context. Hughes argued that substandard wages shift costs onto the public through relief expenditures, converting an employer's private bargaining gain into a public fiscal burden. That move reframes the constitutional question from "is minimum-wage legislation a taking of employer liberty" to "can the legislature prevent employers from externalizing wage costs onto taxpayers." The externality framing anticipates Pigouvian economic reasoning by several decades and provides the doctrinal template for modern regulatory-takings, environmental, and labor cases in which the state acts to internalize a cost that would otherwise fall on third parties.

### Dissent (Sutherland)

Justice Sutherland, joined by the other three "Four Horsemen" (Van Devanter, McReynolds, Butler), wrote a pointed dissent resting on three pillars. First, the textual and methodological premise: the meaning of the Constitution does not shift with economic or social change. Sutherland insisted that constitutional rights are fixed by their text and history, and that to reread the Due Process Clause's "liberty" to accommodate minimum-wage legislation during the Depression would subordinate constitutional meaning to political convenience. In his most-cited line, Sutherland argued that the Constitution's provisions do not enlarge or contract in response to "the ebb and flow of economic events" — a direct rebuke to the majority's functional approach.

Second, the doctrinal premise: *Adkins* was correctly decided. Sutherland had written *Adkins* fourteen years earlier, so the dissent is also a defense of his own prior opinion. He argued that the liberty of contract is a "specified liberty" long recognized by the Court, that the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause meaningfully protects freedom of contract, and that treating adult women as incapable of bargaining for their own wages resurrects the Muller-era paternalism that Adkins had correctly rejected. Sutherland treated the economic duress rationale as a Trojan horse: if wage-and-hour regulation can rest on unequal bargaining power, there is no principled stopping point, and all economic legislation becomes constitutional by virtue of the legislature's say-so.

Third, the institutional premise: the majority was capitulating to political pressure. Sutherland did not name the Court-packing plan directly, but the timing of the decision (argued in December 1936, decided March 29, 1937, weeks after Roosevelt announced the Court-packing plan on February 5) made the implication unavoidable. He framed the shift as a breach of judicial independence, warning that if the Court revises its constitutional reading to avoid political retaliation, the Court "ceases to be a court" in any meaningful sense.

The dissent's force lies in its methodological honesty: Sutherland correctly identified that the majority was performing a revaluation, not merely applying existing doctrine. Its weakness is its political myopia — Sutherland treated the Adkins-era settlement as if it could survive another economic collapse, when the Depression had already destroyed the factual premises on which the liberty-of-contract fiction rested.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

**Hypothetical 1: Minimum wage for domestic workers above employer's claimed market rate.** A state sets a $17-per-hour floor for domestic workers in private homes. A homeowner challenges the law on the ground that the going local market rate is $14 and that the regulation takes her liberty of contract and her property without due process. The legislative record cites evidence that domestic workers disproportionately rely on food stamps and subsidized housing when paid below $17, with most such workers being women of color whose bargaining power is limited by immigration status and informal hiring.

**Why the same result.** The regulation tracks exactly the public-subsidy rationale Hughes endorsed: private wage arrangements below a living threshold externalize costs onto the public fisc. West Coast Hotel treats such externalization as a legitimate target of police-power regulation, and the inequality-of-bargaining-power framing that justified Parrish's case applies directly. The employer's market-rate objection is the modern analogue of West Coast Hotel's own argument and fails for the same reason: the floor exists precisely to override the market result.

**Hypothetical 2: Minimum wage for home health aides with Medicaid-reliance evidence.** A state sets a $18-per-hour minimum for home health aides serving Medicaid-eligible clients. Legislative findings show that 65% of aides at the $12 market rate qualify for Medicaid themselves and that raising the floor to $18 would save the state approximately $40 million annually in Medicaid outlays by reducing aide turnover and the need for higher-cost institutional care.

**Why the same result.** This hypothetical presents the public-subsidy rationale in its purest form: the state itself is the downstream payer of both aide wages and aide benefits, so the minimum wage is a direct policy lever to reduce public expenditures. Post-1937 rational-basis review — the Williamson-Lee Optical version — makes this an easy case: the legislature has articulated a plausible rational basis, economic reasoning supports it, and courts do not second-guess empirical judgments under rational basis.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

**Hypothetical 1: State law setting maximum wages (a cap) for rank-and-file public-sector workers.** A state legislature, in response to a budget crisis, caps the wages of every state employee below a certain grade at $17 per hour, regardless of the collective-bargaining agreement in place. Employees argue the statute is an arbitrary interference with their bargained-for wages and property rights.

**Why a different result.** West Coast Hotel rests on the inequality-of-bargaining-power rationale that justifies the legislature in stepping in to protect workers from exploitative wages. A maximum-wage cap inverts that rationale: it uses the police power to suppress wages rather than to elevate them, contradicting the very worker-welfare premise on which Hughes anchored the constitutional shift. Even under post-1937 rational-basis review, such a cap would be vulnerable to constitutional challenge on the ground that the regulation is not adopted in the interests of the community Hughes described (health, safety, morals, welfare) but in the fiscal interests of the state as employer. A collective-bargaining clause in state law or federal preemption under the NLRA might also defeat the statute on non-constitutional grounds.

**Hypothetical 2: Wage floor specifically for CEOs of publicly-traded companies in the state, set at $2 million.** A state legislature, reasoning that low CEO compensation signals financial distress, requires every publicly-traded company headquartered in the state to pay its CEO at least $2 million annually. A shareholder group sues, arguing the law interferes with the corporation's liberty of contract and produces absurd results.

**Why a different result.** The inequality-of-bargaining-power rationale is absent: CEOs are canonical examples of individuals with strong personal bargaining power and ample market alternatives, not the exploited class Hughes described. The public-subsidy rationale is also inverted: no public-fisc externality is at stake, and minimum CEO pay does not prevent third-party cost-shifting. The law would fail rational-basis review because the legislative classification (CEOs, not workers) has no conceivable connection to the community-welfare premise. West Coast Hotel is best read as a shield for worker-protective legislation, not as a general license for arbitrary wage regulation.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

**Hypothetical: State-mandated livability bonus pegged to local cost of living.** A state legislature requires every private employer with more than fifty employees to pay each worker an annual "livability bonus" equal to the positive difference between the state-computed cost of a local basket of essentials and the worker's prior-year gross wages, capped at $6,000. The bonus is owed regardless of hours worked, productivity, seniority, or performance. The legislative record cites evidence that uncompensated cost-of-living shocks drive working families onto emergency assistance and that localized cost-of-living indexing will reduce public relief spending. A coalition of employers challenges the law as an arbitrary redistribution of private wealth under the Fourteenth Amendment.

**Why genuinely unclear.** The hypothetical sits exactly on the West Coast Hotel / Lochner fault line. On one hand, the public-subsidy rationale Hughes embraced applies: uncompensated cost-of-living gaps predictably shift to the public fisc through food stamps, housing assistance, and emergency Medicaid, and the legislature has articulated a rational connection between the bonus and the externality. On the other hand, the bonus is untethered from work performed or any legislative classification Hughes's Court would recognize as supporting police-power regulation; it looks closer to a pure wealth transfer mandated of a private party than to a wage floor protecting against exploitation in the employment bargain itself. A modern court could uphold the law under the extremely permissive Williamson-Lee Optical version of rational basis (any conceivable rational basis suffices) or strike it under a Lochner-inflected reading that such regulation is not truly economic regulation of the employment bargain but an arbitrary transfer. The case is a genuine fence-sitter because West Coast Hotel never articulated a principled stopping point for the public-subsidy rationale, and the Court has not had occasion to test how far the rationale stretches when the regulation ceases to track the worker-exploitation framing.

## Critique

**The Lochner-survives-formally paradox.** The most striking feature of West Coast Hotel is what it does not do. Hughes targets Adkins, not Lochner, and the 1905 decision survives on the books with its liberty-of-contract reasoning formally intact. The doctrinal consequence is that West Coast Hotel does not resolve the question whether there is any constitutionally protected economic liberty at all; it merely says the liberty of contract is subject to reasonable regulation. Whether any economic regulation will still fail that reasonableness test was left to later cases, and in Williamson v Lee Optical (1955) the answer emerged: rational-basis review became so deferential that no economic regulation has been invalidated on substantive-due-process grounds in nearly a century. The Lochner-survives-formally structure means that a future Court could revive economic SDP without formally overruling anything; the sleeping precedent is still there.

**The limiting-principle critique.** Hughes's "liberty in a social organization" framing is rhetorically powerful but doctrinally underdetermined. If liberty means whatever is consistent with the community's reasonable judgments about welfare, morals, and safety, then the constitutional inquiry collapses into legislative deference — the very criticism Sutherland pressed in dissent. The majority does not articulate a principled stopping point distinguishing the minimum wage from, say, a law requiring employers to pay their workers in scrip redeemable only at company stores. The post-1937 Court eventually supplied a structural solution through Carolene Products Footnote Four, which divided the constitutional terrain between toothless rational-basis for economic regulation and heightened scrutiny for specified rights and classifications. But that solution is Stone's, not Hughes's, and West Coast Hotel on its own does not explain why its social-organization framing does not simply read the Due Process Clause out of the Constitution in economic cases.

**Progressive / feminist critique.** Kate Silbaugh and other feminist scholars have noted the irony in West Coast Hotel's sex-specific framing. Hughes anchors the minimum wage's validity on the particular bargaining weakness of women, invoking the same protective-legislation tradition (Muller v Oregon, 1908) that had long treated women as a constitutionally distinct class requiring paternalistic state oversight. The 1937 decision thus secures worker protection for women by reinscribing the premise that women are the naturally vulnerable sex, a premise that will later have to be dismantled in the 1970s sex-classification cases (Reed, Frontiero, Craig) using the same Due Process / Equal Protection architecture Hughes helped rebuild. The generalization of minimum-wage protections to all workers did not arrive constitutionally from West Coast Hotel itself; it arrived through the Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 and subsequent federal labor legislation under the Commerce Clause.

**The switch-in-time historiographic debate.** The traditional narrative (Edward Corwin, Felix Frankfurter as commentator) casts Roberts's vote as a politically motivated surrender to Roosevelt's Court-packing threat. Revisionist scholars (Barry Cushman's Rethinking the New Deal Court, Daniel Ho and Kevin Quinn's empirical analyses, Laura Kalman) have shown that Roberts conferenced his West Coast Hotel vote on December 19, 1936, weeks before Roosevelt announced the Court-packing plan on February 5, 1937. The chronology complicates the pure-politics reading but does not refute it: Roberts knew from the November 1936 election that the political environment had shifted, and Tipaldo's narrow technical distinction (New York had not asked the Court to revisit Adkins) preserved Roberts's ability to vote the other way without formally reversing himself. The honest reading is that the switch was overdetermined, not that it was either purely legal or purely political.

**Originalist / textualist Sutherland-defense angle.** Sutherland's dissent has enjoyed a minor rehabilitation among modern originalists (Richard Epstein's Takings, Randy Barnett's Restoring the Lost Constitution) who argue that the pre-1937 liberty-of-contract tradition was closer to the original public meaning of "liberty" in the Fourteenth Amendment than Hughes's social-organization framing. The argument is that Hughes's opinion is a paradigmatic example of a court revaluing constitutional meaning to accommodate political contingencies, precisely what an originalist method is supposed to foreclose. The counter-argument is Jack Balkin's: the Fourteenth Amendment's "liberty" was understood at ratification to be subject to the states' police power, and the Lochner-era narrowing of the police power to a specific set of permitted ends was itself a post-1868 doctrinal overlay. The originalist case for Sutherland depends on accepting the Lochner-era liberty-of-contract tradition as authoritative, which is contestable even within originalist scholarship.

**Exam framing.** Professor Chandler pairs West Coast Hotel with Carolene Products as the two-step that creates modern tiered scrutiny. The exam-relevant move is to recognize that West Coast Hotel does the liberty-redefinition work (substituting "liberty in a social organization" for Lochner-era absolute liberty) and Carolene Products does the scrutiny-level work (rational basis for economic regulation, heightened scrutiny for the three Footnote Four categories). Treating the two cases as a unit avoids the mistake of reading West Coast Hotel as itself answering the question of what level of scrutiny applies to economic regulation — it does not, because 1937 Hughes had no tiered-scrutiny framework yet.

## Key Points

- Ends the Lochner era (c. 1905–1937) by overruling *Adkins v Children's Hospital* (1923)
- Hughes's social-organization framing of liberty remains influential: liberty is not pre-political but embedded in a web of social relationships requiring protective law
- The "switch in time" narrative: Justice Roberts's vote change occurred during the Court-packing crisis, though scholars debate whether it represented genuine legal reconsideration or political response
- Did NOT itself overrule *Lochner v New York* directly; *Williamson v Lee Optical* (1955) and subsequent cases completed the elimination of Lochner-style economic substantive due process
