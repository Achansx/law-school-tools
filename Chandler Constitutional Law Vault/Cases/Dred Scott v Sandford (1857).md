# Dred Scott v Sandford (1857)

## Memory Jogger

> Persons of African descent, enslaved or free, are not "citizens" within the meaning of the Constitution and cannot sue in federal court; and Congress has no power under the Territory Clause or the Fifth Amendment to prohibit slavery in federal territories, rendering the Missouri Compromise unconstitutional.

---

## Facts

Dred Scott was an enslaved man held in Missouri by Dr. John Emerson, a United States Army surgeon. Emerson took Scott with him to military postings at Fort Armstrong in Illinois (a State where slavery had been abolished by the state constitution and by the Northwest Ordinance) and Fort Snelling in the federal Wisconsin Territory (where slavery had been prohibited by Section 8 of the Missouri Compromise of 1820). Scott resided in free jurisdictions for approximately four years. After Emerson's death, Scott sued in Missouri state court for his freedom, arguing that his residence in a free State and a free Territory had emancipated him. The Missouri Supreme Court ultimately rejected his claim, abandoning its own prior "once free, always free" precedents.

Scott then filed a new suit in federal circuit court in Missouri against John F. A. Sandford (a New York citizen who then held Scott by purported sale from Emerson's estate), invoking diversity jurisdiction and arguing that he was a "citizen" of Missouri for diversity purposes. The case came to the Supreme Court on writ of error.

---

## Procedural History

The federal circuit court in Missouri held it had jurisdiction, tried the case, and ruled for Sandford on the merits, finding Scott still a slave under Missouri law. Scott sought Supreme Court review. The Court initially considered deciding the case narrowly on the Strader v Graham (1851) rule that reattached status depends on the law of the State to which the enslaved person returns. Under political pressure and the influence of President-elect Buchanan, a majority agreed to produce a broader opinion reaching the citizenship, Missouri Compromise, and due-process questions.

The Court affirmed the judgment for Sandford, holding that the federal court lacked jurisdiction, and in dicta (so characterized by later critics) invalidated the Missouri Compromise.

## Holding

Persons of African descent whose ancestors were imported into the United States and sold as slaves are not "citizens" within the meaning of the Constitution and cannot sue in federal court by invoking diversity of citizenship. No State, by its own law, can confer federal citizenship on such persons because doing so would introduce a new class into the political community created by the Constitution. On the merits, Congress lacks power to prohibit slavery in federal territories: the Territory Clause of Article IV applies only to territory held by the United States at the time the Constitution was adopted, and the Fifth Amendment's protection of property in slaves forbids Congress to deprive owners of that property by crossing into federal territory. The Missouri Compromise, to the extent it prohibited slavery north of the 36°30′ line, is unconstitutional and void; neither Scott nor his family was made free by residence at Fort Snelling.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion (Taney, C.J.)

Chief Justice Taney's opinion proceeded in two principal steps. First, on citizenship, he distinguished state citizenship from the "citizenship" referenced in Article III's diversity provision, treating the latter as the political community the Constitution brought into being. Surveying colonial and founding-era statutes regulating the status of persons of African descent, he concluded that the framing generation universally understood such persons as outside the political community and unprotected by the Declaration of Independence's language about equality. Because they could not be "citizens" in the constitutional sense, the federal circuit court lacked diversity jurisdiction and the judgment had to be affirmed on that ground. Second, Taney proceeded to the merits despite the jurisdictional ruling (later critics argued this was self-contradictory dicta). He read the Territory Clause as limited to territory held in 1787 and rejected a general congressional power over post-ratification territorial acquisitions. He then invoked the Fifth Amendment: because the Constitution recognizes property in slaves and makes no distinction between that form of property and any other, Congress cannot deprive a citizen of that property merely by his crossing into a federal territory. Finally, he applied Strader v Graham to conclude that Scott's status on his return to Missouri was governed by Missouri law, which still held him enslaved.

## Key Points

- Formally superseded by the Thirteenth Amendment (1865), the Fourteenth Amendment's Citizenship Clause (1868), and the Fifteenth Amendment (1870), which together abolished slavery, constitutionalized birthright citizenship, and secured Black male suffrage.
- Widely treated as the Supreme Court's greatest self-inflicted wound and a central episode in the collapse of antebellum constitutional politics.
- An early (and infamous) example of "substantive" due process reasoning: Taney's Fifth Amendment argument treats property in slaves as a liberty interest protected against legislative deprivation, a move that later scholars have traced as a methodological precursor to Lochner-era economic substantive due process.
- The decision's narrowly legal holding on diversity jurisdiction ("Scott is not a citizen, case dismissed") and its expansive merits reasoning exist in uneasy tension; critics from Curtis to modern scholars treat the merits analysis as dicta.
- Remains central to any modern discussion of originalism, stare decisis, and the moral limits of constitutional interpretation.
