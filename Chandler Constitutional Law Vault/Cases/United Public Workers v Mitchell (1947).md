# United Public Workers v Mitchell (1947)

## Memory Jogger

> The Hatch Act case: federal employees may be restricted from political campaign activity, the Ninth and Tenth Amendments cannot override a granted power, and most plaintiffs who have not yet violated the law lack a ripe controversy for pre-enforcement challenge.

---

## Facts

The Hatch Act of 1939 prohibited federal executive-branch employees from taking "an active part in political management or in political campaigns." The United Public Workers union and several federal civil servants brought a pre-enforcement challenge, arguing the Act violated the First Amendment and exceeded congressional power by conditioning government employment on the surrender of political rights. Most plaintiffs had not violated the Act and sought only a declaratory judgment that it was unconstitutional as applied to them. One plaintiff, Poole, had actually violated the Act by serving as a ward executive committeeman and actively soliciting votes and contributions in a political campaign. Poole faced removal proceedings.

---

## Procedural History

Plaintiffs brought suit in the District Court for the District of Columbia, which upheld the Hatch Act's constitutionality. The case came to the Supreme Court on appeal. Justices Murphy and Rutledge did not participate.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority:** Reed (joined by Vinson, C.J., Frankfurter, Jackson, and Burton) (5-2)
- **Concurrence:** Frankfurter (brief, on justiciability of non-violating plaintiffs)
- **Dissent:** Douglas (joined by Black)
- **Not participating:** Murphy, Rutledge

---

## Holding

(1) The claims of plaintiffs who had not violated the Hatch Act are non-justiciable -- courts cannot adjudicate hypothetical controversies before a real enforcement action ripens the dispute into a concrete case. Poole's claim is justiciable because he has been subjected to actual removal proceedings. (2) On the merits, the Hatch Act's restrictions on federal employees' political activity are a valid exercise of congressional power over the executive branch; when a granted federal power is exercised, the Ninth and Tenth Amendments provide no independent constitutional shield.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Justice Reed addressed justiciability first. For the employees who had not violated the Act, no concrete enforcement action was pending and no concrete harm had been suffered. The Court will not render advisory opinions on abstract or hypothetical applications of a statute; the controversy must be definite, concrete, and touching on the legal relations of parties having adverse interests. Because most plaintiffs faced only a speculative future enforcement, their claims were dismissed. Poole was different -- his removal proceedings gave rise to a live controversy.

On the merits, Reed articulated a structural principle about the relationship between the Ninth and Tenth Amendments and federal power. Those amendments reserve to the states and the people powers "not delegated" to the federal government -- they are a floor, not a ceiling on individual rights, and they operate by subtracting from the federal government powers it was never granted. But when Congress is acting within a granted power, the amendments have nothing to subtract. Here, Congress plainly has power to regulate the civil service and to prescribe the conditions of federal employment in order to maintain an efficient, non-partisan executive branch. The Hatch Act's political activity restrictions are reasonably adapted to that purpose. Because the granted power exists, the Ninth and Tenth Amendment objections necessarily fail.

Reed's analysis of the First Amendment was similarly deferential: the government has an interest in maintaining a politically neutral civil service, and conditioning employment on refraining from active political campaigning is a reasonable exercise of that interest.

## Key Quotations

> "When objection is made that the exercise of a federal power infringes upon rights reserved by the Ninth and Tenth Amendments, the inquiry must be directed toward the granted power under which the action of the Union was taken. If granted power is found, necessarily the objection of invasion of those rights, reserved by the Ninth and Tenth Amendments, must fail." (Reed, J.)
