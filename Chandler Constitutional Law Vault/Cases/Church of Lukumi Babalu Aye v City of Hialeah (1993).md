# Church of Lukumi Babalu Aye v City of Hialeah (1993)

## Memory Jogger

> Laws that are not neutral and generally applicable must survive strict scrutiny — Hialeah's anti-sacrifice ordinances failed because they targeted Santeria practice by design, exempting virtually all secular animal killing while banning only religiously motivated sacrifice.

---

## Facts

The Church of Lukumi Babalu Aye practices Santeria, a religion of Cuban origin that combines West African Yoruba traditions with Roman Catholic elements. Animal sacrifice — the killing of animals in religious rituals, followed typically by the consumption of the animal — is a central practice of Santeria, used in ceremonies marking life events, healing, and spiritual devotion.

In 1987, the Church announced plans to establish a house of worship, school, and cultural center in Hialeah, Florida. The announcement prompted an immediate reaction from city officials and residents, including an emergency city council session at which council members and speakers expressed strong opposition to Santeria practices. The city subsequently enacted a series of ordinances that prohibited "unnecessary" killing of animals, defined broadly to include slaughter and sacrifice, but contained explicit exemptions for hunting, kosher slaughter, euthanasia of animals by licensed entities, and extermination of pests. The practical effect was to prohibit animal sacrifice as practiced by Santeria while leaving virtually all other forms of animal killing in the city undisturbed.

---

## Procedural History

The Church sued the City of Hialeah in federal district court, challenging the ordinances as violations of the Free Exercise Clause. The district court upheld the ordinances, finding them to advance compelling interests in protecting public health and preventing animal cruelty. The Eleventh Circuit affirmed, applying Employment Division v Smith's deferential standard for neutral, generally applicable laws. The Supreme Court granted certiorari.

## Holding

The City of Hialeah's ordinances were not neutral and of general applicability because they were designed to suppress religious practice rather than to advance legitimate secular interests across all contexts. Laws that fail the neutrality or general applicability requirements must satisfy strict scrutiny — a compelling governmental interest pursued through the least restrictive means — and Hialeah's ordinances failed that test because their underinclusiveness betrayed religious targeting.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Justice Kennedy's opinion developed the two-part framework for identifying when a law is constitutionally suspect under the Free Exercise Clause. Employment Division v Smith (1990) had held that neutral, generally applicable laws do not require religious exemptions and need only satisfy rational basis review. But Smith did not eliminate free exercise protection — it preserved strict scrutiny for laws that are either (1) not neutral (targeting religion on their face or by design) or (2) not generally applicable (burdening religious conduct while exempting comparable secular conduct).

The Hialeah ordinances failed both prongs. On neutrality: the legislative record — the emergency session, the statements of council members, the express reference to Santeria in debate — demonstrated that suppressing Santeria practice was the object of the legislation, not an incidental consequence. On general applicability: the network of exemptions (hunting, pest control, kosher slaughter, euthanasia) meant that animal killing for any secular purpose was generally permissible, while animal killing for the specific religious purpose of sacrifice was prohibited. This asymmetry — burdening religious conduct that the secular analogue would not address — is the hallmark of a non-generally-applicable law.

Once the ordinances failed the neutrality and general applicability requirements, they were subject to strict scrutiny. The city's interests in public health and preventing animal cruelty were legitimate, but the underinclusive ordinances could not plausibly be aimed at protecting public health (other animal killing was equally unsanitary) or preventing animal cruelty (hunting and pest extermination were exempted). The city's real interest was suppressing Santeria, which is not a compelling governmental interest.

### Concurrence in part and in judgment (Scalia, joined by Rehnquist)

Scalia agreed that the Hialeah ordinances failed Smith but objected to two analytical moves in Kennedy's opinion. First, he rejected Kennedy's decision to treat neutrality and general applicability as two distinct tests rather than as two sides of the same inquiry: in Scalia's view "a law that is not of general applicability (in the sense I have described) can be considered 'nonneutral'" and any law that is nonneutral in any relevant sense is not generally applicable. Collapsing the two eliminates the possibility of a law that is neutral but not generally applicable -- a category Kennedy's framework opens. Second, Scalia refused to endorse any judicial inquiry into legislative motive (the city council's subjective "object" in enacting the ordinances). He would have confined the inquiry to the text and operation of the law, partly out of skepticism about attributing a collective purpose to a multimember legislative body and partly out of concern that a motive-based inquiry would invite strict scrutiny of any law whose drafters spoke impolitely about a regulated group. The split is practically important because Scalia's text-and-operation approach would narrow Lukumi's reach: laws that target religion in operation would still fail, but laws that merely originate in religious hostility without textual or operational asymmetry would survive.

### Concurrence in part and in judgment (Souter)

Souter wrote separately to question whether Employment Division v Smith itself was correctly decided. He accepted that the Hialeah ordinances were invalid under any plausible version of free exercise doctrine but declined to join the portions of Kennedy's opinion that treated Smith as settled law. His concurrence raised the possibility that the Smith rule -- that neutral, generally applicable laws need no religious exemption -- "deserves to be reexamined" and suggested that the Sherbert compelling-interest test might better capture the Clause's protective function. In structural terms, Souter's opinion preserved his option to vote to overrule Smith in a future case (a vote he in fact cast when City of Boerne v Flores made Smith's rule unavoidable).

### Concurrence in part and in judgment (Blackmun, joined by O'Connor)

Blackmun and O'Connor joined the result but argued that the majority's framework effectively restored strict scrutiny for any law "that discriminates against religion as such" -- a category they read as broader than Kennedy's neutrality-plus-general-applicability test and congruent with the pre-Smith Sherbert compelling-interest test. Their reading would have let Sherbert do the work directly rather than through the Smith-era neutrality analysis. The concurrence signals the same Smith-skeptical pole as Souter's and foreshadows the RFRA coalition that Congress would pass into law four months after Lukumi.

What does "generally applicable" mean? Lukumi's operational answer is that a law is not generally applicable when it prohibits religiously motivated conduct while permitting comparable secular conduct -- the underinclusiveness inquiry. The formal test looks at the law's text and structure (what exemptions exist), its operation (what conduct actually goes unpunished), and its context (the legislative record). The critical question -- unresolved in 1993 and still live after [[Cases/Fulton v City of Philadelphia (2021)|Fulton]] -- is what level of secular exemption triggers the non-general-applicability conclusion. Lukumi's answer seems to be: enough exemptions that the law is "underinclusive" relative to the asserted interest. Fulton sharpens this: a single secular exemption in a scheme of discretionary individualized assessments can trigger strict scrutiny.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

**(1) City ordinance banning door-to-door solicitation on Sunday mornings, with exemptions for commercial sales.** A municipality enacts a rule barring residential canvassing between 8 and 11 a.m. on Sunday but exempts commercial delivery, political canvassing, and girl-scout cookie sales. A Jehovah's Witness congregation challenges the rule as burdening their sabbath-morning door-to-door ministry.
*Why: The network of secular exemptions renders the ordinance underinclusive relative to any neutral interest in Sunday quiet, and the burden falls precisely on a religious practice. Strict scrutiny applies, and the city's interest cannot be genuine when commercial activity of equal intrusiveness is exempted. Lukumi controls.*

**(2) State kosher-food labeling statute that gives enforcement authority to a single denomination's rabbinical council.** A state law prohibits misrepresentation of food as kosher and delegates the definition of "kosher" to a specific Orthodox rabbinical council.
*Why: Like Hialeah's ordinances, the law's text references a religious category and its operation privileges one religious tradition's understanding while burdening competing interpretations (Conservative, Reform, Sephardic) whose products are exposed to enforcement under the dominant denomination's definition. The law fails neutrality on its face and in operation.*

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

**(1) Neutral drug-enforcement statute applied to sacramental peyote use.** A state criminalizes possession of Schedule I controlled substances with no exemptions for hunters, medical researchers, or any other class; a Native American Church member prosecuted for sacramental peyote use challenges the application.
*Why: The law is neutral on its face (no reference to religion) and generally applicable in operation (no secular exemptions that would render it underinclusive). Under [[Cases/Employment Division v Smith (1990)|Employment Division v Smith]], rational basis review applies and the challenge fails. Lukumi's strict scrutiny gate does not open because the ordinance is not non-neutral or non-generally-applicable.*

**(2) Facially neutral zoning ordinance with no secular exemptions, applied to a new house of worship.** A city enacts a historic-district zoning ordinance prohibiting new construction of any building over 35 feet without any exemption for commercial, residential, or institutional use, and a mosque's proposed 45-foot minaret is denied a permit.
*Why: If the ordinance applies to all structures over 35 feet without secular exemption, it is neutral and generally applicable, and Smith rather than Lukumi controls. (A different case arises if the ordinance exempts church steeples or commercial towers -- then Lukumi and Fulton-style individualized-assessment analysis re-enters.) RLUIPA may provide statutory protection independent of the constitutional claim.*

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

**State animal-welfare statute banning the killing of any animal other than for food or licensed scientific research, applied to a Santeria sacrifice for a religious ceremony where the animal is subsequently consumed.** The statute is facially neutral (no reference to religion), has two narrow secular exemptions (food and licensed research), and the Santeria ceremony ends with consumption of the sacrificed animal.
*Why: The "consumed as food" element may bring the religious practice within a secular exemption, collapsing the underinclusiveness concern; but if the state enforces the rule against ceremonial killings where the animal is nominally consumed, Lukumi's underinclusiveness logic may still attach. The decisive question is whether the statute is genuinely aimed at non-food killing or at religious sacrifice; legislative record evidence, enforcement patterns, and the size of the secular exemption all matter. Courts have divided on analogous questions in the RFRA context.*

## Critique

**Progressive critique (Smith-skeptical).** Progressive religion scholars (Michael McConnell, Douglas Laycock) have argued that Lukumi's strict-scrutiny-on-the-margin framework is too narrow to protect minority religions from the cumulative impact of neutral legislation. The Smith rule means that a Muslim prisoner's grooming-standard claim, a Jewish police officer's yarmulke claim, or a Native American tribe's sacred-site claim typically loses unless the plaintiff can identify a secular exemption that renders the rule not generally applicable. Because background legal rules are written against majority-religion practices, "neutrality" systematically protects majority religions whose customs are already built into the legal fabric. The critique is that Lukumi polices only the most blatant forms of targeting while leaving structural disparate impact untouched.

**Originalist critique (Scalia/text-and-operation).** The opposite critique, articulated in Scalia's own concurrence, is that Kennedy's majority opinion invites judges to probe legislative motives -- a practice originalist-textualists regard as both unprincipled (who is the "collective" legislator whose intent governs?) and destabilizing (a motive finding can transform a facially neutral law into a constitutionally suspect one). Scalia would confine the inquiry to the text and operation of the law, drawing from the tradition that Arlington Heights had already made difficult in equal protection cases. The critique is that Kennedy's motive-based test is manipulable and standardless.

**Smith-Lukumi tension.** A recurring scholarly concern is that Lukumi's strict scrutiny gate has swallowed Smith's rational-basis floor. If every case that successfully reaches strict scrutiny wins, and the path to strict scrutiny requires only the identification of any secular exemption ([[Cases/Fulton v City of Philadelphia (2021)|Fulton]] takes this still further with the "mechanism for individualized exemptions" trigger), then Smith increasingly functions as a paper tiger. [[Cases/City of Boerne v Flores (1997)|Boerne]]'s invalidation of RFRA as applied to states relies on Smith's holding; if Lukumi-plus-Fulton effectively restores Sherbert-level scrutiny through the neutrality/general-applicability backdoor, Boerne's federalism reasoning is harder to defend. The unresolved question for post-Fulton free exercise doctrine is whether Smith remains meaningful law or has been functionally overruled in all but name.

**Administrability.** The general-applicability inquiry is easier to administer than the motive inquiry -- exemptions are identifiable from a statute's text, while motive must be reconstructed from a legislative record. But underinclusiveness is a matter of degree: how many secular exemptions (and how closely related to the regulated interest) does it take before a law is "not generally applicable"? Lukumi's Hialeah ordinances were easy (the hunting, kosher-slaughter, and pest-control exemptions left the prohibition functioning almost exclusively against Santeria); ordinary zoning and labor laws with incidental exemptions are much harder. The administrability problem is what Fulton's "individualized assessments" gloss purports to solve and what the most-recent generation of free exercise cases is still working through.

## Key Points

- **The Smith-Lukumi framework.** Smith (1990) held neutral, generally applicable laws need not satisfy strict scrutiny even if they burden religion. Lukumi is Smith's indispensable companion: it establishes that strict scrutiny re-enters the moment a law fails *either* neutrality or general applicability. The two cases together define the constitutional floor for free exercise, with RFRA and state analogs providing a statutory overlay.
- **Neutrality has a text prong and an object prong.** Facial neutrality requires that the law not single out religion by its terms (though terms like "sacrifice" or "ritual" can coexist with secular meaning). Object-neutrality asks whether the purpose of the law is to suppress religious practice, evaluated through text, structure, legislative history, and contemporaneous statements — the same Arlington Heights-style inquiry borrowed into the free exercise setting.
- **General applicability is about underinclusiveness.** A law burdening religious conduct while permitting comparable secular conduct is not generally applicable. The inquiry compares the burden on religion with the law's treatment of analogous secular conduct that undermines the same asserted interest. Lukumi's ordinances exempted kosher slaughter, hunting, pest control, and euthanasia — virtually all secular animal killing — while banning Santeria sacrifice, making the law "drafted with care to forbid few killings but those occasioned by religious sacrifice."
- **"Religious gerrymander" as the operative metaphor.** Kennedy's Walz-derived vocabulary frames the inquiry as one of elimination: the court must "survey meticulously the circumstances of governmental categories to eliminate . . . religious gerrymanders." The image captures both the text-plus-structure inquiry (exemptions carved around religion) and the object inquiry (drafting aimed at religion) as complementary modes of evasion.
- **Strict scrutiny means what it says.** Kennedy explicitly disavows the watered-down strict scrutiny of the 1980s: once a law fails Smith, the compelling interest must be real, and the tailoring must be genuinely narrow. Underinclusive laws cannot satisfy the test because underinclusiveness itself shows the interest is not what the government claims.
- **Legislative motive is admissible evidence.** Unlike equal protection doctrine under Washington v. Davis (where motive matters only when facial neutrality holds), free exercise analysis after Lukumi treats legislators' statements as probative of object. This is the point Scalia's concurrence contests — he would confine the inquiry to text and operation to avoid motive-based constitutional adjudication.
- **Splits inside the majority.** The "unanimous result" masks three analytically distinct positions: Kennedy's motive-permissible, two-prong framework; Scalia's text-and-operation gloss (motive irrelevant, neutrality and general applicability collapse into one test); and the Souter/Blackmun/O'Connor Smith-skeptical coalition that treats the discrimination-against-religion inquiry as a vehicle for restoring Sherbert. These cleavages resurface in Fulton (2021).
- **Exam-relevant trigger.** When a free exercise question presents a law with *any* secular exemption, the Lukumi/Fulton general-applicability prong becomes the winning theory. Look for (a) text that carves out secular conduct, (b) a system of individualized assessments, or (c) a legislative record showing anti-religion intent.
- **Doctrinal reach.** Lukumi has been extended in Fulton (2021) via the "mechanism for individualized exemptions" gloss, in Tandon v. Newsom (2021) via most-favored-nation comparison with any secular exemption, and in Kennedy v. Bremerton (2022) via merger of free exercise with free speech doctrine. The trajectory points toward Lukumi doing much of the work Sherbert once did, with Smith's rational-basis rule increasingly a paper tiger.

## Connections

- [[Cases/Employment Division v Smith (1990)]] — Smith announced the neutral-and-generally-applicable rule; Lukumi supplies its mirror image by defining when the rule does not apply. Smith and Lukumi are inseparable in the doctrinal map.
- [[Cases/Sherbert v Verner (1963)]] — the pre-Smith compelling-interest baseline. Lukumi's strict scrutiny resembles Sherbert's but is triggered only when neutrality or general applicability fails; Blackmun/O'Connor's concurrence reads Lukumi as a partial restoration of Sherbert.
- [[Cases/Fulton v City of Philadelphia (2021)]] — extends Lukumi's general-applicability prong via the "mechanism for individualized exemptions" doctrine; a single secular exemption in a discretionary scheme triggers strict scrutiny.
- [[Cases/City of Boerne v Flores (1997)]] — Smith-Lukumi is the constitutional floor that Boerne says Congress cannot raise via Section 5 against the states; RFRA survives only as to federal action after Boerne.
- [[Cases/Wisconsin v Yoder (1972)]] — pre-Smith exemption case that Scalia recast in Smith as a "hybrid rights" claim; Lukumi's strict-scrutiny threshold is a different but related route to free exercise protection.
- [[Cases/Masterpiece Cakeshop v Colorado Civil Rights Commission (2018)]] — applies Lukumi's hostility-as-nonneutrality logic to a state commissioner's anti-religion statements; remedy was vacatur rather than doctrinal extension.
- [[Cases/Tandon v Newsom (2021)]] — shadow-docket application of Lukumi's general-applicability prong to pandemic-era restrictions; most-favored-nation comparison approach.
- [[Cases/Kennedy v Bremerton School District (2022)]] — merges free exercise with free speech doctrine; relies on Lukumi's neutrality inquiry to invalidate the school district's policy.

- [[Cases/Reynolds v United States (1879)|Reynolds v United States (1879)]] — the belief-conduct foundation that Smith (and therefore Lukumi) rests on; Lukumi's strict scrutiny is the mirror image of the Reynolds-Smith baseline, triggering only when neutrality or general applicability fails.
