# United States v Rahimi (2024)

## Memory Jogger

Rahimi is the first major Supreme Court decision applying Bruen's text-history-and-tradition framework to uphold a federal firearms restriction. The Court upholds 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(8), which criminalizes firearm possession by persons subject to a qualifying domestic-violence restraining order, as applied to Zackey Rahimi. The significance lies less in the specific holding than in the clarification of how Bruen actually works. The majority announces that the Second Amendment is not trapped in amber: historical analysis requires principled analogical reasoning, not a hunt for an identical founding-era statute. The two historical analogues are surety laws and going-armed laws, which together support temporary disarmament of a person adjudicated to pose a credible threat of violence. Thomas dissents alone. He is the author of Bruen and he accuses the majority of diluting his own framework by accepting piecemeal analogies that mix and match the justification of one historical law with the burden of another.

---

## Facts

Federal law, 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(8), makes it a crime for a person to possess a firearm while subject to a domestic-violence restraining order, but only if the order satisfies three conditions. First, the order must have been issued after a hearing at which the restrained person received actual notice and an opportunity to participate. Second, the order must restrain the person from harassing, stalking, or threatening an intimate partner or the partner's child, or from engaging in conduct that would place the partner in reasonable fear of bodily injury. Third, the order must either include a specific finding that the person represents a credible threat to the physical safety of the intimate partner or child, or must explicitly prohibit the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force against the partner or child.

Zackey Rahimi was subject to a civil protective order entered by a Texas state court after he assaulted his girlfriend in a parking lot and, when a bystander witnessed the assault, fired a gun at the bystander. The restraining order included an express finding that Rahimi represented a credible threat to the physical safety of his girlfriend. While the order was in effect, Rahimi was involved in five additional shooting incidents over a two-month period.

## Procedural History

Rahimi was indicted under § 922(g)(8). The Fifth Circuit, applying Bruen, held § 922(g)(8) unconstitutional on its face because the government could not identify a sufficiently similar historical analogue. The Fifth Circuit's decision illustrated the concern that Bruen's historical test, read rigidly as demanding a founding-era twin for every modern regulation, would invalidate even intuitively sensible firearms restrictions. The Supreme Court granted certiorari to decide whether § 922(g)(8) violates the Second Amendment on its face.

## Holding

When an individual has been found by a court to pose a credible threat to the physical safety of another, that individual may be temporarily disarmed consistent with the Second Amendment. Since the founding, the Nation's firearm laws have included regulations to stop individuals who threaten physical harm to others from misusing firearms. Section 922(g)(8), as applied to a person subject to a qualifying restraining order with a specific finding of credible threat after notice and a hearing, fits comfortably within that tradition.

The holding is narrow on its own terms: it requires (1) a judicial finding, (2) of a credible threat, (3) after notice and hearing. The Court does not decide whether disarmament is permissible on lesser process or for lesser reasons.

---

## Analysis

### Majority (Opinion of the Court, Chief Justice Roberts)

**Clarifying Bruen: the Second Amendment is not trapped in amber.** Some lower courts, after Bruen, understood the historical-analogue requirement to demand a precise founding-era match for every modern regulation. The Court now clarifies that this reading is wrong. The Second Amendment permits more than just regulations identical to those existing in 1791. The appropriate analysis involves considering whether the challenged regulation is consistent with the principles that underpin the Nation's regulatory tradition. A court must ascertain whether a modern law is relevantly similar to laws that the tradition is understood to permit, applying faithfully the balance struck by the founding generation to modern circumstances.

Why and how the regulation burdens the right are central. As [[Cases/New York State Rifle and Pistol Association v Bruen (2022)|Bruen]] explained, a challenged regulation that does not precisely match its historical precursors still may be analogous enough to pass constitutional muster. A regulation need not be a dead ringer or a historical twin. The question is whether the modern law and its historical analogue are relevantly similar in the burden they impose on the right and in the justification for imposing that burden. The Court is looking for shared principles, not identical regulatory mechanics. The error of the lower courts was not that Bruen's test was unclear; they read Bruen to require a historical twin rather than a historical analogue.

**The two historical analogues.** The Court identifies two principal historical traditions supporting § 922(g)(8).

The first is the surety tradition. Throughout American history, legislatures required individuals suspected of future misbehavior to post a bond, a surety of the peace. If the individual could not post the bond, he could be jailed. If he posted the bond and violated the peace, he forfeited the money. Massachusetts enacted a particularly relevant surety statute in 1836 that targeted those who caused others to have reasonable cause to fear an injury or breach of the peace. These laws were not identical to § 922(g)(8): they imposed financial penalties rather than disarmament, and they applied to a broader range of threatening conduct. But they reflected the same principle: when an individual has been shown to pose a threat to others' safety, the government may act to prevent armed violence before it occurs.

The second is the common-law prohibition against going armed to the terror of the people. Under this long-standing rule, individuals could be punished for carrying weapons in a manner that menaced or alarmed others. The rule addressed the same fundamental concern as § 922(g)(8): preventing persons who have demonstrated a willingness to threaten violence from carrying the means of inflicting it.

Together, the surety and going-armed laws confirm what common sense suggests: when an individual poses a clear threat of physical violence to another, the threatening individual may be disarmed. Section 922(g)(8) is not identical to these founding-era regimes, but it does not need to be. Like them, § 922(g)(8)(C)(i) applies to individuals found by a court to threaten the physical safety of another. The statute is relevantly similar in both why and how: the why is preventing threatened violence, and the how is comparable, temporarily restricting the ability of a demonstrably threatening individual to possess firearms.

**Temporariness and judicial process.** The Court underscores two features of § 922(g)(8) critical to the holding. First, the restriction is temporary. It lasts only as long as the restraining order remains in effect. The person is not permanently disarmed; the firearm disability expires when the order does. Second, the restriction is tied to a judicial finding. A neutral magistrate, after providing notice and an opportunity to be heard, must determine that the restrained person poses a credible threat to the physical safety of an identified individual. This is not disarmament by accusation; it is disarmament by adjudication. These limiting features do major doctrinal work. They help explain why Rahimi does not automatically resolve every other firearm-disability case such as challenges to § 922(g)(1) (felon-in-possession) or § 922(g)(3) (drug-user prohibition), where the disarmament is permanent or not tied to individualized judicial findings.

**Rejecting the irresponsible-citizen theory.** The government also argued that Rahimi could be disarmed simply because he is not a responsible citizen. The Court rejects this contention. Heller and Bruen used the term "responsible, law-abiding citizens" to describe the class of persons who undoubtedly enjoy Second Amendment rights, but those decisions did not define "responsible" as a constitutional category and said nothing about the legal status of citizens who might not qualify as responsible. The government may not convert a descriptive phrase from Heller into a free-standing standard for stripping constitutional rights. This passage prevents the government from using "responsible citizen" as an all-purpose exception to the Second Amendment and preserves the doctrinal discipline Bruen demanded.

### Dissent (Justice Thomas, solo)

Thomas, the author of Bruen, dissents alone. His central concern is that the majority has weakened Bruen's test by accepting analogies that are far too loose. Thomas does not dispute that domestic violence is a grave problem. The question is whether the government has carried its burden of identifying a historical tradition of firearm regulation sufficiently analogous to § 922(g)(8) to sustain it under the Second Amendment. Thomas concludes it has not.

**Piecemeal analogies.** Thomas's most pointed criticism targets the majority's piecemeal approach of mixing and matching historical laws, relying on one law's burden and another law's justification to assemble an analogue no actual historical regime embodied. Surety laws imposed financial penalties, not disarmament. Going-armed laws punished public conduct, not private domestic threats. To combine the justification of one with the burden of the other is not analogical reasoning but invention.

**The slippery slope.** Thomas warns that the majority's loosened standard effectively guts Bruen. Because imprisonment (which necessarily involved disarmament) existed at the founding, the government can always satisfy a comparable-burden requirement if the standard is that some historical law imposed some restriction on some threatening person. If the how and why metrics are applied at such a high level of generality, any modern firearms restriction will find an analogue somewhere in the historical record. The test ceases to constrain. What Bruen gave with one hand, a text-history-and-tradition framework that displaced means-end balancing, the majority now takes back with the other by allowing the government to satisfy the historical-analogue requirement through loose principles rather than genuinely comparable regulations.

For Thomas, the correct answer is not that § 922(g)(8) is obviously constitutional because domestic violence is obviously terrible. The correct answer is that the government has failed to identify a founding-era tradition of disarming persons subject to civil protective orders, and therefore the statute fails Bruen's test. If the result is unpalatable, the remedy lies in constitutional amendment, not in judicial loosening of a framework adopted only two Terms ago.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side Applications (Consistent with the Holding)

**Hypothetical 1 (Same-Side).** A state statute prohibits firearm possession by a person subject to a civil harassment restraining order that includes an express finding of credible threat of physical harm, entered after notice and a hearing, for the duration of the order. This is functionally identical to § 922(g)(8) except that the underlying threat is harassment-based rather than domestic-violence-based. Under Rahimi, the surety-law and going-armed-law analogues support temporary, judicially-adjudicated disarmament of a person found to pose a credible threat of physical violence. The statute survives.

**Hypothetical 2 (Same-Side).** Congress amends § 922(g)(8) to cover persons subject to a court order issued after notice and a hearing finding them to pose a credible threat of mass violence, such as someone who has made specific threats against a workplace and been the subject of a judicial red-flag order. Rahimi's framework applies. The judicial finding, notice and hearing, credible threat, and temporary scope all track the features the Court emphasized. The surety and going-armed analogues are if anything stronger here because mass-violence threats map more directly onto the going-armed tradition.

### Opposite-Side Applications (Inconsistent with the Holding)

**Hypothetical 3 (Opposite-Side).** Congress amends the felon-in-possession statute, 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1), to apply for life to any person ever convicted of any felony, including non-violent felonies such as tax evasion and securities fraud committed decades earlier, with no mechanism for restoration of rights. Rahimi does not resolve this case, and its limiting features cut against it. The disarmament is permanent rather than temporary and is not tied to an individualized judicial finding of credible threat of physical violence. The surety-and-going-armed analogues do not map onto lifetime disarmament of non-violent offenders. A challenger would invoke Rahimi's emphasis on temporariness and threat-based adjudication; the government would need independent historical analogues for lifetime categorical disarmament of non-violent felons.

**Hypothetical 4 (Opposite-Side).** A state statute authorizes police to seize firearms from any person whom a police officer concludes, without notice to the person or a hearing, poses a danger to themselves or others. The disarmament is not by adjudication but by executive accusation. Rahimi emphasizes that the restriction must be tied to a judicial finding after notice and an opportunity to be heard. The surety tradition required a magistrate's finding after a process. The statute, lacking those features, falls outside Rahimi's safe harbor and would likely be invalidated.

### Fence-Sitter

**Hypothetical 5 (Fence-Sitter).** Congress enacts a statute barring firearm possession by persons currently under involuntary commitment for severe mental illness, with the commitment order serving as the required adjudication. Is this like Rahimi, where a judicial finding after notice and a hearing of credible threat of physical violence supports temporary disarmament, or is it more like a categorical status restriction where the commitment finding is not itself a finding of threat of physical violence to another? The answer depends on whether the commitment process includes findings analogous to the credible-threat findings in § 922(g)(8) and whether the surety-and-going-armed analogues can be stretched to cover mental-illness-based disarmament. Heller's dicta about presumptively lawful prohibitions on possession by the mentally ill provides an independent anchor, but Rahimi's methodology requires historical-analogue analysis. The outcome is genuinely uncertain and is an exam-likely fact pattern.

## Critique

Rahimi is the Court's answer to the administrability critique of Bruen. The Fifth Circuit's reading of Bruen was a stress test: a rigid historical-twin requirement would invalidate an intuitively sensible statute aimed at a class of persons adjudicated to pose a credible threat of physical violence. The Court's response is to soften the test toward shared principles rather than identical mechanics. That is a defensible move, but it is vulnerable to Thomas's dissent on its own terms. If the how and why metrics can be applied at a high level of generality, and if the burdens and justifications of different historical regimes can be mixed and matched, then the test loses much of the disciplining force Bruen claimed for it. The majority answers that this is what analogical reasoning has always meant, and that the error of the lower courts was to read Bruen as demanding identity rather than relevant similarity. The answer is not unreasonable, but it exposes the central difficulty that Breyer flagged in Bruen dissent: analogical reasoning is genuinely indeterminate at the margin.

The two doctrinal stabilizers in Rahimi, temporariness and judicial process, function as Bruen's Kavanaugh concurrence did: they reassure that the Court has not dismantled the architecture of federal firearms regulation, while preserving room for as-applied challenges. The rejection of the irresponsible-citizen theory is a clean win for doctrinal discipline: the government may not bootstrap a descriptive phrase from Heller into a categorical exception. That holding will matter in the next wave of challenges to § 922(g)(1), § 922(g)(3), and other status-based disqualifications.

The lecture and the vault's Incorporation Doctrine materials together suggest a synthesis point worth remembering for exam purposes. The Heller-McDonald-Bruen-Rahimi chain traces a complete doctrinal arc: Heller establishes the individual right, [[Cases/McDonald v City of Chicago (2010)|McDonald]] incorporates it against the States, [[Cases/New York State Rifle and Pistol Association v Bruen (2022)|Bruen]] extends it outside the home and replaces the analytical framework, and Rahimi clarifies how the new framework accommodates targeted regulation of demonstrably dangerous persons. Exam prompts are likely to test the interplay between Bruen's text-history-and-tradition test and Rahimi's clarification that analogical reasoning does not require a historical twin.

Thomas's solo dissent is consequential. As the author of Bruen, his objection carries unusual doctrinal weight. If future cases align with Thomas's more demanding reading, Rahimi will look like an outlier rather than a settlement. If future cases align with Roberts's majority, Bruen's framework will become more flexible and less predictable than Thomas intended. The tension is genuine and unresolved.

## Key Points

- Rahimi upholds § 922(g)(8) as applied to a person subject to a domestic-violence restraining order with a specific judicial finding of credible threat of physical violence after notice and a hearing.
- The key doctrinal clarification: the Second Amendment is not trapped in amber. Bruen requires a historical analogue, not a historical twin. The test is shared principles, not identical mechanics.
- Two historical analogues support the statute: surety laws (temporary restraint of persons shown to threaten the peace) and going-armed laws (prohibition on menacing armed conduct).
- Two limiting features carry most of the doctrinal work: temporariness (the disarmament lasts only as long as the order) and judicial process (notice and hearing leading to a finding of credible threat).
- The Court rejects the government's "irresponsible citizen" theory: Heller's descriptive phrase "responsible, law-abiding citizens" is not a free-standing standard for stripping Second Amendment rights.
- Thomas alone dissents. His critique is that the majority has diluted Bruen by mixing and matching the burden of one historical law with the justification of another, which is not analogical reasoning but invention.
- Rahimi does not resolve categorical status-based disqualifications such as § 922(g)(1) (felon-in-possession) or § 922(g)(3) (drug users). Those cases will be argued against the temporariness-and-judicial-process frame that Rahimi establishes.
