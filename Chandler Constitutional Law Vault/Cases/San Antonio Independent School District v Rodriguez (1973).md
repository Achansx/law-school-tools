# San Antonio Independent School District v Rodriguez (1973)

## Memory Jogger

> Education is not a fundamental right under the federal Constitution, and wealth is not a suspect classification; Texas's property-tax school financing system therefore satisfies rational basis review by rationally furthering local control of education.

---

## Facts

Texas financed public education through a combination of state funds and local property taxes, producing substantial inter-district spending disparities. In the San Antonio metropolitan area, the predominantly Mexican-American Edgewood Independent School District had the lowest property values. Despite taxing its property at a higher effective rate than the affluent Alamo Heights district, Edgewood generated only $356 per pupil in 1967–68 compared to Alamo Heights' $594. The gap was attributable to assessed property value per pupil: $5,960 in Edgewood versus $49,000 in Alamo Heights. Demetrio Rodriguez and other parents of children attending Edgewood schools filed a class action in federal district court challenging the Texas financing system under the Equal Protection Clause. They argued that the system's reliance on local property wealth discriminated against residents of property-poor districts, who were disproportionately poor and minority, in access to a fundamental right — education.

---

## Procedural History

A three-judge federal district court found the Texas financing system unconstitutional, holding that it disadvantaged a "suspect class" of poor persons residing in property-poor districts with respect to a "fundamental interest" — education. The court applied strict scrutiny and found the system failed. Texas appealed directly to the Supreme Court, which noted probable jurisdiction and reversed 5-4.

## Holding

The Texas school financing system does not violate the Equal Protection Clause. Neither of the prerequisites for strict scrutiny is present: wealth-based disparities in school district property values do not create a "suspect class" of poor residents, and education is not a "fundamental right" under the federal Constitution. Applying rational basis review, the Texas system rationally furthers the legitimate state interest of local control over public education, and the Equal Protection Clause does not require absolute equality in the provision of education across school districts.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion (Powell, J.)

Powell's analysis proceeds in two steps. First, he considers whether the plaintiffs constitute a suspect class. The Court's suspect class jurisprudence historically protects discrete and insular minorities who lack political power — characteristics associated with race, alienage, and national origin. Property-poor district residents are neither discrete nor insular; they are not a politically powerless group, and the correlation between district poverty and individual poverty is imperfect. Second, Powell assesses whether education is a fundamental right. The key to fundamentality, he explains, is not comparative societal importance but whether the right is explicitly or implicitly guaranteed by the Constitution — "Education, of course, is not among the rights afforded explicit protection under our Federal Constitution. Nor do we find any basis for saying it is implicitly so protected." The fact that education facilitates other rights (voting, speech) does not make it constitutionally fundamental; that logic would make virtually every government service essential to constitutional participation a fundamental right. Because neither trigger for heightened scrutiny is satisfied, rational basis applies. Texas's system rationally promotes local control — a legitimate state interest — by allowing districts to supplement state funds with locally generated revenue. Whether the tradeoff is optimal is a legislative question.

## Key Points

- *Rodriguez* closes the door on federal constitutional challenges to inter-district educational spending disparities; state courts applying state constitutions have been the primary venue for such litigation since (see *Serrano v. Priest* (Cal. 1971) and subsequent state school finance cases).
- The case defines "fundamentality" for equal protection purposes by reference to constitutional text and structure, not the social importance of the interest — a limiting principle that constrains strict scrutiny expansion.
- Marshall's dissent introduced the "sliding scale" / "substantial-interest" approach to equal protection, which the Court never formally adopted but which appears implicitly in intermediate scrutiny cases.
- *Plyler v. Doe* (1982) later holds that a Texas statute denying free public education to undocumented children violates Equal Protection, but does so without calling education a fundamental right, relying instead on the especially vulnerable nature of the affected class and the severity of the deprivation.
