# Whole Womans Health v Jackson (2021)

## Memory Jogger

> Texas Senate Bill 8's novel scheme of deputizing private citizens to enforce a six-week abortion ban largely evaded pre-enforcement federal court review: only suits against state licensing officials could proceed, while judges, clerks, the attorney general, and private plaintiffs were immune or unsuitable defendants.

---

## Facts

Texas Senate Bill 8 prohibited abortions after detection of fetal cardiac activity, which typically occurs around six weeks of pregnancy. Rather than authorize state officials to enforce the prohibition, the statute disclaimed public enforcement and instead created a private cause of action allowing any person to sue anyone who performed, aided, or abetted such an abortion for statutory damages of at least ten thousand dollars per violation plus attorneys' fees. The drafters designed the scheme to evade pre-enforcement federal constitutional review by ensuring no identifiable state officer enforced the law, thereby stripping Ex parte Young of an executive-branch target.

Whole Womans Health and other abortion providers sued state court judges, state court clerks, the Texas Attorney General, state licensing officials with professional-disciplinary authority over physicians and nurses, and a private individual who had expressed intent to sue. They sought to enjoin enforcement before the statute took effect.

---

## Procedural History

The district court denied a preliminary injunction in part. Plaintiffs sought emergency relief in the Fifth Circuit and the Supreme Court. The Supreme Court initially declined to stay enforcement pending appeal. The Court then granted certiorari before judgment, consolidating the petition with the federal government's parallel suit, and heard argument on an expedited basis regarding whether the various defendants could be sued.

## Holding

Suit against state licensing officials (members of the Texas Medical Board and analogous bodies with authority to take disciplinary action against physicians and nurses) may proceed under Ex parte Young because those officials retained some enforcement-adjacent power over abortion providers. Suit against state court judges and clerks is barred by sovereign immunity and traditional limits on equitable relief against judicial officers. Suit against the Texas Attorney General fails because he lacks enforcement authority over SB 8's private cause of action. Suit against a private individual defendant fails because he is not a state actor, and sovereign immunity plus traditional equitable rules preclude the claim against him as pleaded.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Justice Gorsuch's majority opinion applies Ex parte Young's requirement that a sued state officer have "some connection with the enforcement" of the challenged law. Licensing officials qualify because SB 8 did not fully strip them of disciplinary authority over medical professionals who violate state law, and the Texas Medical Practice Act references compliance with state law as a disciplinary predicate. Judges and clerks do not qualify because federal courts do not ordinarily enjoin state judicial officers from adjudicating cases: they are neutral arbiters, not adverse enforcers, and enjoining them would transform the federal court into an appellate reviewer of potential state proceedings. The Attorney General has no enforcement role under SB 8's text. The private individual defendant is not a state actor and sovereign immunity plus prudential equitable rules foreclose relief. The opinion carefully preserves licensing-officer suits as the narrow avenue for pre-enforcement challenge, declining to treat SB 8's design as altogether immune from federal review but also declining to expand Ex parte Young to reach unconventional enforcement schemes.

## Key Points

- The principal justiciability lesson of SB 8: private-enforcement statutes can largely evade pre-enforcement federal review under Ex parte Young.
- Licensing officials remain a viable defendant when they retain residual disciplinary power tied to compliance with state law.
- Judicial officers are not proper Ex parte Young defendants because they adjudicate rather than enforce.
