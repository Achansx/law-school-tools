# Gibbons v Ogden (1824)

## Memory Jogger

> Commerce means intercourse, and the federal coasting license for a steamboat crossing between New Jersey and New York preempted a conflicting state-granted monopoly, establishing the plenary sweep of the Commerce Clause over interstate navigation.

---

## Facts

In 1798 the New York legislature granted Robert R. Livingston and Robert Fulton an exclusive right to navigate the waters of the State by steam. Aaron Ogden held a licensed assignment from that monopoly to operate steamboats between New York City and the New Jersey shore. Thomas Gibbons ran a competing steamboat line on the same route under a federal coasting license issued pursuant to the Coasting Act of 1793, which permitted licensed vessels to engage in the coasting trade of the United States.

Ogden sued Gibbons in the New York Court of Chancery seeking an injunction against Gibbons's operation of steamboats in New York waters. The question was whether the state-granted monopoly could be enforced against a vessel navigating between two states under a federal license. The dispute arrived at the Supreme Court with the Court confronting its first major Commerce Clause case and with significant political attention because state-granted navigation monopolies were common along the Atlantic seaboard.

---

## Procedural History

The New York Court of Chancery granted the injunction to Ogden, enforcing the state monopoly. The New York Court of Errors affirmed. Gibbons appealed to the Supreme Court of the United States on a writ of error.

The Supreme Court unanimously reversed. Chief Justice Marshall wrote for the Court. Justice Johnson concurred separately, arguing that the commerce power is exclusive to Congress even without a conflicting federal statute.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority:** Marshall, C.J. (joined by Washington, Duvall, Story, Todd) — 6-0
- **Concurrence:** Johnson
- **Dissent:** None

---

## Holding

Commerce is not confined to the buying and selling of goods but describes commercial intercourse in all its branches, including navigation. The congressional power to regulate commerce among the several states is plenary, reaches activity that crosses state lines or is introduced into the interior, and acknowledges no limitations other than those prescribed in the Constitution. When a federal statute enacted under that power conflicts with a state law, the Supremacy Clause compels the state law to yield. The New York monopoly, which forbade a federally licensed steamboat from operating on New York waters, was accordingly void as applied to interstate navigation.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Chief Justice Marshall rejected the narrow definition of commerce urged by Ogden's counsel and held that commerce encompasses intercourse in all its branches, expressly including navigation. The commerce power is complete in itself, may be exercised to its utmost extent, and is plenary as to the objects it reaches. The phrase "among the several States" does not stop at state boundaries but reaches into the interior wherever the activity concerns more than one state. Marshall declined to decide whether the commerce power is exclusive of state legislation in the absence of congressional action, because an actual collision existed here: the federal Coasting Act licensed Gibbons's vessels to navigate the waters of the United States, and the New York monopoly barred precisely that navigation. The Supremacy Clause resolved the collision in favor of the federal license. Marshall's broad definition laid the doctrinal foundation for federal regulation of interstate transportation and for the later New Deal Commerce Clause expansion, while his refusal to decide exclusivity preserved space for the later dormant Commerce Clause jurisprudence.

### Concurrence (Johnson)

Justice Johnson's concurrence would have resolved the case on a broader ground that Marshall carefully avoided: the commerce power is exclusive to Congress even without a conflicting federal statute. Johnson reasoned that "the power to prescribe the limits to [commerce's] freedom, necessarily implies the power to determine what shall remain unrestrained," so "the power must be exclusive; it can reside but in one potentate; and hence, the grant of this power carries with it the whole subject, leaving nothing for the State to act upon" (lines 400). For Johnson, foreign commerce made the exclusivity case obvious: "The States are unknown to foreign nations; their sovereignty exists only with relation to each other and the general government," so foreign commerce regulation had to be a federal monopoly or the United States would be held internationally responsible for state-imposed burdens it did not choose (lines 410-412). Extending the reasoning to interstate commerce, Johnson treated state power over commerce as "nothing more than a power to limit and restrain it at pleasure" (line 397). The concurrence is the doctrinal forebear of the dormant Commerce Clause: it accepts the premise that the grant of commerce power to Congress itself displaces state authority, a position Marshall left open and that the Court took up piecemeal beginning with Cooley v Board of Wardens (1852).

### Dissent

No dissent was filed, and the absence is analytically meaningful. Marshall's Court still operated under the opinion-of-the-Court practice he had established after Chase's seriatim era, which pushed dissenting justices to acquiesce absent a deep conviction. Two structural features of the dispute also drained the case of potential dissenters. First, the political pressure cut against the state monopoly: navigation monopolies were unpopular along the Atlantic seaboard, and Congress had already legislated a licensing scheme that Gibbons had complied with. Second, Marshall's narrow resolution (conflict with a federal statute rather than exclusivity of the commerce power) left almost nothing for a states'-rights-oriented justice to oppose. Any justice who wanted to preserve state police-power autonomy had nothing to fight over; the opinion expressly acknowledged concurrent state authority over inspection, quarantine, and health regulations (lines 182-186). The only justice who wanted a broader rule, Johnson, wrote separately to add that rule rather than to resist the judgment.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

1. **Interstate airline route preemption.** A state statute bars a federally certificated regional airline from operating certain passenger routes within the state in order to protect a state-chartered competitor. The Federal Aviation Act authorizes the FAA to license interstate air carriers, and the state rule directly forbids the use of that federal license. Under Marshall's reasoning, the federal license authorizes the activity and the state rule forbids it, so the Supremacy Clause resolves the collision in favor of the federal statute. The plenary-power framing from the "complete in itself" passage (lines 118) underwrites the modern doctrine that federal aviation licensing preempts conflicting state economic regulation.

2. **Interstate pipeline certification.** FERC issues a Natural Gas Act certificate to build an interstate pipeline across two states; the downstream state then passes a statute forbidding any pipeline crossing its territory without a state permit that the operator cannot obtain because the state has decided it disfavors the project. As in Gibbons, a federal licensing statute authorizes the very activity the state rule bars. The federal certificate collides with the state siting veto, and Marshall's Supremacy Clause passage (lines 246-247) controls: "the law of the State, though enacted in the exercise of powers not controverted, must yield to it." The outcome tracks Gibbons directly because the state regulation targets the interstate commercial actor's right to operate under a specific federal license.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

3. **Purely intrastate ferry.** A small operator runs a ferry across a landlocked lake that lies entirely within one state, serves only in-state passengers, and never connects to any interstate route. The state forbids competing operators; the operator invokes Gibbons. Under Marshall's own definition, this is "that commerce which is completely internal, which is carried on between man and man in a State, or between different parts of the same State, and which does not extend to or affect other States" (lines 92-93). Marshall expressly excluded such commerce from the reach of the Commerce Clause. The state regulation stands because no federal regulatory interest is triggered.

4. **Evenhanded state inspection regime.** A state enacts inspection and quality-standards legislation for goods sold within its borders; the statute applies to interstate and intrastate goods equally and there is no conflicting federal law. An out-of-state producer argues Gibbons requires invalidation. The challenge fails. Marshall himself identified inspection laws as a component of "that immense mass of legislation, which embraces every thing within the territory of a State, not surrendered to the general government" (lines 182-186). Gibbons turned on an actual collision with a federal statute; without such a collision, states retain their traditional police-power authority to regulate commerce that passes through their territory.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

5. **State-licensed non-commercial activity with diffuse aggregate effects.** A state licenses and regulates purely intrastate firearm-possession near a school (the Lopez-era scenario) or a purely intrastate non-economic act (the Morrison-era scenario). Congress passes an overlapping federal statute. Is the state regulation void under Gibbons because the federal law covers the same subject, or is the federal law itself void under Lopez and Morrison because the underlying activity is not "commerce among the States"? Marshall's "cannot stop at the external boundary line" passage (lines 92-93) supports federal reach, but his exclusion of "completely internal" activity supports state regulation. The answer depends on whether the Court follows the Marshall-Wickard framing (substantial aggregate effect suffices) or the Rehnquist-era framing (non-economic intrastate activity is a state matter). This is the doctrinal seam that Gibbons left open and that modern Commerce Clause doctrine has been fighting over since 1995.

---

## Critique

**Progressive critique.** Marshall's plenary reading of the commerce power is the doctrinal foundation for the modern federal regulatory state. Without the "complete in itself, may be exercised to its utmost extent" formulation (lines 118), the New Deal Court could not have sustained Wickard v Filburn's aggregation theory, and without Marshall's refusal to limit commerce to interstate trade in the narrow sense, Heart of Atlanta Motel and Katzenbach v McClung would have had no foothold. Progressive commentators treat Gibbons as foundational precisely because it read commerce structurally, focused on national needs that escape any single state, and rejected the kind of formalistic boundary that Lopez and Morrison would later revive. The critique from this side is that the Rehnquist Court's narrowing (non-economic intrastate activity falling outside the commerce power) departs from Marshall's understanding of the commerce power as a functional response to national commercial integration rather than a categorical catalog.

**Originalist critique.** Randy Barnett's work argues that Marshall's equation of "commerce" with "intercourse" may exceed the eighteenth-century meaning of commerce, which was more commonly limited to "trade" in the sense of buying, selling, and exchanging, with "intercourse" serving as a metaphor rather than a definition. On this reading, Marshall's expansive framing was a nineteenth-century extension rather than a retrieval of original meaning, and it made the Commerce Clause available for twentieth-century uses (regulating manufacturing, agriculture, labor conditions) that no Framer would have accepted. The counterargument, developed by Jack Balkin and others, observes that "commerce" in the 1780s commonly included navigation and transportation, and that Marshall's definition is supported by period dictionaries, Hamilton's Federalist essays, and the commercial practice the Founders were trying to address. The dispute turns on the weight given to narrow lexicographic evidence versus the broader functional and constitutional-convention record.

**Doctrinal observation.** Marshall's deliberate refusal to decide exclusivity produced a century of dormant-Commerce-Clause doctrine instead of a single clean rule. That refusal was prudential (he did not need to decide the question) and strategic (Johnson's concurrence showed the Court was split on the issue), but it left a structural ambiguity that modern commerce-clause litigation still negotiates. National Pork Producers Council v Ross (2023) is the most recent iteration of the question Marshall ducked.

## Key Points

- Foundational Commerce Clause opinion; defines commerce broadly and establishes federal supremacy over interstate navigation.
- Vote: 6-0 (Marshall), with Johnson concurring on exclusivity.
- Marshall's refusal to decide whether the commerce power is exclusive preserves the dormant Commerce Clause question for later.
- Cited repeatedly in New Deal expansion (Wickard) and in modern contraction (Lopez, Morrison) as the doctrinal anchor.
