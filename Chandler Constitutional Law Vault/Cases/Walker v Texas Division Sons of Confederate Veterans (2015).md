# Walker v Texas Division Sons of Confederate Veterans (2015)

## Memory Jogger

> Specialty license plates issued pursuant to Texas's statutory scheme constitute government speech; because the government is the speaker, it may reject proposed plate designs without implicating the First Amendment.

---

## Facts

In 2009, the Texas Division of the Sons of Confederate Veterans (SCV), an organization of descendants of Confederate soldiers, applied for a specialty license plate featuring the Confederate battle flag — a controversial symbol — surrounded by the words "Sons of Confederate Veterans 1896" and a gold border. Under Texas's specialty license plate program, private organizations propose plate designs that must be approved by the Texas Department of Motor Vehicles Board, which has final authority over all designs. The Board initially deadlocked in April 2011, then in November 2011 unanimously rejected the SCV design after receiving extensive public opposition. The Board found it "necessary to deny th[e] plate design application… because public comments ha[d] shown that many members of the general public find the design offensive." Texas offered approximately 350 other specialty plate designs, including plates featuring the Confederate battle flag through the Sons of Confederate Veterans national organization in some other states. The SCV sued, arguing the rejection constituted unconstitutional viewpoint discrimination under the Free Speech Clause.

---

## Procedural History

The district court ruled in Texas's favor. The Fifth Circuit reversed, treating license plate designs as private speech in a limited public forum and holding that Texas had engaged in unconstitutional viewpoint discrimination by rejecting a design based on its message. The Supreme Court granted certiorari to resolve whether specialty license plate programs constitute government speech or private speech in a government forum.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority (5-4):** Breyer, J. (Thomas, Ginsburg, Sotomayor, Kagan, JJ., joined)
- **Dissent:** Alito, J. (Roberts, C.J., Scalia, Kennedy, JJ., joined)

---

## Holding

Texas's specialty license plate designs constitute government speech. The State of Texas owns the designs, requires their display on government-issued vehicle identification, retains final approval authority over all designs, and has a long history of communicating messages through its plates. Because the plates convey government speech, the First Amendment's prohibition on viewpoint discrimination does not apply; Texas was entitled to reject the SCV design and did not violate the Free Speech Clause.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion (Breyer, J.)

The majority applies the three-factor government speech analysis derived from *Pleasant Grove City v. Summum* (2009): (1) history of the government using the medium to communicate its own messages; (2) whether the government is closely identified in the public mind as the communicator of messages on the medium; and (3) the degree to which the government has actively shaped or controlled the messages on the medium. All three factors support treating Texas license plates as government speech. First, license plates have historically been used by states to communicate messages — from state slogans to specialty messages. Second, because plates are state-issued, state-mandated identification documents, viewers are likely to attribute their messages to the state rather than the vehicle owner. Third, Texas maintains final editorial control: the Board may reject any design it finds inappropriate. The fact that private organizations propose designs does not transform the plates into a forum for private speech; under *Summum*, the government may accept contributions from private sources without ceding control of the resulting speech. Because the plates are government speech, forum analysis is inapplicable and the ordinary Free Speech viewpoint-discrimination rules do not constrain Texas's choices.

## Key Points

- *Walker* marks the expansion of the government speech doctrine to a specialty plate program with hundreds of private-proposed designs — a more complex context than the donated monuments in *Summum*.
- The case is significant for its unusual coalition: Breyer, Thomas, Ginsburg, Sotomayor, and Kagan in the majority; Alito, Roberts, Scalia, and Kennedy in dissent.
- The government speech doctrine as expanded in *Walker* creates a powerful escape valve: once speech is labeled "government speech," virtually all First Amendment scrutiny drops away.
- The Court later acknowledges in *Matal v. Tam* (2017) and *Iancu v. Brunetti* (2019) that not every government subsidy or program qualifies as government speech, limiting *Walker*'s reach in the trademark context.
- The case pairs with *Legal Services Corp v. Velazquez* (2001), where the same government-speech rationale fails because the government subsidy was distorting an otherwise independent medium (the legal system).
