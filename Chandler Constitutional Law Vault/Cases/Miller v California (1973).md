# Miller v California (1973)

## Memory Jogger

> The Court established the three-part Miller test for obscenity, replacing the "utterly without redeeming social value" standard and holding that juries apply contemporary community standards rather than a hypothetical national standard.

---

## Facts

Marvin Miller operated a mass-mailing campaign from California, distributing unsolicited brochures advertising sexually explicit books and a film. When a Newport Beach restaurant manager and his mother opened one of the envelopes, they immediately complained to the Orange County Police Department. The brochures contained explicit pictures depicting sexual acts and advertised books with titles such as *Intercourse*, *Man-Woman*, and *Sex Orgies Illustrated*. Miller was charged under California Penal Code § 311.2(a), which prohibited the knowing distribution of obscene material. The trial court instructed the jury to apply "contemporary community standards" in assessing obscenity.

---

## Procedural History

The trial court convicted Miller. The California Court of Appeal affirmed. Miller sought review, arguing that the jury instruction failed to specify a national obscenity standard and that the conviction therefore violated the First Amendment. The Supreme Court granted certiorari to reconsider the constitutional standards governing obscenity prosecutions, which it described as having produced "no majority agreement on a definitive standard" in the years following *Roth v. United States* (1957) and *Memoirs v. Massachusetts* (1966).

## Holding

Obscene material is not protected by the First Amendment, and a state may prohibit its distribution. The constitutionally appropriate standard for identifying obscene material requires the trier of fact to determine: (a) whether "the average person, applying contemporary community standards" would find that the work, taken as a whole, appeals to the prurient interest; (b) whether the work depicts or describes, in a patently offensive way, sexual conduct specifically defined by the applicable state law; and (c) whether the work, taken as a whole, lacks serious literary, artistic, political, or scientific value (SLAPS). The First Amendment does not require juries to apply a hypothetical national standard rather than contemporary community standards.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion (Burger, C.J.)

Burger writes to displace the *Memoirs* "utterly without redeeming social value" standard, which he criticized as unworkable because it permitted almost any material to escape obscenity regulation by injecting the slightest social value. The majority retains the core framework of *Roth* — that obscenity is categorically outside First Amendment protection — but reformulates the test in three respects. First, community standards are local and state-based, not national; requiring jurors to hypothesize a "national standard" produces a "fictive and unascertainable" determination. Second, the state law must specifically define the sexual conduct to be regulated, giving fair notice and preventing censorship of legitimate expression. Third, the third prong asks whether the work lacks serious SLAPS value — a more demanding standard for the government than the *Memoirs* "redeeming social value" test but still requiring courts to consider the work as a whole. The majority insists it is not defining obscenity beyond narrowly drawn categorical regulation of hard-core pornography.

## Key Points

- The three-part Miller test replaced the *Memoirs* "utterly without redeeming social value" standard and remains the governing federal obscenity test today.
- The SLAPS prong (serious literary, artistic, political, or scientific value) is judged by a national "reasonable person" standard rather than local community standards — the Court later clarified this in *Pope v. Illinois* (1987).
- The first two prongs (prurient interest and patent offensiveness) are judged by contemporary community standards — local juries apply their own community's norms, not a national average.
- *Miller* was decided on the same day as *Paris Adult Theatre I v. Slaton* (1973), which upheld Georgia's power to close an adult theater showing hard-core films to consenting adults.
- *Ashcroft v. Free Speech Coalition* (2002) uses the Miller test as one of two independent grounds for striking the CPPA's "appears to be" provision.
