# Rochin v California (1952)

## Memory Jogger

> Police-directed forcible stomach pumping to retrieve swallowed capsules "shocks the conscience" and violates the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment; convictions cannot be brought about by methods that offend a sense of justice.

---

## Facts

Acting on a tip that Antonio Rochin was selling narcotics, three Los Angeles County deputy sheriffs forced open the door of his room and found him partially dressed on his bed with his wife. When officers spotted two capsules on a nightstand and asked "Whose stuff is this?", Rochin seized the capsules and put them in his mouth. The officers jumped on him and attempted to extract the capsules by force but were unsuccessful. Rochin was handcuffed and taken to a hospital. At the officers' direction, a doctor forced an emetic solution through a tube into Rochin's stomach against his will. The resulting vomiting produced the two capsules, which contained morphine. The capsules were the chief evidence at trial. The California District Court of Appeal affirmed the conviction, characterizing the case as "chock-full of illegality," yet holding the illegally obtained evidence admissible under state authority. Rochin was sentenced to sixty days' imprisonment.

---

## Procedural History

California's courts declined to suppress the stomach-pump evidence, reasoning that while the officers' conduct may have been improper, California law at the time did not require exclusion. The Fourth Amendment exclusionary rule had not yet been incorporated against the states (that would not occur until *Mapp v. Ohio* (1961)). Rochin sought certiorari, framing the issue as whether his conviction rested on a method that violated the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. The Supreme Court reversed.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority:** Frankfurter, J. (for the Court — all Justices agreed on reversing the conviction, though by different reasoning)
- **Concurrence:** Black, J. (agreed on reversal but on Fifth Amendment self-incrimination grounds incorporated against states)
- **Concurrence:** Douglas, J. (agreed on reversal on Fifth Amendment grounds)

---

## Holding

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment prohibits convictions obtained by methods that "shock the conscience." Police conduct that is so brutal and offensive to a sense of justice that it cannot be "squared with fundamental standards of decency" violates due process regardless of whether the evidence produced is relevant and credible. The forcible extraction of stomach contents via a stomach pump was bound to offend even hardened sensibilities; the resulting evidence must be excluded and the conviction reversed.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion (Frankfurter, J.)

Frankfurter's opinion employs a deliberately open-ended due process standard, rejecting Black's proposal for a more rule-based approach tied to specific Bill of Rights provisions. Due process, Frankfurter writes, is "a summarized constitutional guarantee of respect for those personal immunities which are so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental, or are implicit in the concept of ordered liberty." The standard cannot be confined to specific enumerated rights; it requires "an evaluation based on a disinterested inquiry pursued in the spirit of science, on a balanced order of facts exactly and fairly stated." When government agents illegally break into a home, struggle to open a suspect's mouth, and then forcibly pump his stomach — this course of conduct "is bound to offend even hardened sensibilities." The analogy to coerced confessions supports the conclusion: just as confessions obtained through physical coercion are inadmissible because they offend the community's sense of fair play, so too is evidence extracted from a person's body by force. The majority deliberately declines to tie the holding to the Fifth Amendment self-incrimination clause, which it treats as not yet incorporated, leaving the doctrinal basis in the broad guarantee of "fundamental fairness."

## Key Points

- *Rochin* is a landmark in the debate between substantive due process and incorporation doctrine; Black and Douglas's concurrences lay the groundwork for total-incorporation arguments against Frankfurter's fundamental-fairness approach.
- The "shocks the conscience" standard survives as the test for substantive due process claims involving executive action (as opposed to legislation), confirmed in *County of Sacramento v. Lewis* (1998).
- The case foreshadows *Mapp v. Ohio* (1961) which finally incorporated the Fourth Amendment exclusionary rule; had *Rochin* come later, the analysis might have rested on *Mapp*.
- Contrast *Schmerber v. California* (1966), where the Court upheld a blood draw over a DUI suspect's objection as NOT shocking the conscience because blood draws are routine medical procedures performed by trained professionals.
