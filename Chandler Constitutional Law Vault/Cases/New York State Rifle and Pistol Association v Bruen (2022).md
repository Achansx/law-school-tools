# New York State Rifle and Pistol Association v Bruen (2022)

## Memory Jogger

Bruen is not merely a gun case. It is a major opinion about constitutional method. The Court strikes down New York's proper-cause requirement for concealed carry licenses and, in doing so, announces a new framework for all Second Amendment challenges: if the text covers the conduct, the Constitution presumptively protects it, and the government must then justify the regulation by showing that it is consistent with the Nation's historical tradition of firearm regulation. Means-end scrutiny, strict or intermediate, is out. Text-history-and-tradition is in. Thomas writes for six Justices. Kavanaugh (joined by Roberts) files a concurrence that functions as a doctrinal stabilizer by emphasizing that shall-issue licensing regimes remain constitutional. Breyer dissents, joined by Sotomayor and Kagan, arguing that the history-only test is indeterminate, ignores modern public-safety realities, and represents a radical methodological claim whose implications extend far beyond firearms.

---

## Facts

New York made it a crime to possess a firearm without a license, whether inside or outside the home. To obtain a license to carry a handgun in public, an applicant had to prove proper cause, a special need for self-defense distinguishable from that of the general community. In practice, proper cause required a particularized and non-speculative threat. The licensing officer had broad discretion to grant or deny an unrestricted carry license.

Brandon Koch and Robert Nash applied for unrestricted carry licenses. Both were denied. Koch had cited evidence of robberies in his neighborhood. Nash received a restricted license valid only for hunting and target practice but was denied unrestricted carry despite citing recent robberies in his area. The New York State Rifle and Pistol Association joined the suit. The challenged regime was discretionary: the case was framed as a challenge to a may-issue licensing regime, not to all regulation of public carry.

## Procedural History

The district court dismissed the complaint. The Second Circuit affirmed, applying the post-Heller two-step framework that had coalesced in the courts of appeals: step one asked whether the regulated conduct fell within the Second Amendment's scope as informed by text and history, and step two applied means-end scrutiny, typically intermediate scrutiny, to determine whether the regulation was sufficiently tailored to an important governmental interest. The Supreme Court granted certiorari to resolve the constitutionality of New York's proper-cause requirement and, as it turned out, to overhaul the governing framework.

## Holding

The Second and Fourteenth Amendments protect an individual's right to carry a handgun for self-defense outside the home. New York's proper-cause requirement violates the Fourteenth Amendment by preventing ordinary, law-abiding citizens with ordinary self-defense needs from exercising that right.

The governing framework: when the Second Amendment's plain text covers an individual's conduct, the Constitution presumptively protects that conduct, and the government must then demonstrate that the regulation is consistent with this Nation's historical tradition of firearm regulation. Means-end scrutiny has no place in Second Amendment analysis.

---

## Analysis

### Majority (Opinion of the Court, Justice Thomas)

**Scope: the right extends outside the home.** In Heller the Court recognized that the Second and Fourteenth Amendments protect the right of an ordinary, law-abiding citizen to possess a handgun in the home for self-defense. In [[Cases/McDonald v City of Chicago (2010)|McDonald]], the Court incorporated that right against the States. But nothing in the Second Amendment's text draws a home-versus-public distinction with respect to the right to keep and bear arms. The definition of "bear" naturally encompasses public carry. The Second Amendment guarantees an individual right to possess and carry weapons in case of confrontation, and confrontation can surely take place outside the home. Bruen is thus both a scope-of-right case and a standard-of-review case.

**Rejecting the two-step framework: "one step too many."** Since Heller and McDonald, the Courts of Appeals had coalesced around a two-step framework. Step one asked whether the regulated conduct fell within the scope of the Second Amendment as informed by text and history. Step two applied means-end scrutiny. The Court rejects this approach as having one step too many. Step one is broadly consistent with Heller, which demands a test rooted in the Second Amendment's text as informed by history. But Heller and McDonald do not support a second step applying means-end scrutiny in the Second Amendment context. Heller's methodology centered on constitutional text and history, did not invoke any means-end test, and expressly rejected Justice Breyer's proposed interest-balancing approach.

**The governing test.** The Court's framework is straightforward in statement, if not in application. Step 1 is the challenger's burden: when the Second Amendment's plain text covers an individual's conduct, the Constitution presumptively protects that conduct. Step 2 is the government's burden: the government must then demonstrate that the regulation is consistent with this Nation's historical tradition of firearm regulation. Only if the government carries that burden may a court conclude that the individual's conduct falls outside the Second Amendment's unqualified command. This burden allocation matters enormously. Under the old intermediate scrutiny, the government needed only to show a substantial relationship to an important interest. Now it must produce historical evidence.

**Analogical reasoning, not a historical twin.** The Court acknowledges that its test requires courts to engage in analogical reasoning. The Second Amendment is not a regulatory straightjacket. The government need not identify a historical twin, a regulation that is a dead ringer for the modern law. Rather, the government must identify a relevantly similar historical analogue. Courts should use at least two metrics: how and why the regulations burden the right to armed self-defense. The how question asks whether the modern regulation imposes a comparable burden on the right. The why question asks whether the modern regulation is comparably justified. A regulation need not perfectly match its historical predecessor but must be relevantly similar on both dimensions.

When a challenged regulation addresses a general societal problem that has persisted since the eighteenth century, the historical inquiry will be relatively simple: the lack of a distinctly similar historical regulation is relevant evidence that the challenged regulation is inconsistent with the Second Amendment. When a regulation addresses unprecedented societal concerns or dramatic technological changes, a more nuanced approach may be required.

**The historical survey.** The Court surveys English antecedents (the Statute of Northampton and Sir John Knight's Case), antebellum American law (common-law prohibitions on going armed to the terror of the people, surety statutes, and bans on concealed but not open carry), Reconstruction-era evidence (right to arms as central to the Fourteenth Amendment's protection of freedmen), and late-nineteenth-century Western territorial outliers (which the Court treats as too isolated, too geographically concentrated, and too temporally remote to establish a nationwide tradition). The upshot is that none of these historical limitations amounted to anything approaching New York's proper-cause requirement, which conditions the right to carry at all on a showing of special need.

Evidence from the Founding era (1791) and the Reconstruction era (1868) receives the most weight, because those are the two moments at which the relevant constitutional text was adopted. Evidence from later periods receives progressively less weight. The absence of regulation is itself evidence of tradition, a tradition of permitting the conduct at issue.

**Sensitive places.** The Court reaffirms that the Second Amendment permits prohibitions on firearms in sensitive places such as legislative assemblies, polling places, and courthouses, where governments have historically prohibited arms. But the Court warns against expanding that category so broadly that all places of public congregation become sensitive places. Such expansion would in effect exempt cities from the Second Amendment and would eviscerate the general right to publicly carry arms for self-defense.

**Shall-issue versus may-issue.** The Court draws a crucial distinction. Forty-three States employ objective shall-issue systems, which may require fingerprinting, background checks, mental-health records checks, and firearms training but do not condition licensure on a showing of special need. The Court indicates that shall-issue regimes are constitutionally permissible, subject to as-applied challenges if a particular regime is administered in a discriminatory manner. The holding invalidates discretionary proper-cause requirements, not all licensing systems for public carry.

### Concurrence (Justice Kavanaugh, joined by the Chief Justice)

Kavanaugh writes separately as a doctrinal stabilizer. First, the decision does not prohibit States from imposing licensing requirements for carrying a handgun for self-defense. Shall-issue licensing regimes, with objective criteria such as background checks, mental-health records checks, and firearms training, are constitutionally permissible and are not affected by the Court's decision. Second, Heller's list of presumptively lawful regulatory measures, prohibitions on possession by felons and the mentally ill, prohibitions in sensitive places, and conditions on commercial sale, remains good law. Nothing in the opinion should be taken to cast doubt on those longstanding regulations. Lower courts and litigants immediately invoked this concurrence as a limiting principle, a reminder that Bruen did not dismantle the architecture of American firearms regulation.

### Dissent (Justice Breyer, joined by Sotomayor and Kagan)

**The rejection of means-end scrutiny is wrong.** The majority claims that its near-exclusive focus on history accords with how the Court protects other constitutional rights. Breyer answers that this is not true. In First Amendment cases the Court regularly applies strict and intermediate scrutiny. Heller cannot plausibly be read as having rejected all traditional forms of means-end scrutiny. Heller spent pages on text and history because it faced a threshold question about whether the Second Amendment protects an individual right at all. Having answered that question, there was no need to go further; that does not mean Heller ruled out means-end scrutiny for every future case.

**The history-only method is indeterminate.** The two-metric test, how and why, sounds crisp but in practice invites the very judicial discretion the majority purports to eliminate. The choice of level of generality will often determine the outcome. A judge who describes a historical regulation at a high level of abstraction will find more analogues than a judge who describes it narrowly. What counts as a tradition? What is the proper level of generality? Which historical period matters most? How much mismatch is tolerable? Who bears uncertainty when the historical record is mixed?

**The Court ignores modern realities.** New York and its amici presented substantial data showing that stricter gun regulations are associated with lower rates of firearm-related death and injury, and that may-issue licensing regimes are associated with lower homicide and violent crime rates than shall-issue regimes. The majority does not engage with this evidence at all. By replacing means-end scrutiny with a history-only test, the Court removes the government's interest in public safety from the constitutional equation entirely.

**The broader methodological stakes.** The dissent warns that the majority's approach has implications far beyond the Second Amendment. If means-end scrutiny is inappropriate for firearms, on what ground does the Court continue to apply it in First Amendment, equal protection, and due process cases? Bruen's method invites comparison with recent doctrinal moves toward history and tradition analysis in Kennedy v. Bremerton School District and the deeply-rooted inquiry in substantive due process. If the method is general, it portends a sweeping transformation of constitutional adjudication. If it is specific to the Second Amendment, the Court owes an explanation for why firearms regulation alone is exempt from the scrutiny frameworks that govern the rest of the Bill of Rights.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side Applications (Consistent with the Holding)

**Hypothetical 1 (Same-Side).** A state passes a statute requiring concealed carry applicants to demonstrate a "specific and articulable need greater than that of the general public" and vesting denial authority in a local sheriff with no standards for review. This is a classic may-issue regime. Under Bruen, the Second Amendment's plain text covers the conduct (public carry for self-defense), and a discretionary showing of particularized need has no sufficiently similar historical analogue. The statute fails, following Bruen's direct application to New York's proper-cause requirement.

**Hypothetical 2 (Same-Side).** A municipal ordinance declares every public park, subway platform, city sidewalk, and shopping district a sensitive place where carry is prohibited. Under Bruen, expanding the sensitive-places category to cover essentially all places of public congregation that are not isolated from law enforcement defines the category far too broadly, and such an ordinance eviscerates the general right to public carry for self-defense. The ordinance fails.

### Opposite-Side Applications (Inconsistent with the Holding)

**Hypothetical 3 (Opposite-Side).** A state enacts a shall-issue concealed carry regime requiring a fingerprint-based background check, a mental health records check, completion of an approved safety course, and a modest processing fee. Bruen expressly preserves such regimes. Kavanaugh's concurrence, joined by the Chief Justice, specifies that objective shall-issue regimes are constitutionally permissible and not affected by the decision. The regime survives facially, subject only to as-applied challenges if administered in a discriminatory manner.

**Hypothetical 4 (Opposite-Side).** A federal statute bars firearm possession by persons subject to a domestic-violence restraining order that includes a judicial finding of credible threat of physical harm, entered after notice and a hearing. Bruen's analogical reasoning supports such a statute where the government can identify relevantly similar historical analogues (surety laws and going-armed laws) that justify temporary disarmament of persons adjudicated to pose a credible threat of violence. See [[Cases/United States v Rahimi (2024)|Rahimi]], which upholds 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(8) applying Bruen's framework.

### Fence-Sitter

**Hypothetical 5 (Fence-Sitter).** A state enacts a statute requiring every firearm purchased in the state to have microstamping technology that imprints a unique identifier on each cartridge case. Does this burden the Second Amendment's plain text covering the keeping and bearing of arms? If the government carries its step-one burden, the inquiry turns to historical analogues. Can the government identify a relevantly similar tradition of regulating the identifiable characteristics of firearms, or is this the sort of unprecedented regulation of a dramatic technological change for which Bruen demands a more nuanced approach? Courts divided sharply on technology-specific regulations after Bruen, and the level-of-generality question Breyer flagged in dissent is precisely the pressure point.

---

## Critique

Bruen's deepest contribution and its deepest vulnerability both lie in its methodological ambition. The opinion claims that it is merely following Heller, but the two-step framework it displaces had developed precisely because Heller did not supply a detailed doctrinal architecture. That Thomas reconstructs a framework he attributes to Heller while acknowledging that Heller never applied that framework is a pedagogically valuable illustration of how doctrines get built after foundational cases: the foundational case supplies text and history and a holding, and subsequent cases either apply a balancing test that the Court can later disown or, as here, extract a more demanding history-centered methodology.

The administrability problem is real. The level-of-generality question Breyer flagged is not abstract. In the Fifth Circuit's Rahimi decision, the court read Bruen to demand a historical twin for § 922(g)(8). The Supreme Court corrected that reading in [[Cases/United States v Rahimi (2024)|Rahimi]] by explicitly allowing analogical reasoning at a higher level of generality, using shared principles rather than identical mechanics. But the correction itself illustrates Breyer's point: how loose can the analogy be before history-and-tradition becomes indistinguishable from a freewheeling balance of policy considerations dressed in historical garb? Thomas himself, in solo dissent in Rahimi, warned that Rahimi's looseness effectively guts Bruen.

The shall-issue-versus-may-issue distinction is doctrinally load-bearing but textually thin. The Court offers no principled reason grounded in text, history, or tradition for why shall-issue regimes are permissible while discretionary regimes are not. The distinction is a practical compromise, a stabilizer that keeps forty-three States' licensing systems intact. That is a legitimate judicial choice, but it sits uneasily with the majority's insistence that text-history-and-tradition is the exclusive methodology.

The sensitive-places doctrine is undertheorized. The Court names three historical sensitive places (legislative assemblies, polling places, courthouses) and warns against expansion without offering courts a method for identifying new sensitive places. After Bruen, litigation over public parks, subway systems, churches, and nightlife districts has produced fragmented results that the opinion does not clearly resolve.

Finally, the dissent's methodological warning deserves weight. If Bruen's framework is general, constitutional adjudication across many domains may shift toward historical inquiry and away from judicial balancing. If it is specific to the Second Amendment, the Court owes an explanation for why firearms regulation alone is exempt from the scrutiny frameworks that govern the rest of the Bill of Rights. The lecture materials suggest that Kennedy v. Bremerton and post-Dobbs substantive due process cases reflect the general reading. If so, Bruen is a methodological manifesto as much as it is a gun case.

## Key Points

- Bruen is a scope-of-right case (the right to public carry for self-defense) and a methodology case (text-history-and-tradition replaces the two-step framework).
- Step 1 is the challenger's burden: plain text covers the conduct. Step 2 is the government's burden: historical tradition justifies the regulation. No means-end scrutiny.
- Analogical reasoning requires a relevantly similar historical analogue, not a historical twin. The how and why metrics are: comparable burden on the right, and comparable justification for imposing the burden.
- Founding-era (1791) and Reconstruction-era (1868) evidence get the most weight. Later outliers and isolated jurisdictions carry little.
- Shall-issue regimes with objective criteria remain permissible. Kavanaugh's concurrence (joined by Roberts) is widely cited as a limiting principle.
- Sensitive-places doctrine survives but cannot be expanded to swallow the right. Legislative assemblies, polling places, and courthouses are the paradigm cases.
- The dissent's administrability critique (level of generality, indeterminacy of analogical reasoning) is borne out by post-Bruen litigation and is partially acknowledged by the majority's rejection of a historical twin requirement in [[Cases/United States v Rahimi (2024)|Rahimi]].
