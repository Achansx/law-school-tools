# McDonald v City of Chicago (2010)

## Memory Jogger

Two years after Heller recognized an individual Second Amendment right against the federal government, McDonald holds that right fully applicable to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment. Alito's plurality takes the well-trodden Due Process path, finding the right to keep and bear arms fundamental to our scheme of ordered liberty and deeply rooted in history and tradition. Thomas reaches the same result but through the Privileges or Immunities Clause, urging the Court to overrule Slaughter-House and Cruikshank. Stevens dissents on his own substantive due process grounds, using a multi-factor liberty analysis that ironically produces less liberty than the plurality's historical test. Breyer dissents on federalism grounds, worried that incorporation forecloses state experimentation with gun policy. The case is the most doctrinally revealing modern incorporation decision: Thomas's concurrence openly names what the Court has spent over a century concealing, that incorporation is a dramatic reversal achieved through clause-shifting and silent treatment of Slaughter-House.

---

## Facts

Chicago and Oak Park, Illinois effectively banned handgun possession through municipal ordinances closely resembling the D.C. law struck down in Heller. Otis McDonald, a seventy-six-year-old retired maintenance engineer, and other Chicago residents challenged these bans on Second Amendment grounds. McDonald's neighborhood had deteriorated to the point that he feared for his safety, and he wanted to keep a handgun in his home for self-defense. Chicago's ordinance made handgun registration essentially unobtainable: the city refused to register any handgun not already properly registered, and the registration requirement had itself been frozen for decades.

## Procedural History

McDonald and co-petitioners sued in the United States District Court for the Northern District of Illinois, seeking declaratory and injunctive relief against the ordinances. The district court granted the City's motion to dismiss on the pleadings. The Seventh Circuit affirmed, holding itself bound by nineteenth-century precedent, specifically United States v. Cruikshank (1876), Presser v. Illinois (1886), and Miller v. Texas (1894), each of which had held that the Second Amendment does not apply to the States. The Supreme Court granted certiorari to decide whether the Second Amendment right recognized in Heller is incorporated against the States through the Fourteenth Amendment.

## Holding

The right to keep and bear arms recognized in District of Columbia v. Heller is fully applicable to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment. Chicago's and Oak Park's effective handgun bans are therefore subject to the Second Amendment's protections.

The plurality rests the holding on the Due Process Clause through the standard selective incorporation framework: the right is fundamental to our scheme of ordered liberty and deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition.

Thomas would rest the holding on the Privileges or Immunities Clause and would overrule the Slaughter-House Cases and Cruikshank to do so.

---

## Analysis

### Majority (Plurality Opinion, Justice Alito)

The plurality's method is selective incorporation through Due Process. The test has two components, which operate together rather than as sequential hurdles: whether the right is fundamental to our scheme of ordered liberty and whether it is deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition.

Alito marshals an extensive historical record. The Founders understood the right to keep and bear arms as inherited from English law. The right was central to the political thought surrounding ratification of the Fourteenth Amendment, when Congress sought to protect the freedmen's right to arms against Southern Black Codes that had systematically disarmed newly freed slaves. State constitutional protections for the right ran unbroken throughout American history. On this record, the right satisfies both components of the selective incorporation test.

The plurality declines to revisit the Slaughter-House Cases' narrow reading of the Privileges or Immunities Clause. For decades, the question of which rights the Fourteenth Amendment protects against state infringement has been analyzed under Due Process. Although petitioners and many legal scholars argue that Slaughter-House was wrongly decided, the scholars who agree on that point cannot agree on the Clause's full scope. The plurality takes no position in this academic debate and writes that it sees no need to reconsider Slaughter-House's interpretation here. The same result can be reached through the well-trodden path of selective incorporation under Due Process, which has been used for decades to incorporate nearly every Bill of Rights guarantee. Taking the road more traveled avoids the need to define the full scope of a clause that scholars, despite intense study, cannot agree about.

Alito is careful about scope. The plurality incorporates the right as defined in Heller, but adds no new definition of its boundaries. It does not decide the scope of the right outside the home, the standard of review, or anything about carrying firearms in public. Those questions are left to future cases. (They will be answered in large part by [[Cases/New York State Rifle and Pistol Association v Bruen (2022)|Bruen]] and [[Cases/United States v Rahimi (2024)|Rahimi]].)

### Concurrence (Justice Thomas, concurring in part and in the judgment)

Thomas agrees that the Second Amendment right applies to the States, but he reaches the result through the Privileges or Immunities Clause rather than Due Process. His method is different from Alito's. Where Alito uses history to identify a right as fundamental under a judicially constructed framework, Thomas uses history to recover the original public meaning of the Fourteenth Amendment's text. Both rely on history, but they are proving different things.

Thomas argues that the Slaughter-House Cases were grievously wrong. The Privileges or Immunities Clause, as originally understood, protected the rights of national citizenship, including at a minimum the rights enumerated in the Bill of Rights, against state abridgment. The evidence from the Thirty-Ninth Congress that drafted the Fourteenth Amendment overwhelmingly shows that its members understood the Clause to guarantee that freed slaves and all citizens would enjoy the full panoply of Bill of Rights protections, including the right to keep and bear arms. Senator Jacob Howard, who introduced the Amendment in the Senate, specifically listed the right to keep and bear arms among the privileges the Clause would protect.

Thomas is equally emphatic that Cruikshank is not a precedent entitled to any respect. Cruikshank held that the right to keep and bear arms was not a privilege of American citizenship, thereby overturning the federal convictions of white militia members responsible for the Colfax Massacre, a mass murder of approximately 150 Black citizens in Louisiana. The flaws in Cruikshank's interpretation are made evident by the original meaning of the Privileges or Immunities Clause, and its consequences, enabling the disarmament and terrorization of Black citizens throughout the Reconstruction South, make it all the more unworthy of deference.

Thomas acknowledges that substantive due process is a contradiction in terms. The notion that a clause guaranteeing only process before deprivation of liberty could define the substance of protected rights strains credulity for even the most casual user of words. The Privileges or Immunities Clause, by contrast, was designed to do exactly this work. Thomas would place incorporation on its proper textual and historical footing.

### Dissent (Justice Stevens)

Stevens agrees that the case should be decided under Due Process but rejects the plurality's incorporation framework. The question, he argues, is not whether the Second Amendment right as defined in Heller applies wholesale to the States. The question is whether liberty in the Due Process Clause independently protects a right to possess firearms, and if so, to what extent. This is a substantive due process case, and the Due Process Clause has its own content. It does not simply incorporate federal constitutional provisions as a package.

Stevens proposes a multi-factor analysis for identifying the substance of liberty. The Court should consider the nature and scope of the asserted right, its historical pedigree, the extent to which the right is bound up with personal autonomy, and the degree to which state regulation may serve competing interests. Critically, Stevens draws a distinction between liberty in the robust sense, the core freedoms that demand heightened judicial protection, and a mere liberty interest that receives only minimal scrutiny under rational basis review.

Applying this framework, Stevens concludes that firearms possession is not liberty in the robust constitutional sense but at most a liberty interest that state and local governments may broadly regulate. Although Stevens employs a more capacious, open-ended definition of liberty, one not tethered to the specific guarantees of the Bill of Rights, he ends up denying McDonald's claim. The plurality's more rigid history and tradition test, by contrast, protects the right. There is thus a deep irony: the Justice with the broader theory of liberty protects less of it here than the Justice with the narrower historical approach.

### Dissent (Justice Breyer, joined by Ginsburg and Sotomayor)

Breyer dissents on grounds of federalism and democratic experimentation. His central argument is that incorporation forecloses the ability of state and local governments to experiment with different approaches to gun violence. Different communities face different levels of gun violence and have different policy preferences. Incorporating the Second Amendment nationalizes a single constitutional standard and removes these choices from the democratic process.

Breyer also argues that the right to keep and bear arms differs fundamentally from other incorporated rights because its exercise implicates a primary concern of every government: the safety and indeed the lives of its citizens. Unlike free speech or the right to counsel, firearm possession creates direct risks of physical harm that justify a wider margin of regulatory latitude. He would hold that the Second Amendment need not be incorporated, and that even if it were, the interest-balancing approach he proposed in his Heller dissent should govern, permitting the kind of local, tailored regulation that Chicago's handgun ban represents.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side Applications (Consistent with the Holding)

**Hypothetical 1 (Same-Side).** A suburb near a major city passes an ordinance requiring that every handgun kept in the home be stored in a locked safe to which only the registered owner knows the combination, with criminal penalties for a handgun kept unlocked when the owner is present in the home. McDonald incorporates Heller against the States, and Heller expressly rejected a D.C. trigger-lock requirement that rendered operable handgun possession for home self-defense effectively impossible. The suburb's ordinance likewise frustrates the core of the right, the use of a handgun for immediate self-defense in the home, and would fail under McDonald for the same reasons.

**Hypothetical 2 (Same-Side).** A rural state enacts a statute that permits municipal governments to ban handgun ownership inside city limits so long as long guns remain permitted. McDonald incorporates the Heller right fully against state and local governments. A categorical ban on the most popular type of firearm for self-defense is precisely what Heller invalidated at the federal level, and McDonald extends that rule to state and local governments. The municipal bans would fall.

### Opposite-Side Applications (Inconsistent with the Holding)

**Hypothetical 3 (Opposite-Side).** A state enacts a law requiring that applicants for a concealed carry permit demonstrate proficiency with the firearm, complete an approved safety course, and pass a criminal background check. This is not the kind of categorical prohibition McDonald addressed. Heller expressly stated that nothing in its holding should cast doubt on longstanding prohibitions on firearms possession by felons or the mentally ill, and training and licensing requirements fit comfortably within that range of permissible regulation. McDonald does not forbid such regulation; it incorporates the Heller right, which itself accommodated it. (This intuition will be reinforced by [[Cases/United States v Rahimi (2024)|Rahimi]], which upholds a targeted disarmament for individuals found to pose a credible threat of violence.)

**Hypothetical 4 (Opposite-Side).** A state bars possession of firearms by persons convicted of domestic violence misdemeanors. The incorporation of the Second Amendment does not immunize individuals from categorical disqualifications grounded in the founding-era tradition of disarming those who pose a threat of violence. McDonald's holding applies the Heller right to the States, and Heller itself preserved longstanding prohibitions on possession by dangerous persons. [[Cases/United States v Rahimi (2024)|Rahimi]] later confirms that such disarmament fits within the historical tradition.

### Fence-Sitter

**Hypothetical 5 (Fence-Sitter).** A state passes a statute imposing a one-year waiting period and mandatory psychological evaluation before any first-time purchase of a handgun. Is this a permissible regulation akin to licensing, or a de facto prohibition that frustrates the right McDonald incorporates? The answer depends on whether the waiting period and evaluation are administered in good faith and whether they have a realistic completion timeline, and on whether the burden on the core self-defense right is categorical or merely incidental. A Bruen-style text-history-and-tradition analysis of the specific regulation would be needed, and the result could go either way. This is the sort of intermediate regulation that McDonald itself does not answer, leaving the question to future litigation.

## Critique

McDonald is doctrinally revealing precisely because of the Thomas concurrence. Alito's plurality presents incorporation as the ordinary application of settled doctrine. Thomas's concurrence reveals that the settled doctrine is not textually grounded and is in tension with the clause the Fourteenth Amendment's framers expected to do the work. The honest observation is that the plurality's hesitation to revisit Slaughter-House is driven less by confidence in Due Process incorporation than by concern about unsettling the many doctrines that now rest on substantive due process, including equal protection, enumerated and unenumerated rights. The plurality writes that it sees no need to reconsider that interpretation, but the deeper reason is that doing so would force the Court to confront the scope of Privileges or Immunities, which might protect more than the Bill of Rights or might simply duplicate the incorporated rights under a more defensible textual hook.

Stevens's dissent is analytically interesting for its irony. A more capacious theory of liberty, in his hands, produces less liberty than the plurality's narrower historical approach. That outcome suggests that a freewheeling substantive due process inquiry is no more predictable than a history and tradition inquiry, and may be more manipulable because it lacks the disciplining force of text or history. Breyer's dissent is vulnerable to the charge of selective federalism. In Lopez (1995) he argued that Congress should prevail over States to regulate guns near schools. In McDonald he champions state authority to regulate guns. The common thread may be deference to the regulating government, but the tension with principled federalism is evident.

The case leaves a great deal open. It does not define the scope of the right outside the home, does not specify a standard of review, and does not address public carry, the commercial sale of firearms, or regulations short of categorical prohibition. These questions become central in [[Cases/New York State Rifle and Pistol Association v Bruen (2022)|Bruen]] and [[Cases/United States v Rahimi (2024)|Rahimi]].

## Key Points

- McDonald incorporates the Heller right against the States through the Fourteenth Amendment by a 5-4 judgment, with only a 4-Justice plurality on the Due Process rationale; Thomas supplies the fifth vote on Privileges or Immunities grounds.
- The plurality uses the standard selective incorporation test: fundamental to our scheme of ordered liberty and deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition.
- Alito declines to revisit Slaughter-House, characterizing the question as an academic debate and preferring the well-trodden Due Process path.
- Thomas's concurrence is the most doctrinally revealing modern incorporation opinion. He would overrule Slaughter-House and Cruikshank, ground incorporation in Privileges or Immunities, and abandon substantive due process.
- Stevens dissents on an independent substantive due process theory with a multi-factor liberty analysis; Breyer dissents on federalism and experimentation grounds joined by Ginsburg and Sotomayor.
- McDonald does not define the scope of the right outside the home, does not specify a standard of review, and leaves public carry and non-categorical regulation to future cases.
- The case's deep tension, exam-likely, is between incorporation via Due Process (which ties the right to substantive due process doctrine) and incorporation via Privileges or Immunities (which would have a stronger textual footing but could unsettle adjacent doctrines).

## Connections

- [[Cases/Barron v Baltimore (1833)|Barron v Baltimore (1833)]]: the pre-Reconstruction baseline McDonald treats as the fixed historical point the Fourteenth Amendment was drafted to reverse; Alito's plurality uses Barron as the starting point for the incorporation history, and Thomas's concurrence frames the Privileges or Immunities revival as the clause the Reconstruction framers designed specifically to overturn Barron's 1833 holding that the Bill of Rights does not bind the States.
- [[Cases/Palko v Connecticut (1937)|Palko v Connecticut]], the selective-incorporation predecessor whose ordered-liberty test McDonald reworks into "fundamental to our scheme of ordered liberty" for the Second Amendment; the direction here is backward, with McDonald treating Palko as the doctrinal ancestor it cites and then re-glosses.
- [[Cases/Duncan v Louisiana (1968)|Duncan v Louisiana]], the jury-trial incorporation case whose Anglo-American-legal-system gloss McDonald borrows almost verbatim; Duncan supplies the precedent for shifting from Palko's "civilized societies" formulation to a narrower American-tradition test.

- [[Cases/New York State Rifle and Pistol Association v Bruen (2022)|New York State Rifle and Pistol Association v Bruen]]: answers the scope-and-standard questions McDonald explicitly left open (public carry, methodology).
- [[Cases/United States v Rahimi (2024)|United States v Rahimi]]: supplies the post-Bruen doctrinal stabilizer for targeted disarmament, complementing McDonald's incorporation move.

- [[Cases/Saenz v Roe (1999)]]: the doctrinal pivot eleven years earlier that briefly reanimated the Clause for a narrow national-citizenship right (equal treatment of new state residents) without disturbing Slaughter-House's general reading. Thomas's concurrence in McDonald treats Saenz as a proof-of-concept that the Clause can carry substantive weight when the Court wants it to, and as evidence that the obstacle to full revival is Slaughter-House alone. The pairing is the P or I Revival Pattern: Saenz works inside the Slaughter-House framework, McDonald's Thomas concurrence proposes to abandon the framework entirely.
- [[Topics/Individual Rights|Individual Rights]]

- [[Cases/Adamson v California (1947)|Adamson v California (1947)]]: the Palko-regime high-water mark whose Black dissent is the direct doctrinal ancestor of Thomas's McDonald concurrence. Black marshaled the same Reconstruction-era historical record (Bingham speeches, Howard's 1866 floor address, the pre-Slaughter-House understanding of P or I) that Thomas now deploys to argue for a full revival of the Privileges or Immunities Clause as the textual vehicle for incorporation. The plurality's unwillingness in McDonald to take up Thomas's invitation is the 2010 reply to Black's 1947 argument.
