# Pierce v Society of Sisters (1925)

## Memory Jogger

> Oregon's Ku Klux Klan-backed compulsory public school law struck down as an unconstitutional interference with parental rights and private school property — establishing that "the child is not the mere creature of the State."

---

## Facts

In November 1922, Oregon voters passed by initiative the Compulsory Education Act, which required all children between ages 8 and 16 to attend public school. The law, championed by the Ku Klux Klan and anti-Catholic nativist groups seeking to eliminate Catholic parochial schools, would have forced the closure of private and parochial schools as competitive educational institutions.

The Society of Sisters of the Holy Names of Jesus and Mary, a Catholic religious organization operating a network of parochial schools in Oregon, and Hill Military Academy, a private non-sectarian military school, both sought injunctive relief against enforcement of the Act. They argued that the law would destroy their institutions and that it unconstitutionally deprived parents of the liberty to direct their children's education and deprived the schools of their property without due process of law.

Governor Pierce of Oregon defended the Act as a legitimate exercise of the state's police power to standardize education, promote civic unity, and eliminate private institutions that allegedly fostered un-American loyalties.

---

## Procedural History

The United States District Court for the District of Oregon granted preliminary injunctions to both the Society of Sisters and Hill Military Academy, ruling that the Act unconstitutionally deprived the schools of their property and the parents of their liberty. Governor Pierce appealed directly to the U.S. Supreme Court under a provision then allowing direct appeal from three-judge district court decisions. The case was argued and decided together with an appeal by the Hill Military Academy.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority:** McReynolds (unanimous, 9-0) — no dissent

---

## Holding

The Oregon Compulsory Education Act of 1922, requiring all children to attend public schools to the exclusion of private and parochial schools, unconstitutionally interferes with the liberty of parents and guardians to direct the upbringing and education of children under their control. The Act also deprives private school corporations of their property and business without due process of law. "The fundamental theory of liberty upon which all governments in this Union repose excludes any general power of the State to standardize its children by forcing them to accept instruction from public teachers only." The child is not the mere creature of the State; those who nurture a child and direct the child's destiny have the right, coupled with the high duty, to recognize and prepare the child for additional obligations.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Justice McReynolds wrote again for a unanimous Court, extending and applying *Meyer v Nebraska* (1923) directly. The holding rested on two separate constitutional injuries: (1) the liberty of parents to choose private educational institutions for their children, and (2) the property rights of private school corporations whose businesses would be destroyed. On the parental-liberty side, McReynolds applied Meyer's "reasonable relation" test and found the Act lacked a reasonable relation to a legitimate state end: the state could require children to attend school, regulate curriculum, inspect facilities, and require that teachers be of "good moral character and patriotic disposition," but it could not mandate attendance at public schools only. The state's power to standardize education stopped short of compelling all children through a single state-controlled educational channel. McReynolds articulated the most enduring sentence of the case: the child is not "the mere creature of the State," and parents retain the right — "coupled with the high duty" — to direct their children's development. On the property side, the private schools had students, teachers, contracts, and expected patronage; the Act threatened to annihilate that business by eliminating parental choice. This property rationale grounded the decision in classical due process terms that all justices, including those skeptical of expanded liberty doctrine, could accept.

Four features of the opinion deserve close treatment. First, the political context. The Oregon Compulsory Education Act of 1922 was not a neutral educational regulation. It passed by ballot initiative after an organizing campaign led by the Oregon chapter of the Ku Klux Klan and the Scottish Rite Masons, and the Act was openly understood at the time as an instrument for eliminating Catholic parochial schools (which served largely Irish and Italian immigrant populations) and, to a lesser extent, the Hill Military Academy. The Court does not name the Klan in its opinion, but the unanimity of a bench that otherwise split along ideological lines in the 1920s reflects the justices' shared recognition that the Act was an attempt to use state power to close disfavored religious institutions. The property-rights framing let the Court reach the right result without having to confront the religion question directly — an elegant but historically suppressive choice. Second, the tension with Buck v Bell (1927). The same McReynolds Court that protected parental authority over educational choice in 1925 upheld compulsory sterilization of institutionalized women in 1927, suggesting that the Court's "liberty" catalog was not a principled limitation on state power but a shifting set of favored interests. Pierce protects the parent's choice of school; Buck authorized the state to intervene in the most intimate reproductive choices of the institutionalized. If Pierce's logic is that "the child is not the mere creature of the State," the logic plainly does not extend to institutionalized persons whom the Court thought lacking in full constitutional personhood. This inconsistency is one of the central indictments of Lochner-era doctrine. Third, the property-rights dual holding is strategically important. Because Pierce rests independently on private school corporations' property interests — the Act would "destroy [the schools'] business" by eliminating parental choice — the holding survives the 1937 repudiation of Lochner and the freedom-of-contract line. When West Coast Hotel retired economic substantive due process, Pierce's parental-liberty holding was vulnerable in theory, but the property-rights branch gave later courts an orthodox due-process hook for the same result. Fourth, the modern legacy: Pierce has become the cornerstone of parental-rights jurisprudence. Troxel v Granville (2000) treats Pierce as foundational authority for the parental presumption that protects nuclear-family decision-making from state override, and the framework Pierce announces now reaches home schooling, private religious education, and voucher controversies (though Pierce does not require public funding of private schooling, as Espinoza v Montana Department of Revenue and Carson v Makin later worked out).

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

1. **Hypo:** A state law requires that every student attend public school for at least three of four high-school years, regardless of parental preference, on the ground that the public-school exposure promotes civic cohesion. **Why:** The three-year requirement is a partial version of the all-or-nothing compulsory rule Pierce struck down. It does not eliminate private schools, but it forces every family that wants private schooling to mix four years of private instruction with three years of public instruction. That is a substantial interference with parental liberty to direct the child's education and with private schools' business interests in full-cycle enrollment. The asserted civic-cohesion rationale is essentially the same "standardize children" justification the Oregon Act offered, which Pierce rejected as outside "any general power of the State."

2. **Hypo:** A state law permits private schools to exist but requires them to teach curriculum content identical to public schools — the same textbook lists, the same lesson plans, the same assessments. **Why:** Pierce expressly preserves the state's power to "reasonably [regulate] all schools, to inspect, supervise and examine them, their teachers and pupils; [and] require that all children of proper age attend some school" — but the full-curriculum mandate in this hypo is not regulation, it is the elimination of educational distinction. If private schools cannot differ from public schools in any meaningful way, the parental choice of institution becomes merely the choice of location, which is not the liberty Pierce identifies. The hypo is a functional replay of the Oregon Act: parental choice of private school is formally preserved but substantively hollowed out.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

3. **Hypo:** A state law requires all private schools to comply with the same fire-safety, building-code, and sanitation standards as public schools. **Why:** Pierce is explicit that the state retains "power to regulate all schools, to inspect, supervise and examine them, their teachers and pupils; [and to] require that all children of proper age attend some school." Safety regulation is the paradigm case of regulation the opinion preserves. The law does not interfere with parental choice of school and does not destroy private schools' business interests beyond the ordinary compliance burden that applies to public schools too; there is no constitutional injury.

4. **Hypo:** A state directs its own public schools to offer bilingual education programs in designated communities. **Why:** The state's power to set the curriculum in its own schools is at the core of what Pierce preserves. Parental choice of school remains intact (parents who prefer monolingual instruction can choose private schools or move to a different district), and private schools are not affected at all. Pierce is a limit on what the state may do to private schools and parental choice; it is not a limit on public-school curricular design.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

5. **Hypo:** A state permits private schools to operate but conditions their accreditation, and therefore their graduates' eligibility for in-state university admission, on teaching a standardized state curriculum stripped of religious content. Accreditation is formally optional; the schools can refuse and continue to operate as unaccredited institutions. **Why (unclear):** This is the unconstitutional-conditions question dressed in Pierce's clothing. On one reading, the state is not prohibiting private schools from existing and is not compelling public-school attendance, so Pierce's core holding is not directly engaged; accreditation is a regulatory privilege the state may define. On another reading, conditioning the graduates' access to a major state benefit (public university admission) on the abandonment of religious content is precisely the kind of pressure that substantively forces private religious schools into public-school content, reproducing the Oregon Act's result by softer means. Modern unconstitutional-conditions doctrine (Rust v Sullivan, Legal Services Corp v Velazquez) and the Carson v Makin line on generally available state benefits pull in opposite directions here; the case fits awkwardly inside Pierce and probably resolves under a combination of Free Exercise and unconstitutional-conditions analysis rather than Pierce alone.

## Critique

**Progressive / left critique.** The feminist critique of Pierce is largely about whose liberty the opinion protects. McReynolds frames the right as belonging to "those who nurture [the child] and direct his destiny," but the legal and cultural default in 1925 was that this authority ran through fathers and male heads of households. The parental-rights tradition Pierce launches thus encodes a gendered power structure: it secures the patriarchal family unit against state interference without attending to whether all members of that unit share equally in the liberty being protected. Later scholars (Martha Minow, Barbara Bennett Woodhouse) have argued that a child-centered or family-welfare conception of liberty would look different and might authorize more state intervention in cases where parental authority itself threatens the child's interests. The progressive critique also worries about Pierce's consistency with Buck v Bell two years later: the same Court that protected parental authority against KKK-backed legislation allowed the state to sterilize institutionalized women whose parents and guardians had not consented. If Pierce's premise is that the child is not a creature of the State, Buck's premise that the institutionalized can be physically altered by state order is hard to reconcile.

**Originalist / textualist critique.** The Fourteenth Amendment's drafting history is silent on the specific question of educational choice, and originalists argue that Pierce's elaboration of "liberty" to include parental direction of schooling goes beyond what ratifiers would have recognized as the Due Process Clause's meaning. The property-rights holding is on firmer originalist ground because private schools' business interests fit more comfortably in traditional due-process protection against legislative destruction of established occupations; the parental-liberty holding, by contrast, imports a natural-law conception of family authority that originalists find unmoored from constitutional text. Some originalist commentators (Philip Hamburger, for example) have argued that the parental right Pierce announces is better understood as a matter of state common law than constitutional doctrine; others (the libertarian originalists) defend Pierce on the ground that the Fourteenth Amendment's "liberty" was originally understood to cover common-law freedoms including parental authority. The dispute inside originalism mirrors the debate outside it.

**Doctrinal anchor and modern reach.** The property-rights branch of Pierce is a strategically conservative choice that let the holding survive West Coast Hotel: a Fourteenth Amendment that protects schools' business interests against destruction-by-legislation is a narrower claim than one that protects a free-floating parental liberty. The Court could have reached the private-school result on property grounds alone and skipped the "child is not the mere creature of the State" language, but McReynolds insisted on both rationales, which is why the opinion now does double duty in parental-rights and property cases. On the modern reach: Pierce's logic plainly protects parental choice of home schooling, religious schooling, and private secular schooling, but it does not require the state to fund those choices. Zelman v Simmons-Harris (2002), Espinoza v Montana (2020), and Carson v Makin (2022) work out the separate question of whether, when the state chooses to subsidize education via voucher or tuition-assistance programs, it must include private religious schools on equal terms. Those decisions are Free Exercise and Establishment-Clause cases grafted onto Pierce's structural concession of parental choice; Pierce itself is agnostic on the funding question.

## Key Points

- Companion case to *Meyer v Nebraska* (1923), decided two years later; together the Meyer-Pierce pair establishes the parental-rights arm of substantive due process
- The KKK-backed legislative history is significant context: the Act was explicitly designed to eliminate Catholic parochial schools; the Court's unanimity signals that nativist-motivated educational coercion falls outside permissible state power
- The property-rights rationale (schools' business interests) gave the holding a doctrinal foundation independent of contested liberty-of-contract theories; this insulated Pierce from the Lochner-era repudiation
- "Child is not the mere creature of the State" becomes canonical shorthand for limits on state power over family and education; cited in *Troxel v Granville* (2000) and repeatedly in parental-rights litigation
- Survived West Coast Hotel because it addresses personal and family liberty, not economic liberty; Carolene Products Footnote 4 later theorized why non-economic liberties receive heightened protection
