# Corfield v Coryell (1823)

## Memory Jogger

> Justice Bushrod Washington's foundational gloss on Article IV Privileges and Immunities, defining them as fundamental rights belonging to citizens of all free governments, while holding that a state may still exclude outsiders from its common property like oyster beds.

---

## Facts

New Jersey enacted a statute reserving its oyster beds to state residents and prohibiting non-residents from taking oysters from New Jersey waters during certain seasons or with certain instruments, with penalties and vessel forfeitures for violators. Corfield, a Pennsylvania citizen, owned a vessel that was taken into New Jersey waters and used to dredge oysters in violation of the act. New Jersey officers, including Coryell, seized the vessel under the statute. Corfield, as general owner, brought a trespass action for the seizure. The act was challenged under both the Privileges and Immunities Clause of Article IV and the Commerce Clause.

---

## Procedural History

The action came before Justice Bushrod Washington sitting as Circuit Justice in the United States Circuit Court for the District of Eastern Pennsylvania. The case was presented to the court on the pleadings, allowing the circuit court to address the constitutional challenges to the New Jersey statute as well as the technical trespass question of whether Corfield, who had parted with possession to a bailee at the time of the seizure, could maintain the trespass action.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Opinion:** Justice Bushrod Washington, Circuit Justice (sitting at circuit)

---

## Holding

(1) The Privileges and Immunities Clause of Article IV, Section 2 protects only rights that are fundamental in nature, that belong of right to citizens of all free governments, and that have been enjoyed by citizens of the several states since they became sovereign. (2) The right to share in another state's common property, such as its oyster beds and fisheries, is not among those fundamental rights; a state may exclude non-residents from such common property without violating the Clause. (3) The New Jersey statute regulates common state property, not interstate trade or navigation, and therefore does not infringe the Commerce Clause. (4) As to trespass, a plaintiff who has parted with both actual and constructive possession to a bailee for a term cannot maintain trespass for an injury to the chattel occurring during the bailment.
