# National Pork Producers Council v Ross (2023)

## Memory Jogger

> The dormant Commerce Clause does not contain an almost-per-se rule against state laws whose practical effect reaches commerce outside the State, and facially neutral regulations survive so long as petitioners cannot allege a substantial burden on interstate commerce under Pike balancing.

---

## Facts

In 2018 California voters adopted Proposition 12 by ballot initiative. The measure bans the in-state sale of whole pork meat from pigs (and veal from calves and eggs from hens) confined in spaces that fail to meet California's minimum space standards, regardless of where the animal was actually raised. Because California consumers purchase roughly thirteen percent of the pork produced nationally, compliance with Proposition 12 effectively requires out-of-state producers who want access to the California market to adopt California's confinement standards.

The National Pork Producers Council (NPPC) and the American Farm Bureau Federation, representing pork farmers largely located in the Midwest, sued state officials to enjoin enforcement. They alleged that Proposition 12 violates the dormant Commerce Clause because (1) it has the practical effect of controlling hog farming practices outside California (an "extraterritorial" effect that they contended should be almost per se invalid), and (2) the compliance costs and forced restructuring of the national pork market constitute a substantial burden on interstate commerce that is clearly excessive in relation to California's putative local benefits under Pike v Bruce Church (1970).

---

## Procedural History

The District Court dismissed the complaint, concluding that NPPC's allegations failed to state a dormant Commerce Clause claim. The Ninth Circuit affirmed, rejecting both the almost-per-se extraterritoriality theory (concluding it had not survived the Court's post-Healy cases) and the Pike challenge (concluding that a mere showing of out-of-state compliance costs did not plead a substantial burden).

The Supreme Court granted certiorari to resolve whether Proposition 12's extraterritorial practical effects violate the dormant Commerce Clause and whether the pleadings stated a Pike claim. The Court affirmed the dismissal.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority (in part):** Gorsuch delivered the opinion of the Court (parts I, II, III, IV-A, and V joined by Thomas, Sotomayor, Kagan, Barrett) — affirming dismissal
- **Plurality (IV-B, IV-C, IV-D):** Gorsuch (joined by Thomas, Barrett) — judicial competence to perform Pike balancing of non-economic benefits
- **Concurrence in part:** Sotomayor (joined by Kagan) — agreeing petitioners failed to plead a substantial burden, but preserving Pike balancing where substantial burden is alleged
- **Concurrence in part, concurrence in judgment, dissent in part:** Barrett — joining all parts of Gorsuch's opinion but declining to endorse certain reasoning
- **Concurrence in part, dissent in part:** Roberts, C.J. (joined by Alito, Kavanaugh, Jackson) — would have held that petitioners adequately alleged a substantial burden and remanded for Pike balancing

---

## Holding

Petitioners failed to state a dormant Commerce Clause claim. The Court rejected the proposed "almost per se" rule against state laws with practical extraterritorial effects, reaffirming that the antidiscrimination principle, not an anti-extraterritoriality principle, lies at the core of dormant Commerce Clause doctrine. Proposition 12 is facially neutral and applies evenhandedly to in-state and out-of-state producers. Under Pike, petitioners failed to allege facts plausibly suggesting a substantial burden on interstate commerce (as opposed to compliance costs or forced restructuring of business methods), so the complaint was properly dismissed at the threshold without judicial balancing of local benefits and interstate burdens.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion (Gorsuch)

Justice Gorsuch rooted the decision in the Court's settled dormant Commerce Clause doctrine. The "very core" of that doctrine is antidiscrimination: Congress has affirmative power to regulate interstate commerce, and the negative implication forbids States from enacting laws driven by economic protectionism designed to benefit in-state interests at the expense of out-of-state competitors. Petitioners did not allege that Proposition 12 discriminates in purpose or effect. The Court declined to adopt an "almost per se" rule against state laws with extraterritorial practical effects, treating such a rule as inconsistent with the antidiscrimination focus of cases like Exxon v Governor of Maryland and CTS Corp. v Dynamics Corp. and with the pragmatic reality that almost any state regulation has some extraterritorial practical effect in a national market. On the Pike branch, Gorsuch accepted petitioners' framing that Pike requires a threshold "substantial burden" pleading, then found the complaint deficient: allegations of compliance costs and forced shifts in business structure do not amount to a substantial burden on interstate commerce as distinct from a burden on particular firms or methods of operation. In the plurality-only parts, Gorsuch (joined by Thomas and Barrett) doubted that courts are competent to balance incommensurable values (compliance costs versus moral and health interests) when the Pike analysis moves beyond smoking out hidden protectionism.

### Concurrence in Part (Sotomayor, joined by Kagan)

Justice Sotomayor agreed the judgment should be affirmed, but on a deliberately narrower ground. The complaint, in her view, fails not because Pike balancing is judicially unworkable for noneconomic benefits (the plurality's reach), but because petitioners "fail to plausibly allege a substantial burden on interstate commerce as required by Pike." The distinction matters: Sotomayor preserved Pike balancing as a live doctrinal tool whenever a plaintiff plausibly pleads a substantial burden, declining to sign on to the plurality's incommensurability critique that would effectively retire Pike for any case mixing economic and moral or health benefits. Her concurrence operates as a doctrinal floor: six Justices (Sotomayor, Kagan, Roberts, Alito, Kavanaugh, Jackson) affirm Pike's continuing viability for nondiscriminatory economic regulation, isolating the plurality's stronger anti-balancing position as a minority view despite the bottom-line affirmance.

### Concurrence in Part, Dissent in Part (Roberts, C.J., joined by Alito, Kavanaugh, Jackson)

Chief Justice Roberts agreed the antidiscrimination principle and the rejection of an "almost per se" extraterritoriality rule were correctly stated, but parted ways on the Pike branch. He read the complaint as alleging far more than ordinary "compliance costs": the petitioners pleaded pervasive industry-wide harms, fundamental restructuring of breeding-and-finishing operations across the national pork market, and pricing and supply effects that would be felt overwhelmingly outside California. Those allegations, he argued, do clear the substantial-burden pleading threshold under Pike. The Ninth Circuit erred by treating the harms as a single bucket of compliance costs and dismissing on the pleadings without giving petitioners a chance to develop the record. Roberts would have vacated and remanded for the lower court to perform an actual Pike balance: weigh the substantial interstate burden against California's putative local benefits and decide whether the burden is "clearly excessive in relation to the putative local benefits." On the doctrinal map this dissent matters because it confirms that four Justices read Pike as a meaningful and live test, not a rubber stamp.

### Concurrence in Part (Barrett)

Justice Barrett joined parts of Gorsuch's opinion but wrote separately to take a distinctive position on the judicial-competence question. She agreed with Roberts that the complaint plausibly alleges a substantial burden on interstate commerce: "Proposition 12's costs are pervasive, burdensome, and will be felt primarily (but not exclusively) outside California." But she also agreed with Gorsuch's plurality that Pike balancing requires the burdens and benefits to be "judicially cognizable and comparable" (citing Department of Revenue of Kentucky v Davis), and the moral and health benefits California advances are not commensurable with economic compliance costs. The result of those two moves: petitioners cleared the substantial-burden pleading bar in her view, but Pike balancing cannot proceed because the inputs are incommensurable. She therefore concurred in the judgment affirming dismissal. Barrett's position is the most theoretically interesting in the case, because it accepts Pike as live doctrine while denying its operability whenever moral or aesthetic state interests are in play, a category that includes many of the most contested modern dormant Commerce Clause challenges.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

1. **Labor-practice sales ban.** A state legislature bars the in-state retail sale of consumer goods manufactured by employers who deny basic collective-bargaining rights, framing the law as a moral and labor-standards measure. The statute is facially neutral (it applies to in-state and out-of-state manufacturers alike) and pursues the kind of moral or social-policy interest NPPC accommodates. Under Gorsuch's antidiscrimination framing the law clears the discrimination bar, and under the Pike branch as construed by NPPC the plaintiffs will struggle to plead a substantial burden distinct from compliance costs and forced restructuring of business methods. Result: dismissal at the pleadings, like NPPC.

2. **In-state sales conditioned on out-of-state emissions standards.** A state requires that automobiles sold within its borders meet specified tailpipe-emissions standards, regardless of where manufactured. Domestic and foreign automakers must adapt their production lines to comply if they want access to the market. The law is facially neutral, and any "extraterritorial" effect is the predictable consequence of a large market exercising its in-state regulatory authority. Under NPPC the antidiscrimination test is the operative inquiry; absent allegations of facial or purposive discrimination, the regulation survives. Petitioners would face the same pleading-stage barrier on the substantial-burden inquiry that doomed NPPC.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

3. **Facially discriminatory hog-producer exemption.** California amends Proposition 12 to exempt from the confinement standards any pork raised on a farm physically located in California. The amendment now distinguishes on the face of the statute between in-state and out-of-state producers, granting in-staters a competitive advantage in the in-state market. This is the canonical antidiscrimination violation NPPC reaffirms; the law is per se invalid unless the State can show it advances a legitimate local purpose with no nondiscriminatory alternative (a near-impossible showing under Maine v Taylor). Pike balancing never enters the picture. Result: invalidation.

4. **Direct price control on out-of-state sales.** A state enacts a law forbidding the retail sale of milk anywhere in the country at a price below a specified floor, imposed as a condition of the producer's eligibility to sell milk anywhere within the state. This regime directly fixes the terms of out-of-state commercial transactions, the precise concern animating Baldwin v G.A.F. Seelig and Healy v Beer Institute. NPPC explicitly preserves that narrower extraterritoriality doctrine for direct price regulation and direct control of out-of-state conduct. Result: invalidation under the surviving Healy / Baldwin line.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

5. **Renewable-portfolio standard with regional carbon accounting.** A state's renewable-portfolio standard counts in-state hydroelectric generation toward the renewables mandate but treats out-of-state hydroelectric power less favorably under a "marginal carbon" accounting methodology, on the theory that displacing in-state coal-fired power yields larger climate benefits. The state has a colorable noneconomic justification, but the design distinguishes between in-state and out-of-state hydropower in operation, and the in-state generators capture a regulatory rent. Under NPPC's antidiscrimination test, plaintiffs would argue the differential treatment is purposive economic protectionism dressed in carbon-accounting clothing; the State would respond that the methodology is genuinely neutral and that the in-state advantage flows from the geographic facts of carbon displacement. Lower courts have evaluated similar renewable-portfolio designs under both pre- and post-NPPC dormant Commerce Clause frameworks (see, e.g., Energy and Environment Legal Institute v Epel, Tenth Circuit upholding a Colorado RPS). Pike balancing remains theoretically available under Sotomayor's view but inoperable under Barrett's incommensurability framing. The case is genuinely close.

## Critique

**Federalist / state-autonomy critique.** Defenders of the result, including originalist commentators and animal-welfare advocates, treat NPPC as a salutary reaffirmation of state policymaking authority. On this view the dormant Commerce Clause was always about smoking out economic protectionism, and using it to invalidate facially neutral moral or health regulation invites federal judges to second-guess state legislatures on matters Article I leaves to political resolution. Gorsuch's antidiscrimination framing returns the doctrine to its historical core (Pike v Bruce Church and the antiprotectionist line in cases like Hunt and Carbone) and disciplines a body of law that had become untethered through expansive readings of "extraterritoriality."

**Economic-integration / progressive critique.** A different tradition, animating Roberts's partial dissent and substantial academic commentary, treats NPPC as understating the structural costs of state-by-state moral regulation in a national market. Proposition 12 forces compliance choices on producers whose customers are not California voters and who have no political voice in the regulating state. The pleading-stage threshold the majority erects, critics argue, makes Pike balancing inaccessible whenever regulation works through facially neutral product standards even though the practical economic effects are concentrated outside the regulating state. Donald Regan's classical "balkanization" framing finds its modern test in this case, and critics warn that NPPC opens the door to a patchwork of conflicting state product standards (animal welfare, ESG disclosures, AI-training data restrictions) that aggregate into a substantial drag on interstate commerce no single regulation can be said to impose.

**Doctrinal-fragmentation critique.** Six Justices reaffirm Pike's continuing relevance for nondiscriminatory state laws (Sotomayor and Kagan in concurrence; Roberts, Alito, Kavanaugh, and Jackson in partial dissent) yet only three (Gorsuch, Thomas, Barrett) sign the plurality's incommensurability critique that would shrink Pike's domain. The result is a doctrine that survives in nominal form but with deeply contested boundaries: lower courts have to apply Pike without a controlling majority view of what kinds of benefits are judicially cognizable, and litigants face genuine uncertainty about whether moral and health justifications can defeat substantial economic burdens. Whether NPPC marks a doctrinal realignment or merely a fragmented response to one pleading question may not be clear until the Court takes up a case where Pike balancing must actually be performed.

**Implicit overruling concern.** Roberts's partial dissent does not say so explicitly, but commentators have asked whether Gorsuch's decisive rejection of an "almost per se" extraterritoriality rule, combined with the plurality's incommensurability critique, leaves Baldwin v G.A.F. Seelig and Healy v Beer Institute as live precedent only for direct price-fixing. NPPC reaffirms those cases by name (in the negative, as the majority distinguishes them) but narrows their reach: facially neutral product standards with extraterritorial effects do not implicate the Baldwin / Healy line. The doctrine survives but is shrunk to direct out-of-state regulation, a domain narrower than many commentators previously read it.

## Key Points

- Reaffirms the antidiscrimination principle as the central dormant Commerce Clause test and rejects a per-se extraterritoriality rule.
- Fragments over both the doctrinal status of Pike and the judicial-competence question, producing a plurality-driven splintering despite the 5-4 bottom-line judgment.
- Establishes (or confirms) a pleading-stage "substantial burden" threshold for Pike challenges.
- Plurality's judicial-competence objection foreshadows ongoing friction over whether Pike balancing retains vitality when the benefits are noneconomic (moral, health, environmental).
- Practical result leaves States significant room to regulate products sold within their borders even when compliance reshapes production in other States.
