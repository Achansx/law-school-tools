# Williamson v Lee Optical of Oklahoma (1955)

## Memory Jogger

> "The day is gone" -- the Court's definitive burial of Lochner-era economic substantive due process; under rational basis review, a state law survives if a legislature could have conceived a rational justification, regardless of whether the law is wise, logical, or effective.

---

## Facts

Oklahoma enacted a law regulating the optometry profession. The statute prohibited opticians -- who grind and fill eyeglass prescriptions -- from fitting or duplicating lenses without a prescription from a licensed ophthalmologist or optometrist. A related provision prohibited opticians from soliciting business by advertising on a price basis. The law also restricted where eye doctors could practice, prohibiting them from operating within retail establishments.

Lee Optical, a company that fitted and sold eyeglasses through retail stores, challenged the Oklahoma law as an irrational economic regulation violating the Due Process and Equal Protection Clauses of the Fourteenth Amendment. The district court agreed and invalidated several provisions.

---

## Procedural History

A three-judge federal district court struck down major provisions of the Oklahoma optometry statute as violating the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process and Equal Protection Clauses. Oklahoma appealed to the Supreme Court.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority:** Douglas (unanimous -- all eight participating Justices joined; Harlan did not participate)

---

## Holding

The Oklahoma optometry statute does not violate the Due Process or Equal Protection Clauses of the Fourteenth Amendment. The era of using substantive due process to strike down economic regulations based on the Court's policy judgment is over. A law survives rational basis review if there is an evil at hand for correction and the legislative measure might be thought a rational way to correct it. Under the Equal Protection Clause, the legislature may address a problem one step at a time; the prohibition on invidious discrimination does not require it to regulate all similar evils simultaneously.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Justice Douglas's unanimous opinion is, structurally, the tombstone for Lochner-era economic substantive due process. Douglas acknowledged that the Oklahoma provisions were imperfect -- requiring a new prescription even to duplicate already-fitting lenses was arguably unnecessary, and prohibiting opticians from fitting lenses without a doctor's prescription in every case seemed over-inclusive. But Douglas held that it is enough if the legislature might reasonably have believed the law addressed an evil, even if the law is not perfectly calibrated.

Two key moves define the opinion. First, Douglas employed a "conceivable basis" test: courts ask whether any rational person in the legislature could have conceived a justification, not whether the legislature actually articulated one or whether it is the best available justification. Second, Douglas applied the "one-step-at-a-time" principle to the Equal Protection challenge: Oklahoma could restrict opticians without simultaneously restricting all other sellers of vision-related products. Legislatures are not required to tackle every aspect of a problem in a single statute.

Douglas closed with a direct appeal to democratic accountability: for protection against unwise economic regulations, the people must resort to the polls, not the courts.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

**Hypo 1: Mandatory Plumber Licensing for Water Heater Installation.** State X enacts a law requiring any residential water-heater installation (including replacements by homeowners) to be performed by a licensed master plumber, on the stated ground that improperly vented gas water heaters cause carbon-monoxide deaths. A hardware retailer challenges the law as irrational because most residential installations are mechanically trivial. Under Williamson, the state prevails: the legislature might reasonably have concluded that venting errors are frequent enough and dangerous enough to justify a across-the-board rule, even if some licensed-only installations are mechanically simple. Douglas's opinion expressly accepts this kind of overbreadth ("[T]he law need not be in every respect logically consistent with its aims to be constitutional"), and the one-step-at-a-time logic permits the state to regulate residential installation without simultaneously regulating every comparable task.

**Hypo 2: Pharmacist-Only Sale of Behind-the-Counter Allergy Medicine.** State Y restricts sale of a non-prescription allergy medicine to licensed pharmacists, even though equivalent formulations are sold freely in neighboring jurisdictions and the active ingredient has a long safety record. The legislature invokes possible interactions with prescription drugs. A big-box retailer challenges the rule as irrational and protectionist. Williamson sustains the regulation: even if the empirical case for pharmacist oversight is weak, a legislator could have imagined a benefit (catching dangerous drug interactions at point of sale). The "conceivable basis" move allows courts to credit hypothetical justifications the legislature never articulated, which is why economic rational-basis challenges almost always fail.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

**Hypo 1: Casket-Only-By-Funeral-Homes Statute.** State Z enacts a law making it a crime for anyone other than a licensed funeral director to sell a casket. The record shows the law was sponsored by the funeral-directors' association, funerals in the state do not require use of a casket sold by a funeral home, and the state concedes there is no health-and-safety justification because the statute does not regulate embalming, burial, or casket construction — only the retail channel. On these facts (drawn from Craigmiles v Giles, 312 F.3d 220 (6th Cir. 2002), and St. Joseph Abbey v Castille, 712 F.3d 215 (5th Cir. 2013)), even Williamson's "conceivable basis" test should fail: the record affirmatively refutes every health-and-safety rationale a court could imagine, and bare economic protectionism for a private group is not a legitimate state interest. Williamson speaks of an "evil at hand for correction"; when the only evil is competition against the politically favored incumbents, no rational-basis imagination can save the statute.

**Hypo 2: African-Hairbraiding Cosmetology License.** State enacts a law requiring African-hairbraiders to complete 1,500 hours of cosmetology training that covers no material relevant to braiding (chemical treatments, hair coloring, wet styling). The legislative record shows the cosmetology board lobbied for inclusion. The braiders challenge under Williamson rational basis. The regulation should fail because the training requirement bears no rational relation to the actual activity being regulated: even a "conceivable" health justification collapses when the legislatively required training has zero content overlap with the regulated conduct. This is the fact pattern courts have used (e.g., Cornwell v Hamilton, 80 F. Supp. 2d 1101 (S.D. Cal. 1999)) to show rational basis has a floor, at least when the record affirmatively refutes the imagined justification.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

**Hypo: Interior-Design Licensing Statute.** State A enacts a law requiring any person calling themselves an "interior designer" to hold a state-issued license contingent on a four-year accredited degree and a national examination. Unlicensed practitioners may still perform the same physical work but cannot use the title. The stated justification is public safety: commercial interior-design projects implicate fire code, egress planning, and ADA compliance. Courts have split on this kind of statute. A Williamson-maximalist would sustain it: a legislator could imagine that untrained designers misinterpret egress requirements and cause death in fires, and the title-restriction is one-step-at-a-time legislation. A Williamson-skeptic (following Craigmiles and St. Joseph Abbey) would invalidate it because (a) unlicensed work is allowed, so the "safety" rationale is undermined by the very carve-out; (b) the record typically shows no fires or injuries attributable to unlicensed designers; (c) the benefits accrue chiefly to incumbent licensees and accredited design schools. This is a genuine boundary case: whether Williamson's "conceivable basis" test permits courts to look behind a facially plausible safety rationale is still contested, and lower courts often disagree about how thick a reviewing court's skepticism may get before it starts impermissibly second-guessing the legislature.

## Critique

**Progressive / New-Deal functionalist critique.** The opinion vindicates Carolene Products's two-track settlement: economic regulation is for legislatures, not courts, because voters can punish bad policy at the ballot box while they cannot punish judges. On this reading, Williamson's "conceivable basis" test is not "no scrutiny" but a structural allocation of institutional competence. Sunstein and Ackerman treat Williamson as the endpoint of a principled New Deal settlement that frees the political branches to experiment with economic regulation.

**Libertarian / classical-liberal critique.** Institute for Justice scholars (Chip Mellor, Clint Bolick, Timothy Sandefur) argue that Williamson collapses Due Process into a rubber stamp, stripping economic liberty of any meaningful constitutional protection. They point to the record of Williamson itself: the Oklahoma statute was transparently an optometrists' cartel, and Douglas's willingness to imagine "evils at hand for correction" rewarded rent-seeking legislative behavior. The Sixth and Fifth Circuits' post-2000 decisions (Craigmiles, St. Joseph Abbey) represent an attempted revival of rational-basis-with-teeth that treats Williamson as setting a floor rather than a ceiling.

**Doctrinal-coherence critique.** Williamson's "conceivable basis" test is in tension with rational-basis-with-bite cases the Court itself decided later: City of Cleburne v Cleburne Living Center (1985) struck down a zoning decision targeting the mentally disabled under rational basis without imagining plausible justifications; Romer v Evans (1996) did the same to a Colorado referendum barring gay-rights protections. If rational basis were truly "conceivable basis" review, neither case could have come out that way. The Court has never reconciled the two standards, producing two de facto rational-basis tests that apply unpredictably.

**BMW v Gore tension.** The punitive-damages cases (BMW v Gore, State Farm v Campbell, Philip Morris v Williams) revived substantive due process review of economic impositions through the back door: a punitive-damages award too large becomes "grossly excessive" and violates due process. This is hard to square with Williamson's "day is gone" language — courts ARE striking down state-court economic impositions using substantive due process, but only when cast as due-process limits on damages rather than on regulation. Professor Chandler treats this pair as the quiet return of Lochnerism, with BMW reopening the door Williamson purported to close.

**Originalist / textualist critique.** From an originalist perspective, Williamson is correct for the wrong reasons: the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause was historically a procedural guarantee, so striking down economic regulation as substantively irrational was never within the clause's original meaning. On this view, the problem was not that the Court retreated from Lochner but that Lochner was wrong in the first place — and Williamson should have disavowed the underlying substantive-due-process methodology rather than quietly preserving it for a later revival in Griswold/Roe.

## Key Points

- Often cited as the definitive end of Lochner-era substantive due process for economic regulations.
- "Conceivable rational basis" is the key doctrinal innovation: the Court defers to any basis the legislature might have had, not just the basis articulated.
- The "one-step-at-a-time" Equal Protection principle gives legislatures broad power to regulate one piece of a problem at a time.
- BMW v Gore (1996) quietly creates tension with Williamson by reviving substantive due process review for punitive damages as "arbitrary" -- the same language Williamson says courts must not apply to economic legislation.
- Closely paired with United States v Carolene Products in teaching rational basis review; together they form the post-New Deal settlement.
