# United States v Morrison (2000)

## Memory Jogger

> Congress cannot regulate noneconomic violent conduct through aggregate-effects reasoning, and Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment reaches only state action, not private violence.

---

## Facts

In September 1994, Christy Brzonkala, a freshman at Virginia Polytechnic Institute, alleged that two members of the varsity football team, Antonio Morrison and James Crawford, had raped her in a dormitory shortly after they met. She filed a complaint under Virginia Tech's Sexual Assault Policy. Morrison was initially found guilty by the university's Judicial Committee and suspended for two semesters, but the sanction was set aside on appeal within the university and Morrison was ultimately allowed to return on a scholarship. Brzonkala withdrew from the school.

Brzonkala sued Morrison, Crawford, and Virginia Tech in federal court under 42 U.S.C. § 13981, the civil remedy provision of the Violence Against Women Act of 1994 (VAWA). Section 13981 provided a federal civil cause of action against persons who committed "crimes of violence motivated by gender." Congress had accompanied VAWA with extensive findings documenting gender-based violence's nationwide cost to the economy, estimated in the billions of dollars, through absenteeism, lost productivity, healthcare costs, and reduced interstate travel by women who feared for their safety.

The United States intervened to defend the statute's constitutionality under both the Commerce Clause and Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment.

---

## Procedural History

The U.S. District Court for the Western District of Virginia dismissed Brzonkala's § 13981 claim, holding that Congress lacked authority to enact the civil remedy under either the Commerce Clause or Section 5.

A panel of the Fourth Circuit reversed, upholding the statute. The Fourth Circuit then reheard the case en banc and, by a divided vote, affirmed the district court's dismissal, holding § 13981 unconstitutional.

The Supreme Court granted certiorari in both Brzonkala's and the United States' appeals and consolidated them. The Court affirmed the Fourth Circuit's en banc judgment 5-4, with the same five-Justice majority as in Lopez.

- **Majority:** Rehnquist (joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas) — 5-4

## Holding

Section 13981 exceeds Congress's authority under both the Commerce Clause and Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment. Congress may not regulate noneconomic violent criminal conduct based solely on aggregate effects on interstate commerce. And because the Fourteenth Amendment reaches only state action, Congress may not use its Section 5 enforcement power to create a civil remedy directed at purely private actors.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Chief Justice Rehnquist extended the Lopez framework. Gender-motivated violence is noneconomic, and the Court reaffirmed that aggregation reasoning applies only to economic activity embedded in a broader regulatory scheme. Accepting Congress's "but-for" chain (violence deters travel and work, which affects the economy) would erase any distinction between truly national and truly local matters and would grant Congress a general police power the Constitution withholds. The Court pointedly declined to defer to the extensive congressional findings, reaffirming that whether an activity substantially affects commerce is "ultimately a judicial rather than a legislative question." On Section 5, the Court applied the congruence-and-proportionality test from City of Boerne v Flores and concluded the statute was neither, because Congress had directed the remedy at private persons rather than at state officials who had failed to protect victims. The state-action principle drawn from The Civil Rights Cases (1883) was dispositive.

### Concurrence (Thomas)

Justice Thomas filed a brief concurrence reiterating the view he had expressed in Lopez: the "substantial effects" test is itself constitutionally suspect. On Thomas's original-meaning account, "commerce" at the founding referred to trade and exchange — mercantile activity — not every economic undertaking that might aggregate into something sizable. The majority's economic/noneconomic line is therefore not the right fix; it merely makes an illegitimately broad doctrine slightly less broad. Thomas would replace the entire substantial-effects framework with a narrower test grounded in the actual scope of "commerce" understood in 1789. Morrison's outcome was correct on Thomas's view, but for a deeper structural reason: the very foundation of aggregation-based Commerce Clause doctrine is constitutionally unsound and should be abandoned in a future case.

### Dissent (Souter)

Justice Souter, joined by Stevens, Ginsburg, and Breyer, argued that the majority resurrected the discredited pre-1937 formalism of the direct/indirect distinction under new vocabulary. The economic/noneconomic line is no more principled than the old production/commerce line: both ask judges to categorize activity by abstract label rather than assess its real-world relationship to the interstate market. Souter pointed to the extensive record Congress had assembled — four years of hearings, seventy-plus congressional findings, expert economic testimony estimating the economic toll of gender violence in the billions of dollars — and argued the Court was flatly refusing to engage with that record on the merits. In Souter's view, rational-basis review of Commerce Clause legislation should end the inquiry once Congress has assembled a record demonstrating a plausible economic nexus, which VAWA plainly did. The majority's creation of a "substantially weakened" finding doctrine — where congressional facts become legally irrelevant once the Court labels the activity "noneconomic" — converts Commerce Clause review into an unreviewable judicial classification exercise.

### Dissent (Breyer)

Justice Breyer focused on the institutional question of who should police Commerce Clause limits and, separately, offered a sharp critique of the Section 5 holding. On the Commerce Clause, Breyer argued that the states are structurally represented in Congress through the Senate and through the political process generally; the "political safeguards of federalism" (Herbert Wechsler's concept, also invoked by Garcia v San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority) make judicial enforcement of federalism limits both unnecessary and dangerous. Federal courts are not competent to police the economic/noneconomic line in a way that gives coherent guidance: the majority's distinction has no principle that prevents it from invalidating federal statutes in areas — labor, environmental regulation, civil rights — where Congress's authority has been settled for decades. On Section 5, Breyer contended that the majority's analysis was even more problematic than the Commerce Clause holding: if state law systematically failed to protect women from gender violence — the very factual finding Congress had made — then Section 5 exists precisely to correct that failure. Reading Section 5 to bar a federal remedy against private actors who cause the injury the states have failed to prevent leaves victims in a constitutional no man's land.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

1. **Federal civil remedy for random assault.** Congress enacts a private right of action against any person who commits a violent assault, with extensive findings that aggregate healthcare and lost-productivity costs of assault amount to hundreds of billions of dollars annually and substantially affect the interstate economy. This fails for the same reason Morrison fails: the regulated conduct (physical assault) is noneconomic criminal activity historically within the province of state police power. Aggregate economic effects from violence cannot convert a noneconomic activity into commercial one; the "but-for" chain is identical to the one Morrison rejected. The remedy falls.

2. **Federal anti-stalking civil statute.** Congress creates a federal civil cause of action for stalking, backed by findings about costs to victims' employment and interstate mobility. Stalking is paradigmatically noneconomic personal conduct. No matter how detailed the congressional findings about its economic downstream effects, the Morrison holding bars the aggregation move for non-commercial, non-economic local criminal conduct that has always been regulated by state criminal law. The statute fails.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

3. **Civil remedy tied to instrumentalities of commerce.** Congress enacts a federal civil remedy for gender-motivated violence that occurs on commercial aircraft, passenger trains, interstate highways, or in hotels and restaurants engaged in interstate commerce. This survives Morrison because it falls under the first Lopez category — channels and instrumentalities of interstate commerce — rather than the substantial-effects category that Morrison addressed. Congress may regulate what happens in the channels and instrumentalities of commerce without running the noneconomic-activity gauntlet. The Lopez/Morrison framework has three categories, and this statute operates in the one the majority preserved.

4. **Section 5 remedy targeting state actors.** Congress identifies a systematic pattern of state law enforcement agencies declining to investigate or prosecute gender-motivated violence against women, and enacts a statute imposing federal remedies against the state agencies (not private assailants) and requiring nondiscriminatory investigation of reported crimes. This satisfies Morrison's Section 5 constraint: it is directed at state action (the agencies' discriminatory enforcement policies), it is congruent to the constitutional injury (state denial of equal protection), and it is proportional to the identified pattern of state-actor discrimination. The Civil Rights Cases and Morrison both require state-action nexus; this statute provides one.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

5. **Gender-motivated violence at a commercial establishment.** A woman is sexually assaulted in the parking lot of a national hotel chain by a private actor. The assault is alleged to have been motivated by gender. Congress enacts a federal civil remedy specifically covering gender-motivated violence occurring on premises of businesses engaged in interstate commerce. This is a genuine fence-sitter: the location (a commercial establishment serving interstate travelers) triggers the Heart of Atlanta/Katzenbach v McClung commercial nexus, which the majority carefully did not disturb. But Morrison's holding addresses whether the conduct itself — the assault — is economic, not merely whether the location is commercial. One reading: location-nexus brings the case under the channels-and-instrumentalities category, satisfying Morrison. Another reading: Morrison forbids the aggregation move for any noneconomic conduct, regardless of venue. Courts have split on analogous questions.

---

## Critique

**Progressive critique.** Scholars including Erwin Chemerinsky and Reva Siegel argue the economic/noneconomic distinction is both unprincipled and gendered. The Lopez majority drew the line at guns-near-schools; Morrison extended it to gender violence. But the line is artificial: domestic violence and sexual assault generate demonstrable economic externalities (healthcare, absenteeism, insurance costs) that are no less real than the effects of price-fixing. More pointedly, Siegel argues that the majority's framework systematically undervalues harms that fall disproportionately on women because those harms have historically been consigned to state family-law and criminal-law regimes, which is precisely the regulatory framework that Morrison said Congress must defer to. Reading the Commerce Clause to track pre-existing regulatory distributions entrenches existing subordination patterns rather than enabling congressional remediation.

**Originalist critique.** Thomas's concurrence reflects a different dissatisfaction: the majority's economic/noneconomic test is itself an invention of the post-New Deal period that has no grounding in the original meaning of "commerce." Original-meaning scholars including Randy Barnett and Gary Lawson argue that "commerce" in 1789 meant trade or mercantile exchange — not production, manufacturing, agriculture, or violence. On this view, Morrison is correct in striking VAWA but incorrect in using a "substantial effects" test (even one limited to economic activity) as the governing standard. The right rule would require a direct nexus between the regulated activity and commercial exchange, which even most ordinary economic activity would not satisfy. Both progressive and originalist critiques thus agree that the Morrison majority's line is unstable, but they disagree about which direction it should move.

## Key Points

- Consolidates Lopez's economic-noneconomic distinction into a workable rule for future cases.
- Vote: 5-4, same lineup as Lopez five years earlier.
- Pairs Commerce Clause limit with a separate Section 5 limit grounded in The Civil Rights Cases (1883).
- Doctrinal companion to City of Boerne v Flores (1997) on Section 5 congruence and proportionality.

## Connections

- [[Cases/United States v Lopez (1995)|United States v Lopez (1995)]] — Morrison applies and extends Lopez; Rehnquist majority explicitly rejects any invitation to limit Lopez to its GFSZA context and treats the three-category framework as controlling doctrine.
- [[Cases/Wickard v Filburn (1942)|Wickard v Filburn (1942)]] — Morrison limits Wickard's aggregation principle to economic activity: Congress may aggregate economic effects of commercial activity (Wickard), but may not use the same move for noneconomic criminal conduct (Morrison).
- [[Cases/Gonzales v Raich (2005)|Gonzales v Raich (2005)]] — Raich distinguishes Morrison by holding that homegrown marijuana is an economic commodity within a comprehensive federal drug regulatory scheme; the economic-activity determination saves the statute from Morrison's noneconomic-activity bar.
- [[Cases/City of Boerne v Flores (1997)|City of Boerne v Flores (1997)]] — Morrison's Section 5 analysis applies Boerne's congruence-and-proportionality test; the failure of VAWA's civil remedy to target state action is both a state-action problem (Civil Rights Cases) and a proportionality problem (Boerne's remedial-vs-substantive distinction).
- [[Cases/Civil Rights Cases (1883)|Civil Rights Cases (1883)]] — the Section 5 holding rests on the 1883 principle that the Fourteenth Amendment reaches only state action; Morrison reaffirms that Congress cannot bootstrap its way around this limit by creating federal remedies against private actors for conduct that violates women's equality.
