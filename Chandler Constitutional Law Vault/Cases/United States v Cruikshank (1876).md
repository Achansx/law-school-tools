# United States v Cruikshank (1876)

## Memory Jogger

> A federal prosecution following the Colfax Massacre collapsed because the Fourteenth Amendment was held to reach only state action, not private conspiracies, and the Bill of Rights was held to restrain only Congress.

---

## Facts

On Easter Sunday 1873, a white paramilitary group in Grant Parish, Louisiana, massacred scores of Black Reconstruction-era citizens who had gathered at the Colfax courthouse to defend a contested local election. Federal prosecutors indicted William Cruikshank and other participants under the Enforcement Act of 1870, which criminalized conspiracies to injure, oppress, threaten, or intimidate any citizen in the free exercise of any right or privilege secured by the Constitution or laws of the United States.

The indictment contained sixteen counts alleging, among other things, conspiracy to prevent the victims from assembling for lawful purposes, from bearing arms, from voting, and from enjoying the rights and privileges of life and liberty. The counts were framed generically, tracking the language of the Enforcement Act and referencing constitutional "rights and privileges" without specifying the particular federal right each defendant was alleged to have conspired against.

The prosecution arose against the backdrop of collapsing federal commitment to Reconstruction, and the decision would help dismantle the federal criminal civil-rights apparatus for generations.

---

## Procedural History

A federal jury in the Circuit Court for the District of Louisiana convicted Cruikshank and two co-defendants on several counts. The trial judges, Circuit Justice Bradley and District Judge Woods, divided on motions in arrest of judgment and certified the questions to the Supreme Court on a division of opinion.

The Supreme Court unanimously reversed and ordered the defendants discharged, holding the indictment fatally defective.

---

- **Majority:** Waite, C.J. (unanimous) — 9-0

---

## Holding

The Bill of Rights restrains only the federal government; the First Amendment right of assembly and the Second Amendment right to bear arms are not rights granted by the Constitution that the federal government may protect against private interference. The Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process and Equal Protection Clauses reach only state action; they do not reach private conspiracies and add nothing to the rights of one citizen against another. A federal indictment alleging conspiracy to interfere with such rights must specify the particular federal right targeted and cannot rest on generic statutory language.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Chief Justice Waite first reiterated Barron v Baltimore: the first ten amendments restrain only Congress. The right to assemble, he reasoned, is a pre-constitutional attribute of citizenship that the federal government protects only when the assembly relates to national purposes, such as petitioning Congress. Protection of ordinary assembly is a matter of state police power. The Second Amendment likewise restricts only federal interference; protection against private violence directed at arms ownership belongs to state governments. The Fourteenth Amendment offers no alternative federal hook: its prohibitions run only against the state, so a private conspiracy among citizens cannot satisfy its state-action threshold. With no underlying federal right supporting several of the counts, and with the remaining counts alleged in language so generic that they failed to apprise defendants of the specific federal right at issue, the indictment could not stand. Waite closed by observing that redress for private violence belongs to the states and ordinary criminal law.

## Key Points

- Core statement of the state-action limitation on the Fourteenth Amendment.
- Reaffirms Barron v Baltimore for the First and Second Amendments before incorporation doctrine existed.
- Vote: 9-0, though Clifford concurs only on indictment grounds.
- Helped dismantle Reconstruction-era federal civil-rights enforcement alongside the Slaughter-House Cases (1873) and the Civil Rights Cases (1883).
- The Second Amendment holding was formally interred by McDonald v City of Chicago (2010); the state-action rule largely persists.
