# City of Cleburne v Cleburne Living Center (1985)

## Memory Jogger

**The Group Home Case.** Cleburne Living Center wanted to operate a group home for mentally retarded adults; the city required a special use permit and denied it. The Court (White) held that mental retardation is *not* a quasi-suspect classification — rational basis applies. But then, applying what scholars call "rational basis with bite," the Court struck down the ordinance because the city's stated reasons revealed nothing more than irrational fear and prejudice toward people with intellectual disabilities. Famous for Marshall's concurrence arguing the Court is really doing something between rational basis and intermediate scrutiny.

## Facts

- Cleburne Living Center (CLC) sought to operate a group home for thirteen mentally retarded adults at a specific property in Cleburne, Texas
- The Cleburne zoning ordinance required a special use permit for "hospitals for the insane or feeble-minded, or alcoholic or drug addicts, or penal or correctional institutions"
- The City Council denied the permit after conducting a public hearing; stated reasons included concerns about flooding, the attitude of nearby junior high school students, the size of the home relative to nearby single-family properties, and opposition from neighbors
- The Fifth Circuit held that mental retardation is a quasi-suspect classification requiring intermediate scrutiny, and struck down the permit denial

## Procedural History

- District court: upheld ordinance; rational basis satisfied
- Fifth Circuit: reversed; mental retardation is a quasi-suspect class; intermediate scrutiny; ordinance fails
- Supreme Court: affirmed result (ordinance unconstitutional) but rejected Fifth Circuit's reasoning; declined to apply intermediate scrutiny; applied rational basis but struck down the ordinance under that standard

## Judicial Votes

- **Unanimous in judgment** (9-0) that the permit denial was unconstitutional
- **Majority**: White (joined by Burger CJ, Powell, Rehnquist, Stevens, O'Connor) — rational basis review; but ordinance fails
- **Concurrence/partial dissent**: Marshall (joined by Brennan, Blackmun) — agrees result is correct; argues the Court is actually applying heightened scrutiny, and should be honest about it; advocates a sliding scale of review
- **Concurrence/partial dissent**: Stevens (joined by Burger CJ) — agrees; suggests the Equal Protection Clause requires just reasonableness without sharply tiered standards

## Holding

Mental retardation is not a quasi-suspect classification; rational basis review applies. To withstand equal protection review, legislation distinguishing between the mentally retarded and others must be rationally related to a legitimate governmental purpose. However, the City of Cleburne's denial of the special use permit fails even rational basis review because the reasons offered by the city — concerns about the reaction of students, neighbors' fears, distance to a 500-year flood plain — revealed nothing more than irrational prejudice and bare negative attitudes toward the residents, which are not legitimate governmental interests.

## Analysis

### Majority (White)

White walks through the quasi-suspect factors one by one and concludes that mental retardation does not satisfy them. First, he acknowledges that people with intellectual disabilities "have a reduced ability to cope with and function in the everyday world" and are "different, immutably so, in relevant respects." That observation cuts against quasi-suspect status under White's framing: when a characteristic is genuinely relevant to a wide range of legitimate legislative ends (education, public safety, social services), a presumption of unconstitutionality would be counterproductive because most legislation reasonably distinguishing on that ground is valid. White contrasts this with race and gender, where the trait is "seldom relevant" to legitimate goals.

Second, White points to the substantial legislative record of federal and state statutes protecting people with intellectual disabilities — the Education for All Handicapped Children Act, the Developmentally Disabled Assistance and Bill of Rights Act, state civil rights statutes, and accommodations requirements. That legislative attention "negates any claim that the mentally retarded are politically powerless in the sense that they have no ability to attract the attention of lawmakers." Political access, for White, is one of the core indicators of whether a group needs special judicial protection, and here it is present in a meaningful way.

Third, White is expressly worried about the cascading effect of creating a new quasi-suspect class. If mental retardation qualifies, so might the elderly (which Murgia had already declined to recognize), the disabled generally, the infirm, and "a variety of other groups who have perhaps immutable disabilities setting them off from others," each of which would then trigger heightened scrutiny. White treats that cascade as both bad doctrine (every legislative line would become potentially suspect) and bad institutional design (courts would have to draw increasingly fine distinctions with no clean textual anchor).

Having declined heightened scrutiny, White then applies rational basis — but a version of it that stops short of its usual deferential extreme. The city's stated grounds for the permit denial are that (1) the residents might be harassed by junior-high students, (2) neighbors had expressed fears, (3) the property sat within a 500-year flood plain, (4) the property sat on a 500-year flood plain (the ordinance did not bar other permitted uses from similar locations), and (5) density and legal-responsibility concerns (other permitted uses, including boarding houses and nursing homes, were not subjected to comparable permit requirements). White takes each reason and compares it against the city's treatment of similarly situated properties. On each, the city cannot explain why the mental retardation of the residents matters to the stated interest: junior-high harassment would target any residents of a group home; flood-plain concerns apply equally to boarding houses; density would distinguish not by classification but by occupancy count. What remains, after stripping away the surface-level justifications, is the underlying fact that the neighbors did not want the home because of who its residents would be. In the opinion's key phrase: "mere negative attitudes, or fear, unsubstantiated by factors which are properly cognizable in a zoning proceeding, are not permissible bases for treating a home for the mentally retarded differently from apartment houses, multiple dwellings, and the like." White adds that "private biases may be outside the reach of the law, but the law cannot, directly or indirectly, give them effect" — the Palmore v Sidoti principle, here applied for the first time to disability.

### Concurrence (Marshall, Brennan, Blackmun)

Marshall, joined by Brennan and Blackmun, concurs in the judgment but rejects the majority's methodological posture. His central argument is that the Court's rigid three-tier framework (rational basis / intermediate / strict) was never how the Court actually decided Equal Protection cases, and Cleburne proves it: what the majority calls "rational basis" here is patently more searching than the forgiving deference given to economic regulation in Railway Express Agency or Williamson v Lee Optical, and the majority's unwillingness to say so produces doctrinal confusion in the lower courts. Marshall's sliding-scale position — which he had defended since his San Antonio v Rodriguez dissent (1973) — holds that "the level of scrutiny employed in an equal protection case should vary with the constitutional and societal importance of the interest adversely affected and the recognized invidiousness of the basis upon which the particular classification is drawn." Honest sliding-scale review would openly acknowledge that interests like housing in one's chosen community, when denied to a historically disadvantaged group, justify more rigorous review than a routine economic-regulation challenge.

Marshall then builds a powerful historical case that people with intellectual disabilities have been subject to "a lengthy and tragic history of segregation and discrimination that can only be called grotesque." He recounts state-mandated sterilization (Buck v Bell is the dark undercurrent), eugenic segregation in "colonies" and warehouse institutions, categorical exclusion from public schools into the 1970s, and state laws classifying the "feebleminded" as unfit for marriage, voting, or independent residence. That history, in Marshall's view, is a paradigmatic quasi-suspect history, closer to the racial segregation history than to the economic-regulation cases the majority cites. Recent protective legislation does not cancel this history; it is an acknowledgment of it, like the Reconstruction amendments do not preclude heightened race-based scrutiny but rather ground it. The cascade concern White voices is also exaggerated, Marshall argues: heightened scrutiny can be calibrated group by group on familiar factors (history of discrimination, political access, immutability, relevance of the trait), and courts do this without trouble for sex, illegitimacy, and alienage.

Marshall's opinion closes by labeling the majority's actual method "second-order rational-basis review" — a frankly heightened scrutiny wearing the label of rationality. His accusation that the majority is doing covertly what it refuses to do openly is the intellectual seed of "rational basis with bite" as a doctrinal category, and later opinions (Romer, Windsor, Obergefell) lean on Marshall's framing even when their authors do not cite him directly.

### Concurrence (Stevens, Burger CJ)

Stevens, joined by Chief Justice Burger, concurs in the judgment but on still narrower ground. For Stevens, the tiered-scrutiny apparatus is simply the wrong frame. The Equal Protection Clause asks one question: is the classification a rational way for a rational decisionmaker to pursue a legitimate public end, taking account of context and the strength of the interests involved? Tier labels (strict, intermediate, rational) describe clusters of answers to that single question, not separate doctrines. Applied to Cleburne, the answer is clear without invoking any label: the city's stated reasons do not rationally explain why a home for thirteen mentally retarded adults is treated differently from a comparably sized fraternity house, boarding house, or nursing home. Stevens's concurrence is rhetorically milder than Marshall's but doctrinally in the same family: both reject the pretense that the Court is applying the same rational basis it applies to economic regulation. Stevens's position would not develop much doctrinal influence in isolation, but it converges with Marshall's in forcing the Cleburne majority's rationale to stand on its own without the cover of tier formalism.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

1. **Hypo:** A city adopts a zoning ordinance requiring a special use permit for "halfway houses for recovering drug addicts" but not for boarding houses, fraternity houses, or nursing homes of comparable size. The ordinance is applied to deny a permit to a residential treatment facility, and neighbors' petitions cite concerns about relapse, crime, and "the character of the neighborhood." **Why:** The case is structurally identical to Cleburne. Applying rational basis with bite, a reviewing court would compare the ordinance's treatment of the halfway house against its treatment of other uses with equivalent density and legal-responsibility profiles. If the stated justifications are proxies for community aversion — concerns that are stereotypical rather than tied to facility-specific conduct — the ordinance fails. The "bare desire to harm" / "mere negative attitude" framework from Cleburne directly controls.

2. **Hypo:** A state statute prohibits gay and lesbian couples from adopting children but allows all other unmarried cohabitants to adopt. The stated purpose is "protecting children's moral development." **Why:** Under Cleburne's animus principle extended through Romer v Evans (1996), United States v Windsor (2013), and Obergefell v Hodges (2015), legislation whose operative justification is hostility toward a politically unpopular group fails even rational basis review. The asserted interest — moral development — would need evidence linking gay-couple adoption to harmful outcomes, and in the absence of such evidence, a court applying Cleburne-style rational basis with bite would treat the classification as animus-driven. The line of cases is a direct descendant of Cleburne's refusal to accept "mere negative attitudes" as a legitimate governmental interest.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

3. **Hypo:** A state mandates retirement at age 50 for uniformed state police officers, citing physical demands of patrol duty. **Why:** This is essentially Massachusetts Board of Retirement v Murgia (1976). Age classifications receive ordinary rational basis, not heightened scrutiny, and the stated interest (public safety through physical fitness) is a legitimate governmental purpose with a rational connection to a mandatory retirement age. Unlike Cleburne, the age classification tracks a real distinction between the classified group and others that is relevant to the legitimate end, so the "proxy for animus" analysis does not apply. Cleburne's rational-basis-with-bite framework sits comfortably alongside Murgia because the two cases involve different evidentiary postures — Cleburne had no real connection to the city's stated interests; Murgia had a plausible one.

4. **Hypo:** Congress enacts Title I of the Americans with Disabilities Act requiring state employers to make reasonable accommodations for disabled employees, and abrogates state sovereign immunity. **Why:** This is Board of Trustees v Garrett (2001). Because Cleburne holds that disability classifications receive only rational basis, Congress cannot use Section 5 to impose a higher standard on state employers without a robust record of irrational state disability discrimination — and the Court held that record insufficient. Garrett is essentially Cleburne applied to the congressional-enforcement context: the low constitutional baseline that made Cleburne-style bite necessary at all also means Congress has limited Section 5 room under Boerne's congruence-and-proportionality test. Title II of the ADA (public services) survived in Tennessee v Lane (2004), but only because the access-to-courts fundamental right raised the constitutional baseline.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

5. **Hypo:** A city zoning ordinance requires a special use permit for "residential facilities for the elderly" (above-65 assisted-living arrangements), and the city denies a permit to a proposed facility after neighborhood meetings at which residents expressed concerns about increased emergency-services demand, reduced property values, and "changing the character of the neighborhood." **Why:** The hypo sits awkwardly between Cleburne (animus-driven permit denial struck down) and Murgia (rational age classification upheld). On the one hand, age is not a quasi-suspect class, so rational basis applies — and "increased emergency-services demand" for a senior facility is a facially plausible, non-stereotypical basis. On the other hand, "changing the character of the neighborhood" language and the pattern of treating elder facilities more stringently than fraternity houses or nursing homes (which are exempted) would look exactly like Cleburne's pretext pattern. The outcome turns on whether a reviewing court reads the record as tracking a genuine land-use concern or as a proxy for discomfort with aging residents. Some courts would treat Cleburne's bite as limited to the specific facts of mental disability; others would generalize it to any group-home zoning case with a thin evidentiary basis for the stated concern. There is no Supreme Court decision squarely on elder-facility zoning that settles the question.

## Critique

**Progressive critique (Marshall's honest accounting and the tiered-scrutiny lie):** The progressive critique is essentially Marshall's concurrence, elevated from a minority opinion to the governing reading of the case in subsequent scholarship. Gerald Gunther's earlier call for a "rational basis with bite" and Cass Sunstein's animus-principle framing both converge on Marshall's complaint: Cleburne is doctrinally honest only if the Court admits that rational basis is not a single standard but a graduated inquiry whose intensity varies with the classification's characteristics. The progressive critique treats the majority's refusal to acknowledge this as producing three bad effects. First, it misleads lower courts about how to handle future disability cases, which must apply "rational basis" without knowing whether the deferential economic-regulation version or the Cleburne-style searching version is intended. Second, it obscures the real grounds for the decision — the city's treatment of the group home was driven by community aversion, and the Palmore principle (private biases cannot be given legal effect) supplies the doctrinal lever — leaving later courts to rediscover those grounds case by case. Third, it marginalizes people with intellectual disabilities doctrinally even while vindicating them in the immediate case: by refusing quasi-suspect status, the Court leaves Garrett-style outcomes (Congress cannot use Section 5 to raise the floor) almost inevitable.

**Originalist/textualist critique (anti-textual tiering and judicial supremacism):** The originalist critique comes from a different angle but reaches an equally skeptical posture. The text of the Equal Protection Clause says nothing about tiers of scrutiny; "nor shall any State deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws" is a unitary command. The tiered-scrutiny framework is an entirely judge-made overlay of mid-twentieth-century origin (Justice Stone's Carolene Products footnote four being the canonical starting point). Cleburne's ad hoc intensification of rational basis — "bite" for some groups and not others, without any textual or historical guide to which groups get bite — is, on an originalist reading, an invitation to judicial policymaking under cover of doctrine. Scholars like Stephen Calabresi and John Harrison have argued that an originalist Equal Protection inquiry would ask whether the classification is genuinely arbitrary (discrimination in the classical sense) rather than policing legislative motives by tiered standards at all. On that reading, Cleburne's permit denial might still fail — because the city's shifting justifications suggest arbitrariness — but the animus principle would be a more structured inquiry tied to objective evidence of pretext, not a free-form sense that the Court dislikes the classification. The originalist also notes that rational-basis-with-bite is least defensible when the tier labels purport to cabin judicial discretion: the whole point of tiered scrutiny was to avoid the Lochner-era discretion, and Cleburne's covert heightening re-imports exactly that discretion under a different label.

**Doctrinal trajectory (the animus principle):** Cleburne's sub silentio animus principle — "mere negative attitude or fear" is not a legitimate state interest — becomes an explicit doctrine of major importance in the next decade. Romer v Evans (1996) cites Cleburne repeatedly and strikes down Colorado's Amendment 2 on the rationale that "a bare . . . desire to harm a politically unpopular group cannot constitute a legitimate governmental interest." United States v Windsor (2013) applies the animus principle to DOMA, and Obergefell v Hodges (2015) folds Windsor's reasoning into its Due Process analysis while quoting Cleburne. What began as a curious exception in a disability-zoning case has become one of the Equal Protection Clause's most important doctrinal tools for protecting groups who would not qualify for strict or intermediate scrutiny but whose treatment nonetheless bears the marks of invidious discrimination.

## Key Points

- **Classification**: mental retardation is not a quasi-suspect class; rational basis review applies
- **Rational basis with bite**: the court struck the ordinance under rational basis because the city's reasons were thinly veiled prejudice — "mere negative attitude or fear" is not a legitimate governmental interest
- **Animus principle**: legislation whose "animating force" is hostility toward a group fails rational basis even without heightened scrutiny (later extended in *Romer v. Evans*, *United States v. Windsor*, *Obergefell*)
- **Factors for quasi-suspect classification** (White majority): (1) discrete and insular minority; (2) history of discrimination; (3) immutable distinguishing characteristic; (4) political powerlessness — White finds the mentally retarded do not fully satisfy these
- **Marshall's sliding scale** (three-Justice concurrence): critiques the two-tier (plus intermediate) framework as obscuring the reality that the Court adjusts scrutiny based on importance of interests and degree of disadvantage; proposes an openly graduated approach
- **Significance for Section 5**: *Board of Trustees v. Garrett* (2001) applies Cleburne's rational basis standard to hold that Congress lacked sufficient evidence of irrational state disability discrimination to justify Title I of the ADA under Section 5
