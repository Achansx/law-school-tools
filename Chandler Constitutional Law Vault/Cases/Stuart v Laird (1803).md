# Stuart v Laird (1803)

## Memory Jogger

> Marbury's companion: Congress may abolish inferior courts and reassign their pending cases, and may require Supreme Court Justices to ride circuit -- settled not by textual argument but by the irresistible weight of early practice and acquiescence since the First Congress.

---

## Facts

The Judiciary Act of 1801, passed by the outgoing Adams administration, abolished circuit riding for Supreme Court Justices and created new circuit courts staffed by Federalist appointees. The incoming Jefferson administration viewed the Act as an attempt to entrench Federalist power in the judiciary. In 1802, the Republican-controlled Congress repealed the 1801 Act and restored the original circuit system under the Judiciary Act of 1789, requiring Supreme Court Justices once again to ride circuit as judges of inferior courts.

Charles Lee -- former Attorney General and John Marshall's brother-in-law -- challenged the constitutionality of this restoration in a debt case. He raised two distinct objections: (1) Congress lacked the power to transfer his pending case from the abolished circuit court to the reconstituted one, and (2) the Constitution did not permit Supreme Court Justices to sit as circuit judges, because their Article III commissions vested them with a distinct judicial office and requiring them to serve on a second, inferior court was unconstitutional.

Chief Justice Marshall recused himself because he had presided over the case in circuit court below. Justice Paterson delivered the unanimous opinion.

---

## Procedural History

The case originated as a debt action in the circuit court for the Virginia district. After Congress repealed the 1801 Act and reconstituted the circuit courts, the case was transferred to the new circuit court under the 1802 statute. Lee challenged both the transfer and the constitutionality of circuit riding. The circuit court ruled against him. The Supreme Court granted a writ of error.

## Holding

Congress may abolish inferior courts created under Article III and transfer their pending cases to reconstituted successor courts without violating the Constitution. Supreme Court Justices may constitutionally be required to ride circuit; the practice was commenced by the First Congress -- the same body that drafted and ratified the Constitution -- and has been acquiesced in for years, fixing the construction by contemporary interpretation of the most forcible nature.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Justice Paterson resolved both constitutional objections with deliberate brevity. On the transfer question, the Court held that because Congress created the inferior courts, it retains the power to alter or abolish them. There is no vested right in a particular forum, and reassigning a pending case to a successor court whose jurisdiction is coextensive with the abolished one does no harm to either party.

On the circuit-riding question, Paterson took a strikingly non-textual approach. Rather than engage with the structural argument that Supreme Court Justices hold a commission for a distinct office, the Court anchored its decision in historical practice. The First Judiciary Act of 1789 -- enacted by the very Congress that had recently drafted and ratified the Constitution -- required circuit riding. That practice had been followed, without serious constitutional challenge, from the founding of the judicial system. This "practice and acquiescence under it for a period of several years, commencing with the organization of the judicial system" provided what Paterson called "an irresistable answer" that had "fixed the construction." No further textual reasoning was offered or required.

The opinion is thus a foundational statement of the principle that early, consistent, and unchallenged governmental practice carries substantial weight as contemporaneous constitutional construction -- a method that differs meaningfully from both textualism and the natural-law reasoning Marshall deployed in Marbury.
