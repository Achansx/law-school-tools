# Students for Fair Admissions v Harvard (2023)

## Memory Jogger

> Race-conscious admissions programs at Harvard and UNC violate the Equal Protection Clause because they lack measurable objectives, use race as a negative, engage in racial stereotyping, and have no meaningful endpoint.

---

## Facts

Students for Fair Admissions (SFFA) is a nonprofit membership organization whose stated mission is to end race-based admissions. It brought two suits challenging race-conscious admissions policies: one against Harvard College (a private university covered by Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 because it receives federal funding) and one against the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill (a public university directly subject to the Fourteenth Amendment). Both schools used "holistic" review in which race was one factor among many. Harvard's process rated applicants on four profile dimensions (academic, extracurricular, athletic, personal) plus an overall rating; statistical evidence at trial showed Asian American applicants received systematically lower "personal" scores. UNC likewise considered race as a "plus factor" within individualized review.

SFFA argued that both programs violated the Equal Protection Clause (UNC) and Title VI (Harvard), and that Grutter v Bollinger (2003), which had sustained race-conscious admissions at the University of Michigan Law School under strict scrutiny, should be overruled or its 25-year durability caveat enforced. Harvard and UNC defended their programs on Grutter's compelling-interest-in-diversity grounds and argued they were narrowly tailored.

---

## Procedural History

After bench trials, the District Courts in both cases ruled for the universities. The First Circuit affirmed in Harvard's case. SFFA petitioned the Supreme Court before the Fourth Circuit ruled on the UNC appeal, and the Court granted certiorari before judgment in UNC. The two cases were briefed and argued jointly but decided in a single opinion. Justice Jackson recused from the Harvard case (she had served on the Harvard Board of Overseers) but participated in the UNC case.

The Supreme Court reversed as to both universities.

## Holding

The race-conscious admissions programs at Harvard and UNC violate the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment (and Title VI, whose standard the Court treated as coextensive). Any use of race in university admissions must survive strict scrutiny, which requires a sufficiently focused and measurable compelling interest and narrow tailoring. Harvard's and UNC's programs fail on multiple independent grounds: their stated interests are too amorphous to be measurable, they unavoidably use race in a negative manner given the zero-sum nature of competitive admissions, they rely on racial stereotyping, and they lack a logical endpoint. Universities remain free to consider an applicant's own discussion of how race affected that applicant's life, so long as such discussion is concretely tied to individual character or unique ability.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion (Roberts, C.J.)

Chief Justice Roberts framed the Equal Protection Clause's guarantee as universal in application: the clause must apply without regard to differences of race, color, or nationality, and any exception must survive the "daunting" strict-scrutiny inquiry. Applying Grutter's framework on its own terms, Roberts concluded that Harvard's and UNC's stated interests (training future leaders, preparing graduates, producing new knowledge, enhancing the robust exchange of ideas) were "commendable goals" but not "sufficiently coherent for purposes of strict scrutiny." The interests had no metrics to permit judicial evaluation of when narrow tailoring is satisfied or when the program has achieved its goal. On narrow tailoring, Roberts emphasized the zero-sum nature of competitive admissions: a preference for one applicant necessarily disadvantages another, so race operates as a negative factor even when labeled a "plus." The universities' racial categories themselves were over-broad and imprecise, running the risk of stereotyping by treating applicants as representatives of their racial group rather than as individuals. Finally, Grutter's envisioned 25-year sunset was not met by an "extension" of race-conscious admissions without a measurable endpoint. The Court formally preserved Grutter rather than explicitly overruling it, while in substance closing the door Grutter had left open.

### Concurrence (Thomas)

Justice Thomas wrote a lengthy concurrence offering what he described as an originalist defense of the colorblind Constitution, arguing the majority should have gone further and explicitly overruled Grutter v Bollinger and Powell's Bakke controlling opinion rather than leaving them nominally intact. Thomas's central historical claim is that the Fourteenth Amendment's Equal Protection Clause was designed to eradicate all race-based legal distinctions, not merely those that disadvantage racial minorities. Drawing on the debates surrounding the Civil Rights Act of 1866, the Freedmen's Bureau Act, and the amendment's ratification, Thomas argued that the Reconstruction framers understood equality to require the removal of all race-based classifications from law -- the colorblind view articulated by Justice Harlan's Plessy dissent. The antisubordination interpretation -- that the clause permits race-conscious remedies so long as they benefit rather than burden racial minorities -- lacks any basis in the amendment's original meaning, because the historical record shows the framers were committed to a formal equality that prohibited government from sorting citizens by race in either direction.

Thomas also pressed a structural critique of diversity rationales in higher education. Race-conscious admissions may harm minority students by placing them in academic environments where they are underprepared relative to their peers, reducing rather than enhancing educational outcomes. The university's premise that race itself confers an educationally valuable perspective that only members of a racial group can provide is a form of racial stereotyping that the Equal Protection Clause prohibits. Thomas concluded by expressing disagreement with the majority's carve-out for individual essays discussing how race has affected an applicant's life, which he viewed as an invitation to circumvent the holding through artful application design.

### Concurrence (Gorsuch, joined by Thomas)

Justice Gorsuch, joined by Thomas, wrote separately to emphasize that the Harvard case was more straightforwardly resolved on statutory grounds under Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 than on constitutional grounds. Title VI prohibits exclusion from or discrimination under any federally funded program "on the ground of race, color, or national origin." Gorsuch argued that this text is at least as demanding as the Equal Protection Clause, and potentially more so, because Congress drafted it without the doctrinal gloss of heightened-scrutiny frameworks that the Court has read into the Constitution. On his reading, the plain statutory text should have disposed of the Harvard case without requiring the Court to traverse Equal Protection doctrine at all. Gorsuch also used the concurrence to express skepticism about whether diversity in higher education qualifies as a compelling governmental interest of the kind that strict scrutiny demands, suggesting the majority was right to hold the programs failed but may have been too generous in accepting diversity as a potentially valid interest in principle.

### Concurrence (Kavanaugh)

Justice Kavanaugh concurred to emphasize what he viewed as the most important temporal feature of the case: Grutter v Bollinger (2003) itself had anticipated an end to race-conscious admissions. Justice O'Connor's majority opinion in Grutter noted that the Court "expect[s] that 25 years from now, the use of racial preferences will no longer be necessary to further the interest approved today." Kavanaugh read that passage not as dicta or aspiration but as a built-in doctrinal limiting principle: strict scrutiny requires a logical endpoint, and Grutter's 25-year expectation provided one. Twenty years had passed. Harvard and UNC had not demonstrated that they were moving meaningfully toward race-neutral alternatives or that their race-conscious programs had achieved the diversity goals that justified them in the first place. On Kavanaugh's account, SFFA was not primarily about overruling Grutter but about enforcing its own terms: a program that lacks a meaningful endpoint by definition fails narrow tailoring because it concedes no point at which the compelling interest will be satisfied.

### Dissent (Sotomayor, joined by Kagan, Jackson in UNC)

Justice Sotomayor, joined by Kagan and, in the UNC case, Jackson, wrote the principal dissent defending the race-conscious admissions programs as consistent with Grutter, correctly applied, and the Fourteenth Amendment, properly understood. On the constitutional question, Sotomayor argued that the Equal Protection Clause does not compel colorblindness; it requires equal treatment, which is a substantive rather than formal concept. The amendment was adopted in the aftermath of slavery precisely to authorize race-conscious remediation of racial inequality, and the majority's reading converts a remedial guarantee into a prohibition on the very tools needed to address entrenched inequality. Sotomayor invoked the antisubordination tradition -- that the clause permits, and may in some circumstances require, race-consciousness to remedy race-based disadvantage -- against the majority's colorblind formalism.

On the merits, Sotomayor argued the majority misapplied Grutter by demanding a level of measurability that Grutter itself never required and that no judicial framework had previously imposed on universities' academic judgments about the educational benefits of diversity. Harvard's and UNC's programs were narrowly tailored within the Grutter framework: they involved individualized review, treated race as one factor among many, and sought to achieve a critical mass of diverse students rather than fixed quotas. The district courts conducted extensive bench trials and made credibility determinations that the Supreme Court had no basis to second-guess on the narrow-tailoring question. Sotomayor warned that the majority's holding would produce measurable declines in Black and Hispanic enrollment at elite universities -- a result already demonstrated by the post-Prop 209 experience in California -- and would exacerbate rather than ameliorate entrenched racial inequality in American higher education.

### Dissent (Jackson, joined by Sotomayor, Kagan in UNC)

Justice Jackson, who was recused from the Harvard case due to her prior service on Harvard's Board of Overseers, dissented in the UNC case with an opinion that took a different approach from Sotomayor's. Where Sotomayor focused on defending the Grutter framework on its own terms, Jackson grounded her dissent in a sweeping account of racial inequality's structural persistence in American life. Her opening framing set the register: "Gulf-sized race-based gaps exist with respect to the health, wealth, and well-being of American citizens. They were created in the distant past, but have indisputably been passed down to the present day through the generations." Jackson argued that the majority's colorblind command is not a neutral position; it is a choice to ignore the present-day consequences of centuries of government-imposed racial hierarchy. Formal race-blindness in law does not dissolve race as a social fact. Deeming race irrelevant in law does not make it so in life. The majority's approach, on Jackson's account, requires courts and universities to pretend that race-linked opportunity gaps do not exist, ensuring they will persist.

Jackson also advanced a pointed critique of the majority's essay carve-out. The holding nominally permits universities to consider an applicant's discussion of how race affected her life, but the majority simultaneously bars universities from giving institutional weight to racial diversity as a goal. The carve-out is thus a false promise: an individual applicant's race-linked story can be credited only for what it reveals about personal character, not for what it contributes to institutional diversity. Jackson argued this distinction collapses in practice and that the majority's true position is that race may not meaningfully factor into admissions in any form -- a result the Constitution does not require and that the history of the Fourteenth Amendment does not support.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

1. A selective law school adopts a policy of using race as a "plus factor" in borderline cases -- the bottom fifteen percent of the admitted class by academic credentials -- to achieve a target of eighteen to twenty-two percent combined Black and Hispanic enrollment. The school argues the program is narrowly tailored because race is used only at the margin. This comes out the same way as SFFA. The Roberts majority identified three independent grounds for invalidating Harvard's and UNC's programs: (a) the objectives are not measurable enough to permit judicial review of when the interest is satisfied; (b) race necessarily operates as a negative in competitive zero-sum admissions; and (c) using race-as-race stereotypes. All three apply here. The eighteen-to-twenty-two percent enrollment target might look like a measurable objective, but it is closer to a quota than a compelling interest with a logical endpoint, which itself runs afoul of Bakke. And any race-plus system in competitive admissions involves race operating as a negative for non-benefited groups. The law school's program is invalidated under SFFA.

2. A state medical school reserves eight of its one hundred first-year seats for applicants from racial groups the school identifies as underrepresented in medicine. This is a direct quota system of the kind Allan Bakke challenged in 1978. Regents of the University of California v Bakke squarely prohibited numerical racial set-asides in admissions, and SFFA reinforces that holding. The reservation of specific seats for members of specified racial groups is unconstitutional without analysis: the Roberts majority expressly reaffirmed that universities may not "establish, through application of the Equal Protection Clause and Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, a quota system" or reserve slots "on the basis of race." This comes out the same way as SFFA, and the unconstitutionality is even clearer because it bypasses the holistic-review framework entirely.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

3. A flagship state university eliminates legacy and donor-preference boosts, significantly increases need-based financial aid, expands geographic recruitment into lower-income school districts, and weights being a first-generation college student heavily as a positive factor in admissions. The university publicly acknowledges that it expects these changes to increase Black and Hispanic enrollment as a result. SFFA does not prohibit this. Roberts's majority expressly stated that universities may consider socioeconomic disadvantage, first-generation status, geographic diversity, and obstacles overcome -- each evaluated at the individual level -- as part of holistic review. If a university designs its admissions criteria in race-neutral terms with the expectation that those criteria will increase racial diversity, that is precisely what SFFA invites. The only limit the majority imposes is that the application cannot reveal or consider the applicant's race directly; the race-neutral criteria and their anticipated demographic effects are permissible. This comes out differently from SFFA.

4. An applicant to a selective university writes an essay describing her experience as one of three Black students in her rural high school's advanced mathematics program, how racial isolation shaped her academic drive, and how becoming part of a diverse campus community has been central to her educational goals. The admissions office considers the essay as evidence of resilience, unusual perspective, and individual character. SFFA explicitly permits this. Roberts's majority carved out a safe harbor for exactly this scenario: "nothing prohibits universities from considering an applicant's discussion of how race affected the applicant's life, so long as that discussion is concretely tied to a quality of character or unique ability that the particular applicant can contribute to the university." The university is crediting the individual's personal experience and what it demonstrates about her character, not treating her race as an institutional diversity metric. This comes out differently from SFFA.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

5. A university's admissions office does not ask applicants to identify their race. Instead, it uses an algorithm that weights zip code, high school racial composition (based on census tract data), whether the primary language spoken at home is other than English, and participation in historically Black colleges' or tribal colleges' dual-enrollment programs. The algorithm was explicitly designed and calibrated using regression analysis to predict race and to achieve the racial distribution that the university's prior race-conscious program achieved. No applicant ever discloses race directly. Roberts's majority warned that "what cannot be done directly cannot be done indirectly. The Constitution deals with substance, not shadows." If a proxy system is demonstrably designed to function as a racial classification and is calibrated against racial outcomes, it raises serious questions under SFFA even though no race box is checked. At the same time, the majority simultaneously invited universities to consider the very demographic factors (zip code, socioeconomic status, first-generation status) that are racially correlated. The line between a permissible race-neutral factor that correlates with race and an impermissible racial proxy designed to replicate a racial program is genuinely unclear. Courts will have to assess intent, calibration method, and outcome, and the doctrinal test for distinguishing them has not yet been developed. Genuinely unclear.

## Critique

Colorblind-constitutionalism defense: The majority's strongest intellectual grounding comes from Thomas's concurrence rather than Roberts's opinion. The Fourteenth Amendment's text -- "equal protection of the laws" -- is phrased in formally neutral terms. The original understanding, traced through the Civil Rights Act of 1866, the congressional debates over the Freedmen's Bureau Acts, and Justice Harlan's Plessy dissent, plausibly supports the view that the amendment was designed to eliminate all government racial classifications, including those intended to benefit minority groups. On this reading, Grutter v Bollinger was wrongly decided from the start, because diversity in higher education is not a compelling governmental interest of constitutional stature; it is an academic preference that universities have styled as a constitutional right. SFFA restored the colorblind command that Grutter improperly suspended for 25 years and -- on Thomas's account -- should have been stated even more clearly.

Diversity-rationale critique: The most serious defense of SFFA from a pragmatic standpoint is that race-conscious admissions programs, whatever their intentions, have not produced the structural educational benefits their proponents claim. Fifty years of affirmative action in elite higher education have not closed the racial achievement gaps that motivated the programs; the pathways into selective universities begin with K-12 educational inequality that race-conscious admissions at the top cannot remedy. If anything, admissions preferences may mask the underlying problem by routing it into college while leaving primary and secondary school inequality unaddressed. This is the core of Sotomayor's own admission that the programs have not produced measurably equal outcomes -- which cuts both ways.

Individual-essay-exception critique: The most debated doctrinal question SFFA leaves open is whether Roberts's carve-out for race-linked personal essays will swallow the holding. The majority says universities cannot treat race as an institutional diversity metric but can credit what an applicant's race-linked experience reveals about individual character. Critics from both directions find this untenable. Thomas and Gorsuch think any consideration of race-linked experience injects race into admissions through the back door. Jackson argues the carve-out is a "false promise" that cannot coherently be operationalized -- how does an admissions officer credit the resilience demonstrated by racial isolation without also implicitly crediting the diversity the applicant's presence would bring? The essay exception may produce years of litigation over where individual-character consideration ends and institutional race-consciousness begins.

Implications beyond higher education: The doctrinal scope of SFFA is formally limited to university admissions, and the Court explicitly reserved the military-academy question. But the opinion's broad language -- "eliminating racial discrimination means eliminating all of it" -- raises questions for race-conscious programs in employment, federal contracting (Adarand Constructors), legislative redistricting, and minority business enterprise programs. Lower courts are already working through whether SFFA's reasoning extends beyond the admissions context, and the result may be a significant contraction of race-conscious governmental programs across domains that SFFA did not directly address.

## Key Points

- Effectively ends race-conscious admissions in its traditional form while nominally preserving Grutter v Bollinger (2003).
- Treats the Equal Protection Clause and Title VI as imposing coextensive requirements, so the holding binds both public and private universities receiving federal funding.
- Distinguishes race-conscious admissions from consideration of an individual applicant's discussion of how race affected her life, creating an "individual character" safe harbor.
- Sidesteps military-academy admissions (explicitly left unresolved) and does not address race-neutral alternatives designed to produce racial diversity.
- Resolves the associational-standing question for SFFA in a way that may facilitate similar membership-organization litigation going forward.

- Regents of University of California v Bakke, Grutter, Gratz, Fisher I, Fisher II together constitute the affirmative action admissions doctrine that SFFA displaces, reducing the line to Bakke's anti-quota holding and the individual-essay carve-out.
