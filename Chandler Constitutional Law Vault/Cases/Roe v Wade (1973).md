# Roe v Wade (1973)

## Memory Jogger

> Texas's near-total criminal abortion ban struck down by a 7-2 Court as a violation of the constitutional right to privacy, with the Court establishing a trimester framework balancing the woman's liberty interest against the state's interests in maternal health and potential life — overruled by Dobbs v Jackson Women's Health Organization (2022).

---

## Facts

Norma McCorvey, using the pseudonym "Jane Roe," was an unmarried woman residing in Texas who sought an abortion in 1969 but could not obtain one under Texas law. Texas Penal Code Articles 1191–1194 and 1196 prohibited all abortions except those "procured or attempted by medical advice for the purpose of saving the life of the mother." Roe filed a lawsuit against Henry Wade, the District Attorney of Dallas County, arguing the statute violated her constitutional rights.

The case was brought as a class action on behalf of herself and all other women in the same situation. The Dallas attorneys who agreed to litigate the case framed it as a constitutional challenge. The Supreme Court ultimately consolidated it with *Doe v Bolton* (1973), a companion case challenging Georgia's more permissive but still restrictive abortion law.

---

## Procedural History

A three-judge federal district court in the Northern District of Texas ruled that the Texas statute was unconstitutionally vague and violated Roe's Ninth Amendment rights as applied through the Fourteenth Amendment, but declined to issue an injunction against enforcement. Both parties appealed directly to the Supreme Court. The case was first argued in December 1971, then reargued in October 1972 after the addition of Justices Powell and Rehnquist. The case was decided January 22, 1973. The Court addressed the mootness problem — Roe was no longer pregnant — by invoking the "capable of repetition, yet evading review" exception.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority:** Blackmun (joined by Burger, Douglas, Brennan, Stewart, Marshall, Powell — 7 justices)
- **Dissent:** White (alone) — argued the Court had no constitutional authority to make the value judgment that abortion should be permitted and characterized the decision as "an exercise of raw judicial power"
- **Dissent:** Rehnquist (alone) — argued the right to privacy derived from Griswold did not extend to abortion, and that the majority's balancing test would have struck down most of the 36 abortion laws in existence at the time of the Fourteenth Amendment's ratification

---

## Holding

The constitutional right of privacy — whether founded in the Fourteenth Amendment's concept of personal liberty or in the Ninth Amendment's reservation of rights to the people — "is broad enough to encompass a woman's decision whether or not to terminate her pregnancy." The right is not absolute, however, and must be balanced against two important state interests that become compelling at different stages: (1) the interest in maternal health, which becomes compelling approximately at the end of the first trimester; and (2) the interest in protecting potential human life, which becomes compelling at viability (approximately the end of the second trimester). The trimester framework: (a) first trimester — abortion decision left to the woman and her physician, state may not interfere; (b) second trimester — state may regulate in ways reasonably related to maternal health; (c) post-viability — state may proscribe abortion except where necessary to preserve the life or health of the mother.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Justice Blackmun's majority opinion worked through extensive historical analysis of abortion law — including ancient Greek and Roman practice, English common law, the American Medical Association's history, and state legislative developments — to conclude that the Constitution's protection of privacy, recognized in *Griswold v Connecticut* (1965) and its predecessors, extended to the abortion decision. The Court located this privacy right in the Fourteenth Amendment's liberty concept (while noting it could also be grounded in the Ninth Amendment). But Blackmun departed from a simple application of strict scrutiny: rather than asking whether the state had a compelling interest in banning all abortions, he devised a trimester framework that calibrated the woman's liberty interest against two state interests that "grow in substantiality" as pregnancy progresses. In the first trimester, the state's interests are not yet compelling enough to override the woman's right; after the first trimester, maternal health regulation is permissible; after viability (when the fetus can survive outside the womb with artificial support), the state may proscribe abortion. The majority also held that a fetus is not a "person" within the meaning of the Fourteenth Amendment, a textual anchor for the conclusion that fetal life does not receive constitutional protection independent of the woman's rights.

## Key Points

- Overruled by *Dobbs v Jackson Women's Health Organization* (2022), which held the Constitution does not confer a right to abortion and returned the issue to the states
- Prior to Dobbs, *Planned Parenthood v Casey* (1992) had already replaced the trimester framework with the "undue burden" standard while nominally reaffirming Roe's "central holding"
- The "capable of repetition, yet evading review" mootness exception was established in Roe and applies to all pregnancy-related litigation
- Blackmun's physician-centered framing (first trimester decision "left to the medical judgment of the pregnant woman's attending physician") was criticized as insufficiently grounded in women's autonomy; Casey corrected this to focus directly on the woman's right
- The fetus-is-not-a-person holding (Fourteenth Amendment "person" does not include the unborn) was not repudiated in Dobbs — Dobbs simply held that the woman's liberty interest does not rise to the level of a constitutionally protected right
