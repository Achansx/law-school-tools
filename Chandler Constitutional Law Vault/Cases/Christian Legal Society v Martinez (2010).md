# Christian Legal Society v Martinez (2010)

## Memory Jogger

> A public law school's all-comers policy -- requiring registered student organizations to open membership and leadership to all enrolled students -- is a reasonable, viewpoint-neutral condition on access to a limited public forum; it does not violate free speech, expressive association, or free exercise.

---

## Facts

Hastings College of the Law, a public law school in the University of California system, established a Registered Student Organization (RSO) program through which it provided official recognition to student groups, along with access to school funds, bulletin boards, meeting rooms, and an annual student organizations fair. To qualify as an RSO, a student group had to agree to abide by Hastings' nondiscrimination policy, which included a rule requiring that student organizations accept any student who wished to participate, become a member, or seek leadership positions -- without regard to status or belief. This was Hastings' "all-comers" policy.

The Christian Legal Society (CLS) sought RSO status but conditioned its membership and leadership on agreement with a statement of faith and a prohibition on "unrepentant homosexual conduct." Hastings offered CLS access to facilities on the same terms as any other non-RSO student group (i.e., without official recognition or funding), but declined to extend RSO status because CLS's exclusionary membership policy violated the all-comers rule. CLS sued under 42 U.S.C. § 1983, claiming violations of its rights to free speech, expressive association, and free exercise of religion.

---

## Procedural History

The federal district court granted summary judgment to Hastings. The Ninth Circuit affirmed. The Supreme Court granted certiorari and affirmed (5-4).

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority:** Justice Ginsburg (joined by Stevens, Kennedy, Breyer, Sotomayor)
- **Concurrence:** Justice Stevens (separate; emphasizing that expressive association claims do not trump nondiscrimination conditions in government programs)
- **Dissent:** Justice Alito (joined by Roberts, Scalia, Thomas; arguing the all-comers policy was applied as a pretext for viewpoint discrimination against CLS's religious beliefs)

---

## Holding

A public university's all-comers policy -- requiring all RSOs to accept any student as a member or officer -- is a reasonable and viewpoint-neutral condition on access to a limited public forum. A student organization that refuses to comply with the policy is not constitutionally entitled to official recognition or its attendant benefits. The all-comers requirement does not discriminate on the basis of viewpoint because it applies equally to all groups regardless of their message or mission; CLS is not denied recognition because of what it believes, but because of what it requires of its members. The Free Exercise Clause does not mandate an exemption from a neutral, generally applicable policy under Employment Division v. Smith.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Justice Ginsburg's majority opinion analyzes the case through the limited public forum framework. When the government opens a forum for expressive activity but limits it to certain speakers or topics, the First Amendment permits reasonable, viewpoint-neutral restrictions on access. Ginsburg holds that Hastings' all-comers policy satisfies both prongs: it is reasonable -- because a school may legitimately want to ensure open access to student programming and avoid the appearance of university endorsement of exclusion -- and it is viewpoint-neutral, because it applies to all groups without regard to the content of their message. The key move is the distinction between restricting speech based on viewpoint (impermissible) and restricting membership practices that happen to correlate with a group's viewpoint (permissible when the rule applies universally). Ginsburg also applies Employment Division v. Smith to the free exercise claim: because the all-comers policy is a neutral, generally applicable rule, the Free Exercise Clause does not require an exemption.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

1. **Hypo:** A public university RSO program applies an all-comers policy uniformly; a conservative political student group, because it requires officers to be registered Republicans, is denied official recognition. The group claims First Amendment protection. **Why:** Martinez holds that a universally applied all-comers policy is a permissible, viewpoint-neutral condition on RSO access; the same rule applies to ideological groups as to religious ones.

2. **Hypo:** A public university requires all RSOs to agree not to discriminate on the basis of sexual orientation; a student religious group that teaches homosexuality is sinful declines and seeks RSO status anyway. **Why:** Martinez establishes that the university's nondiscrimination policy, applied evenhandedly, is a valid condition on RSO participation; the group's religious beliefs are respected but do not entitle it to government benefits without compliance.

3. **Hypo:** A public university grants RSO recognition to virtually all student groups but denies it specifically to groups espousing a particular religious viewpoint, without applying an all-comers policy to any group. **Why:** Martinez depends on the viewpoint-neutrality of the all-comers rule; selectively denying recognition based on viewpoint is a straightforward First Amendment violation under Rosenberger v. 4. **Hypo:** A private university (not a state actor) applies a similar all-comers policy against a religious student group. **Why:** The First and Fourteenth Amendments apply only to state action; a private university is not a government actor, and its RSO policies do not raise constitutional concerns regardless of their content.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

5. **Hypo:** A public university adopts an all-comers policy but explicitly grants exemptions for "ideologically coherent" groups whose membership restriction is central to their expressive mission -- and then denies CLS an exemption on the ground that its religious beliefs do not constitute a sufficiently coherent ideology. **Why:** Martinez's reasoning depends entirely on the uniformity of the all-comers policy; an exemption regime that involves individualized government determinations about the ideological legitimacy of a group's exclusionary practices would trigger Fulton-style analysis, and the outcome is genuinely uncertain.

## Key Points

- Holds that a public university's RSO program is a limited public forum; the government may impose reasonable, viewpoint-neutral conditions on access without violating the First Amendment.
- The all-comers policy is viewpoint-neutral because it applies to all student organizations regardless of their beliefs; CLS is not penalized for what it believes, but for what it requires of members.
- Free exercise claim fails under Employment Division v. Smith: the all-comers policy is neutral and generally applicable, so no compelling interest is required.
- Alito's dissent raises a meaningful factual dispute about selective enforcement, suggesting the policy may have been a pretext for targeting religious groups.
- Tension with Fulton v. City of Philadelphia (2021): Fulton held that an exemption-laden policy is not neutral for Smith purposes; if Martinez's all-comers policy contained exemptions, the free exercise analysis may have come out differently.
