# Florida Prepaid Postsecondary Education Expense Board v College Savings Bank (1999)

## Memory Jogger

> Congress cannot abrogate state sovereign immunity for patent infringement under Section 5 when the legislative record shows no documented pattern of unconstitutional state conduct — a thin record produces a disproportionate remedy.

---

## Facts

College Savings Bank, a New Jersey institution, held a patent on a method for administering college savings plans. Florida Prepaid Postsecondary Education Expense Board, an arm of the State of Florida, marketed a college tuition prepayment program that College Savings Bank alleged infringed its patent. Because Florida Prepaid was a state entity, College Savings Bank could not ordinarily sue it for patent infringement in federal court due to the Eleventh Amendment bar on suits against states by private parties.

Congress had enacted the Patent Remedy Act (PRA) in 1992 specifically to abrogate state sovereign immunity in patent infringement suits, purporting to act under its Article I patent power and its Section 5 enforcement power under the Fourteenth Amendment. The PRA allowed patent holders to sue states in federal court and recover money damages. Congress's stated rationale was that state patent infringement deprived patent holders of property without due process of law, invoking the Fourteenth Amendment's protection of property rights.

---

## Procedural History

College Savings Bank sued Florida Prepaid in the District of New Jersey. Florida Prepaid moved to dismiss on Eleventh Amendment grounds. The district court denied the motion, holding the PRA validly abrogated Florida's immunity. The Third Circuit affirmed. Florida Prepaid petitioned for certiorari. The Supreme Court granted cert and decided the case together with a companion case, College Savings Bank v Florida Prepaid (No. 98-149), which addressed the trademark immunity question separately.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority:** Rehnquist (joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas)
- **Dissent:** Stevens (joined by Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer)

---

## Holding

The Patent Remedy Act does not validly abrogate state sovereign immunity under Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment. Congress lacks power under Section 5 to subject states to patent infringement suits for money damages because the legislative record fails to document a pattern of unconstitutional (procedurally deficient) state patent infringement, and the Act's nationwide abrogation is therefore not congruent and proportional to any demonstrated constitutional violation.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Chief Justice Rehnquist applied the City of Boerne congruence-and-proportionality framework. The threshold question was whether state patent infringement actually violates the Fourteenth Amendment. The majority concluded that states generally do not violate due process merely by infringing patents: if a state infringes a patent, it typically exposes itself to a takings claim for just compensation, satisfying due process requirements. For a constitutional violation to occur, a state would need to deny an adequate remedy — for example, by invoking sovereign immunity in its own courts and leaving the patent holder with no recourse at all.

Measured against this narrower constitutional target, the PRA's legislative record was thin. Congress had assembled only a handful of instances of state patent infringement, almost none of which demonstrated that states had actually denied constitutionally adequate process. The Act nonetheless abrogated immunity for all patent suits against any state, with no requirement that the plaintiff demonstrate inadequate state-court remedies. This "sword" was far too broad for the constitutional "shield" Congress had identified. The remedy was out of proportion to the problem.
