# Buck v Bell (1927)

## Memory Jogger

> "Three generations of imbeciles are enough." -- Holmes extends Jacobson's compulsory vaccination rationale to compulsory sterilization of the "unfit," upholding Virginia's eugenic sterilization statute with procedural safeguards; the decision has never been formally overruled but is universally condemned as one of the Court's worst opinions.

---

## Facts

Carrie Buck was a seventeen-year-old Virginia woman committed to the State Colony for Epileptics and Feeble Minded. State officials sought to sterilize her under the Virginia Eugenical Sterilization Act of 1924, which authorized the sterilization of institutionalized individuals deemed "feeble-minded," epileptic, or insane if the procedure was in the patient's and society's best interest. The Act provided for a formal hearing before the colony's board, with notice to the patient, and a right of appeal to the circuit court and then to the Virginia Supreme Court of Appeals.

Carrie Buck's mother, Emma Buck, was also a patient at the colony and had been classified as feeble-minded. Carrie's infant daughter Vivian was said by state witnesses to show signs of mental deficiency as well -- the "three generations" of Holmes's famous phrase. Modern historical research has established that Carrie Buck was not intellectually disabled but was instead a rape victim whose institutionalization was engineered to conceal a family scandal.

---

## Procedural History

The colony's superintendent sought a sterilization order following the statutory process. After the colony board approved, Carrie Buck's court-appointed guardian challenged the statute in the Circuit Court of Amherst County, which upheld it. The Virginia Supreme Court of Appeals affirmed. The Supreme Court granted certiorari on the constitutional question.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority:** Holmes (joined by Taft, Van Devanter, McReynolds, Brandeis, Sutherland, Sanford, and Stone -- 8-1)
- **Dissent:** Butler (no written opinion)

---

## Holding

The Virginia Eugenical Sterilization Act does not violate the Due Process or Equal Protection Clauses of the Fourteenth Amendment. A state may compulsorily sterilize institutionalized individuals deemed hereditarily unfit if the statute provides adequate procedural safeguards. The police power extends to protection of the state's welfare from the hereditary transmission of disability, and the burden on the individual is outweighed by the state's interest in preventing defectives from "swamping" future generations.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Justice Holmes's opinion is notable for its brevity and its rhetorical compression of contested scientific claims into axiomatic premises. Holmes invoked Jacobson v Massachusetts as authority that compulsory vaccination is constitutional, and reasoned by analogy that "the principle that sustains compulsory vaccination is broad enough to cover cutting the Fallopian tubes." If the state may require individuals to sacrifice a small risk of harm from vaccination for the public good, it may require a larger sacrifice -- sterilization -- for a proportionally greater benefit.

Holmes then performed the three-generations move: Carrie Buck's mother was institutionalized, Carrie herself was institutionalized, and Carrie's daughter showed apparent deficiency. From this Holmes concluded that "three generations of imbeciles are enough." The opinion did not seriously scrutinize the eugenic science underlying the statute; Holmes treated hereditary transmission of feeble-mindedness as established fact.

On equal protection, Holmes dismissed the argument that the statute singled out institutionalized persons: those are the individuals the state can reach, and the legislature may take problems one step at a time.

Professor Chandler uses Buck v Bell to illustrate two points. First, it shows Jacobson's dangerous malleability: a precedent about a five-dollar vaccination fine was stretched to authorize surgical sterilization of thousands. Second, it shows that procedural due process can be a facade -- Carrie Buck's guardian had a conflict of interest and made no meaningful challenge; the "hearing" was theater. The lesson is that formal procedural compliance cannot substitute for substantive constitutional limits.

## Critique

Holmes's single analogical sentence from [[Cases/Jacobson v Massachusetts (1905)|Jacobson]] does almost all the doctrinal work in Buck, and that sentence collapses differences in degree so large that they become differences in kind. Harlan's Jacobson opinion rested on a specific smallpox outbreak, a reversible five-dollar fine, and an express carve-out for cases that would be "cruel and inhuman in the last degree." Holmes silently abandons each of those qualifications while announcing that the principle is "broad enough" to cover sterilization. The table below maps the axes on which the analogy breaks down.

### Comparison: Jacobson's Actual Holding and Buck's Extension of It

| Axis | [[Cases/Jacobson v Massachusetts (1905)\|Jacobson (1905)]] | Buck v Bell (1927) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nature of state intrusion | Compulsory vaccination or a five-dollar fine; the vaccine is reversible and produces immunity as a direct self-benefit | Surgical severing of the Fallopian tubes; irreversible sterilization that confers no self-benefit on the subject |
| Condition triggering the power | A documented smallpox outbreak with an approximately 30 percent case-fatality rate; immediate and measurable public-health emergency | A contested eugenic theory of heritable feeble-mindedness, later shown to be pseudoscience |
| Alternative open to the subject | Pay the fine, or contest the statute in state court; no loss of bodily integrity | No alternative; the statute contemplates the procedure itself as the remedy |
| Reversibility | The vaccination itself is non-reversible but its effect is protective rather than destructive; the fine is trivially reversible | Completely irreversible; no person so treated can reproduce |
| Statutory carve-out | Harlan wrote that an as-applied challenge would lie if the statute were applied to a person for whom vaccination would be "cruel and inhuman in the last degree" | Holmes neither invokes nor mentions the Jacobson carve-out; the statute's procedural safeguards are treated as self-sufficient |
| Scientific evidentiary basis | Accepted medical consensus on smallpox transmission and vaccine efficacy in 1905 | Laughlin's Model Eugenical Sterilization Act science was already contested in 1927 and is now known to have been fraudulent; Carrie Buck was not intellectually disabled |
| Deference owed | Harlan's baseline deference subject to "real or substantial relation" and "plain, palpable invasion" review | Holmes applies deference without ever seriously testing the state's empirical premises |
| Who bears the cost | The vaccinated person bears a small risk for a large personal benefit and a public benefit | The sterilized person bears a total and permanent loss for no personal benefit and a speculative public benefit |

The analogy fails not because the same premise yields a different conclusion but because at least four of Jacobson's load-bearing premises (emergency, reversibility, self-benefit, cruel-and-inhuman carve-out) are absent from Buck. Once those are subtracted, what remains of Holmes's opinion is the bare claim that the state's general police power authorizes compulsory bodily alterations of the "unfit." That is not an application of Jacobson; it is a new principle that merely cites Jacobson.

[[Cases/Skinner v Oklahoma (1942)|Skinner v Oklahoma (1942)]] is the doctrinal check, not the doctrinal cure. Skinner applied strict scrutiny to a statute authorizing sterilization of habitual criminals because procreation is a "basic civil right," but the Douglas majority framed the analysis in equal-protection rather than due-process terms and pointedly did not reach Buck's constitutional premise. That narrowing choice is why Buck has never been formally overruled despite near-universal moral condemnation: no litigant has ever brought a parallel fact pattern under the later-developed fundamental-rights or bodily-integrity frameworks in a posture that would require the Supreme Court to confront the earlier 8-1 judgment. Modern historiography, principally Paul Lombardo's archival work, has established that Carrie Buck was a rape victim whose institutionalization was engineered to conceal a family scandal, that her daughter Vivian was likely of normal intelligence, and that the "adversarial" hearing was collusive. The lesson the lecture draws from Buck is that procedural due process can be window-dressing, and that judicial deference to legislative findings cannot substitute for substantive constitutional limits on what the state may do to the human body.

## Key Points

- Holmes extends Jacobson's police power rationale from minor vaccination fine to compulsory sterilization -- a logical leap most later scholars find illegitimate.
- The opinion is formally good law: the Supreme Court has never overruled it, though Skinner v Oklahoma (1942) applied strict scrutiny to a different sterilization statute without reaching Buck.
- Butler's lone dissent without opinion remains one of constitutional law's great mysteries -- almost certainly grounded in natural law or religious conviction rather than doctrinal reasoning.
- Modern historiography (Paul Lombardo) has established that Carrie Buck was not feeble-minded; the eugenic science underlying the statute was fraudulent; her "hearing" was rigged.
- Professor Chandler pairs Buck with Jacobson to show how police power precedent can be distorted beyond recognition by a single analogical step.
- Skinner v Oklahoma (1942) is the practical check: sterilization now triggers strict scrutiny as a fundamental right, rendering Buck's reasoning effectively obsolete without formal overruling.

## Connections

- [[Cases/Jacobson v Massachusetts (1905)|Jacobson v Massachusetts (1905)]] — the precedent Holmes misuses. Buck extends Jacobson's "real and substantial relation" public-health rationale from a reversible five-dollar vaccination fine to irreversible surgical sterilization, a single analogical step that later scholars uniformly describe as illegitimate. Jacobson rested on a specific outbreak, reversible intrusion, and a cruel-and-inhuman-application carve-out that Buck silently abandons; pairing the two cases shows how a narrowly-framed holding can be abstracted into a general police-power license for coercive bodily intervention.
- [[Topics/Substantive Due Process|Substantive Due Process]] — Buck is the deepest trough in the early SDP record and the strongest case for the Thomas/Scalia institutional critique that SDP in any form is a vehicle for the Court to strike down (or here, uphold) laws it likes; the decision's formal survival despite universal moral condemnation illustrates the stare-decisis stickiness that complicates doctrinal repair.
- [[Lectures/Early Substantive Due Process|Lectures/Early Substantive Due Process]] — the lecture treats Buck as the lowest point in early SDP, reproducing Holmes's opinion at length and asking directly what is wrong with it; pairs Buck with Jacobson to show how police-power deference can be weaponized.

- [[Cases/Pierce v Society of Sisters (1925)|Pierce v Society of Sisters (1925)]] — decided two years before Buck and illustrating the era's doctrinal incoherence: the same Court that had just protected parental authority against compulsory public schooling in Pierce authorized compulsory sterilization of an institutionalized woman here, showing that the Lochner-era "liberty" vocabulary was deployed inconsistently depending on who was asserting the claim.
