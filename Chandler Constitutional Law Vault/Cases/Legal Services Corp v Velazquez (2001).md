# Legal Services Corp v Velazquez (2001)

## Memory Jogger

> A funding restriction prohibiting LSC-funded attorneys from challenging the validity of existing welfare law is unconstitutional viewpoint-based discrimination; unlike *Rust*, the government here is distorting a private advocacy forum — the judicial system — not conveying its own message.

---

## Facts

Congress established the Legal Services Corporation (LSC) to provide civil legal assistance to the poor. Under the LSC Act, recipients of LSC funds may represent clients in civil matters but are prohibited by a funding condition from using those funds to represent clients who wish to challenge existing welfare law. The restriction was added as an appropriations rider: LSC-funded attorneys could help welfare recipients obtain benefits but could not argue that applicable law was invalid or should be changed. Respondents — a group of welfare recipients, LSC attorneys, and a state bar association — challenged the restriction as an unconstitutional restriction on speech.

---

## Procedural History

The district court upheld the restriction on the authority of *Rust v. Sullivan* (1991). The Second Circuit reversed, distinguishing *Rust* and holding that the restriction imposed viewpoint-based conditions on expression in the courts. The Supreme Court granted certiorari and affirmed the Second Circuit 5-4.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority (5-4):** Kennedy, J. (Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer, JJ., joined)
- **Dissent:** Scalia, J. (Rehnquist, C.J., O'Connor, Thomas, JJ., joined)

---

## Holding

The LSC funding restriction is an unconstitutional viewpoint-based restriction on speech. Congress may not use the funding mechanism to distort the legal system by silencing one side of an otherwise existing private discourse. Because LSC subsidizes private speech in the judicial forum — where courts depend on adversarial presentation — the government-speech rationale of *Rust* does not apply. Excluding welfare-law challenges creates a funded viewpoint favoring existing law and suppresses the opposing viewpoint, distorting the usual functioning of the legal marketplace.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion (Kennedy, J.)

Kennedy distinguishes *Rust* on two grounds. First, in *Rust* the government was funding its own message — a family-planning program specifically designed to promote childbirth. The government was the speaker, merely enlisting private contractors to deliver its message, and could therefore define the message's content. In *Velazquez*, by contrast, the government is subsidizing private attorneys who represent private clients in private litigation. The government is not the speaker; it is funding a private advocacy system. Second, and critically, the forum in which the speech occurs matters: the courts depend on the presentation of adverse legal arguments for the proper exercise of judicial power. By prohibiting LSC attorneys from advancing certain legal theories, the restriction does not merely decline to subsidize a viewpoint — it actually prevents courts from hearing legal arguments they would otherwise receive, distorting the judicial system itself. Kennedy emphasizes that the government has no legitimate interest in insulating existing welfare law from judicial challenge by preventing lawyers from raising the issue in court. The restriction therefore is not a permissible program definition but rather a viewpoint-based limitation that corrupts the very institution the subsidy serves.

## Key Points

- *Velazquez* marks the doctrinal limit of *Rust*: the government-speech rationale for selective funding does not extend to subsidies of private speakers engaged in independent advocacy before the courts.
- The key distinguishing principle: in *Rust*, the government was the speaker delivering its own message; in *Velazquez*, the government was subsidizing private speech in a forum (the courts) that depends on adversarial presentation.
- The "judicial forum distortion" rationale is potentially narrow — the Court stresses the courts' special dependence on adversarial argument, suggesting the rule may not extend to every government-funded private speech program.
- *Velazquez* is routinely paired with *Rust* in First Amendment courses as the two poles of the government-speech/spending-conditions spectrum.
- The case is cited in *Agency for International Development v. Alliance for Open Society International* (2013), where similar anti-prostitution pledge conditions on foreign aid were struck down as viewpoint-based.
