# Civil Rights Cases (1883)

## Memory Jogger

> The Court holds the Civil Rights Act of 1875 unconstitutional, ruling that the Fourteenth Amendment reaches only state action (not private conduct) and that discrimination in public accommodations is not a badge of slavery under the Thirteenth Amendment.

---

## Facts

Congress passed the Civil Rights Act of 1875 under the Reconstruction Amendments. Sections 1 and 2 of the Act prohibited private individuals and businesses from denying equal access to inns, public conveyances on land or water, theaters, and other places of public amusement to any person on account of race or color. The Act made violations a criminal offense and created a civil cause of action.

The cases consolidated under this decision involved five separate prosecutions and civil suits arising from denial of equal accommodations in hotels, theaters, and railroads across multiple states. The defendants in each case were private actors, not state officers or municipalities. No state law had been invoked to justify the exclusion; the denial was purely private commercial conduct.

The constitutional question was whether Congress could reach private racial discrimination in public accommodations under its enforcement powers in Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment or Section 2 of the Thirteenth Amendment.

---

## Procedural History

The cases were consolidated from several circuit and district courts and came to the Supreme Court on certificates of division in criminal prosecutions and on direct appeal in civil actions. The United States government defended the Act's constitutionality. The Court treated the five cases together because they all raised the identical constitutional question: whether Congress had authority to prohibit private racial discrimination in public accommodations.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority:** Bradley (joined by Waite, Miller, Field, Woods, Matthews, Gray, Blatchford)
- **Dissent:** Harlan

---

## Holding

Sections 1 and 2 of the Civil Rights Act of 1875 are unconstitutional and void as applied to the several states. The Fourteenth Amendment prohibits only state action, not private discrimination, and Congress's Section 5 power is limited to enacting corrective legislation directed at prohibited state action. The Thirteenth Amendment's enforcement clause does authorize primary and direct federal legislation to abolish badges and incidents of slavery, but private discrimination in public accommodations is not a badge of slavery within the meaning of that amendment.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Justice Bradley's majority opinion turned on two independent grounds, one for each Reconstruction Amendment. Under the Fourteenth Amendment, the text's prohibition runs to "no State," and Section 5's enforcement power therefore extends only to correcting discriminatory state laws and state action, not to creating a general federal code of civil conduct governing private individuals. Until some state law or state action injures federal rights, Section 5 legislation has no predicate; Bradley called this the requirement of "corrective legislation." The Act's broad prohibition on private commercial discrimination had no state-action predicate and therefore exceeded Congress's power.

On the Thirteenth Amendment, Bradley acknowledged that Section 2 authorizes primary and direct federal legislation to abolish all badges and incidents of slavery, not merely state laws upholding it. However, he reasoned that denial of access to a public accommodation was a social injury, not a vestige of the slave system. Once a person has emerged from slavery and enjoys formal legal freedom, further legal protections against social discrimination are matters for state law, not federal enforcement of the abolition amendment.

## Key Points

- Establishes the state-action requirement for Fourteenth Amendment enforcement: Congress under Section 5 may only correct discriminatory state laws or state acts, not regulate purely private conduct.
- Limits Thirteenth Amendment enforcement to abolition of genuine badges and incidents of slavery; holds public-accommodation exclusion to be a social wrong, not a vestige of the slave system.
- Harlan's dissent anticipates the Jones v Alfred H Mayer Co holding by nearly a century and provides the framework for the "badges and incidents" revival.
- Professor Chandler emphasizes this case as the doctrinal origin of the state-action doctrine and the starting point for the enforcement-powers sequence.
