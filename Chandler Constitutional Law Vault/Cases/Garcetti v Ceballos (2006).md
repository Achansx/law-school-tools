# Garcetti v Ceballos (2006)

## Memory Jogger

> A public employee who speaks pursuant to official job duties -- not as a citizen -- receives no First Amendment protection against employer retaliation, regardless of whether the speech addresses matters of public concern.

---

## Facts

Richard Ceballos was a calendar deputy in the Los Angeles County District Attorney's Office, responsible for reviewing cases for possible prosecution. In February 2000, a criminal defense attorney brought to Ceballos' attention a case involving a search warrant that, the defense counsel believed, relied on inaccuracies in the supporting affidavit. Ceballos investigated, concluded that the affidavit contained serious misrepresentations, and prepared a disposition memorandum recommending that the prosecution be dismissed.

His supervisor did not accept the recommendation. A meeting was convened; supervisors decided to proceed with the prosecution notwithstanding the memo. The meeting allegedly became heated. Following this disagreement, Ceballos claimed he suffered adverse employment actions: reassignment from calendar duties to a different unit, transfer to a courthouse in a distant location, and denial of a promotion. He filed suit under 42 U.S.C. § 1983, alleging First Amendment retaliation for the protected speech contained in his memorandum.

---

## Procedural History

The district court granted summary judgment to the defendants. The Ninth Circuit reversed, holding that Ceballos' memorandum addressed matters of significant public concern and that, under the Pickering balancing test, his First Amendment interests outweighed the government's interests as an employer. The Supreme Court granted certiorari and reversed (5-4).

## Holding

When public employees make statements pursuant to their official duties, the employees are not speaking as citizens for First Amendment purposes, and the Constitution does not insulate their communications from employer discipline. The controlling question is not whether the speech addresses matters of public concern, but whether the speech was made in the employee's capacity as a citizen or in the employee's capacity as an employee performing official job responsibilities. Because Ceballos wrote the disposition memorandum in the course of performing his duties as a calendar deputy -- investigating and advising on pending prosecutions -- the memo was official speech, not citizen speech, and the First Amendment does not apply.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Justice Kennedy's opinion begins with the Pickering-Connick framework: for public employees who speak as citizens on matters of public concern, the First Amendment protects them from employer retaliation, subject to a balancing test weighing the employee's interest against the government's interest in efficient public services. Garcetti adds a threshold step: before applying Pickering-Connick, a court must determine whether the employee spoke as a citizen at all. If the speech was made pursuant to official duties -- if it "owes its existence to a public employee's professional responsibilities" -- then the speaker was not acting as a citizen, and the First Amendment simply does not apply. The fact that the speech addresses a matter of public concern (police misconduct, warrant fraud) is irrelevant once the court determines the speech was made in an official capacity. Kennedy acknowledges the difficult policy consequences -- this rule may discourage internal whistleblowing -- but concludes that the Constitution does not resolve the workplace dynamic; statutory schemes (like civil service protections) are the appropriate vehicle for protecting employee disclosures made in the course of official duties.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

1. **Hypo:** A public school teacher is required by school policy to submit monthly reports on student disciplinary incidents to the principal. The teacher's reports include observations that a school administrator is improperly covering up incidents. The principal retaliates by assigning the teacher to a less desirable classroom. **Why:** The reports were a required professional duty; the speech owed its existence to the teacher's professional responsibilities, not to any choice to speak as a citizen; Garcetti bars the First Amendment claim.

2. **Hypo:** A government scientist at the EPA is directed to write a technical assessment of proposed regulations; her assessment concludes the regulations are scientifically inadequate. She is demoted. **Why:** The assessment was prepared pursuant to her job duties; even if it addresses matters of profound public importance, Garcetti categorically denies First Amendment protection to such official-capacity communications.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

3. **Hypo:** The same deputy DA writes a letter to the local newspaper -- outside work hours, without using office resources -- describing the same warrant fraud he discovered in his official investigation. The DA fires him. **Why:** The letter is citizen speech on a matter of public concern: it was written outside the scope of official duties, in the employee's own voice as a private citizen. Garcetti's rule is limited to speech made pursuant to official duties; Pickering-Connick balancing applies to the letter, and the employee's interest likely prevails.
<!-- ENRICH: Apply Pickering balancing: the government's interest in loyalty and efficiency is significant for a line prosecutor, but the public interest in exposure of prosecutorial misconduct is also substantial; analyze how the balance tips. -->

4. **Hypo:** A government employee testifies truthfully under oath in a criminal trial about corruption she observed at her agency. She is fired for the testimony. **Why:** Lane v. Franks (2014) held that sworn testimony outside the ordinary scope of one's job duties is protected citizen speech; even if the testimony concerns the same subject matter as her daily work, the formal act of testifying is not itself an official employment duty.
<!-- ENRICH: Discuss how Lane refines Garcetti and develops the distinction between the subject matter of official duties and the modality of the speech act. -->

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

5. **Hypo:** A public defender discovers during case preparation that a prosecutor has committed Brady violations (withholding exculpatory evidence), reports the violation to the state bar as required by her professional ethics rules, and is terminated for doing so. **Why:** Breyer's dissent in Garcetti specifically flagged lawyer ethics obligations as a case where professional duty and official duty overlap in ways the Court's categorical rule does not handle well; the public defender's report to the bar was arguably both a professional duty (ethics rules) and an official act (part of her case representation) -- and Garcetti leaves this space under-theorized.

## Key Points

- Adds a threshold "citizen or employee" inquiry before the Pickering-Connick balancing test applies; if the speech was pursuant to official duties, there is no balancing -- no First Amendment protection at all.
- The "official duties" inquiry is "a practical one"; formal job descriptions are neither necessary nor sufficient to show the scope of professional duties.
- Lane v. Franks (2014) later held that sworn testimony outside the ordinary scope of job duties is protected citizen speech, even if the testimony concerns subjects encountered at work.
- The Court expressly reserved the question of whether Garcetti applies to academic scholarship and classroom instruction by public university professors.
- With 4,328 citing cases, Garcetti is one of the most-cited First Amendment decisions; it is frequently litigated at the employment level.
