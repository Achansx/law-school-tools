# Calder v Bull (1798)

## Memory Jogger

> The Ex Post Facto Clause applies only to criminal and penal statutes, not civil laws, and the case launched the foundational debate between Chase's natural law limits on legislatures and Iredell's positive-law positivism.

---

## Facts

The Connecticut legislature passed a resolution setting aside a probate court decree and granting a new probate hearing. Caleb Bull and wife had obtained the original decree refusing to approve a will by Calder; the new hearing reversed that outcome, approving the will and awarding the contested property to another party. The Bulls challenged the legislative act as an unconstitutional ex post facto law under Article I, Section 10 of the Constitution, which prohibits states from passing "any bill of attainder, ex post facto law, or law impairing the obligation of contracts."

The central question was whether "ex post facto law" encompassed retrospective civil legislation affecting property rights, or only criminal and penal statutes.

---

## Procedural History

The Connecticut probate court initially refused to approve the contested will in 1793, which operated as a final judgment under state law. The state legislature set aside that decree by resolution in 1795 and granted a new hearing. On rehearing, the probate court approved the will. The Connecticut Supreme Court affirmed. The case reached the U.S. Supreme Court on writ of error, presenting the federal constitutional question of whether the legislative act was an ex post facto law.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority (seriatim):** Chase, Paterson, Iredell, and Cushing each wrote separate opinions; all four concurred in affirming the Connecticut judgment.
- **Concurrence (Chase):** Recognized that certain vital principles in republican government may override legislative acts even absent specific constitutional text, gesturing at natural law limits.
- **Concurrence (Iredell):** Expressly rejected Chase's natural law approach; argued courts may only invalidate statutes for violation of a specific written constitutional provision, not abstract natural justice.

---

## Holding

The Ex Post Facto Clause of Article I, Section 10 applies only to criminal and penal statutes. A retrospective civil law, such as a legislative act granting a new probate hearing, does not violate the clause. The Connecticut resolution was constitutional.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Justice Chase's seriatim opinion identified four categories of ex post facto laws: (1) making criminal an act innocent when done; (2) aggravating or making greater a crime committed before the law; (3) inflicting greater punishment than the law prescribed when committed; and (4) altering the rules of evidence to convict with less or different testimony. All four are criminal in character. Chase reasoned that because the Constitution separately specifies "law impairing the obligation of contracts," the Framers could not have intended ex post facto to encompass civil retroactive legislation, or the contracts clause would be redundant. Chase also suggested, in widely debated dicta, that legislatures may be limited by principles of natural justice even without specific constitutional text, but declined to apply such limits here because the legislative act fell within legitimate power.

Justice Iredell's concurrence directly countered Chase: if a legislature acts within the general scope of its constitutional power, courts cannot strike the law down merely because it conflicts with abstract natural justice. Natural justice is regulated by no fixed standard, and courts must point to a specific constitutional prohibition to invalidate legislation.

## Key Quotations

> "If, on the other hand, the Legislature of the Union, or the Legislature of any member of the Union, shall pass a law, within the general scope of their constitutional power, the Court cannot pronounce it to be void, merely because it is, in their judgment, contrary to the principles of natural justice. The ideas of natural justice are regulated by no fixed standard: the ablest and the purest men have differed upon the subject; and all that the Court could properly say, in such an event, would be, that the Legislature, (possessed of an equal right of opinion,) had passed an act which, in the opinion of the judges, was inconsistent with the abstract principles of natural justice." (Justice Iredell, concurring)

## Key Points

- Defines ex post facto narrowly to criminal/penal statutes only -- the most tested doctrinal point from this case.
- The Chase-Iredell debate is the founding text of the natural-law vs. positive-law argument that resurfaces in Lochner, Griswold, and Dobbs.
- Chase's four categories remain the canonical test for what constitutes a criminal ex post facto law.
- Iredell's positivism anticipates the Glucksberg methodology: rights require grounding in text or tradition, not abstract justice.
- The redundancy argument (ex post facto vs. Contracts Clause) demonstrates the Court using structural/textual inference to define constitutional terms.
