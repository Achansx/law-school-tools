# Agency for International Development v Alliance for Open Society International (2013)

## Memory Jogger

> Congress conditioned HIV/AIDS funding on grantees having an explicit policy opposing prostitution; the Court struck down the pledge requirement because while government can restrict how its money is spent within a program, it cannot use funding conditions to compel recipients to espouse the government's ideological position outside the program's scope -- that crosses from program-definition to unconstitutional leverage.

---

## Facts

The United States Leadership Against HIV/AIDS, Tuberculosis, and Malaria Act of 2003 (the Leadership Act) authorized billions of dollars to fund global efforts to combat HIV/AIDS and related diseases. Section 7631(f) of the Act required that organizations receiving Leadership Act funds have "a policy explicitly opposing prostitution and sex trafficking" -- known as the "Policy Requirement" or the anti-prostitution pledge. A separate provision, § 7631(e), prohibited the use of Leadership Act funds to "promote or advocate the legalization or practice of prostitution or sex trafficking" -- the "Program Requirement."

The Alliance for Open Society International and other NGOs received Leadership Act funding. They challenged only the Policy Requirement (§ 7631(f)), arguing that it compelled them to adopt and publicly affirm a specific ideological position as a condition of receiving federal funds in violation of the First Amendment. The NGOs argued that the requirement interfered with their ability to reach sex workers, who might be deterred from seeking HIV services if providers were required to condemn their profession.

---

## Procedural History

The District Court for the Southern District of New York granted a preliminary injunction against enforcement of the Policy Requirement. The Second Circuit affirmed. The Supreme Court granted certiorari and affirmed, holding the Policy Requirement unconstitutional.

## Holding

The Policy Requirement of the Leadership Act, which conditions federal HIV/AIDS funding on recipients having an explicit policy opposing prostitution, violates the First Amendment. The government may limit how federal funds are spent -- restricting organizations from using the money to promote prostitution -- but it may not use funding as leverage to compel recipients to adopt and publicly espouse an ideological position as their own organizational policy. That condition reaches outside the funded program to regulate the recipient's speech and beliefs, and it does so in a manner that cannot be confined to the use of government funds.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Chief Justice Roberts framed the case around the distinction the Court has drawn between two types of funding conditions. Some conditions define what the government is buying: they specify how funds may be spent and what activities the program subsidizes. These are permissible because the government is simply choosing what to fund. Other conditions leverage the grant to regulate speech outside the program -- they reach beyond the funded activities to shape the recipient's independent expression and identity. These are unconstitutional under the unconstitutional conditions doctrine, which holds that the government may not deny a benefit on a basis that infringes constitutionally protected freedom of speech.

Roberts explained that the Policy Requirement fell into the second category. The program itself was funding anti-HIV activities; the prohibition on using funds to promote prostitution (§ 7631(e)) was a legitimate program-defining condition. But requiring the organization to affirmatively adopt an anti-prostitution policy as its own -- to espouse the government's view as an organizational creed -- was something different. It compelled a statement of belief that could not be cabined to the funded activities. An organization that opposes the pledge as a matter of conscience, or that believes its public health work requires treating sex workers without moral judgment, was forced to choose between its beliefs and its funding.

Roberts rejected the government's argument that affiliated organizations could express divergent views, finding that option either meaningless (if the affiliate is truly separate, the recipient cannot express anything) or hypocritical (if the affiliate is closely identified with the recipient, the recipient's "endorsement" of the policy is evident sham).

## Key Points

- AOSI is the leading case articulating the **program-defining vs. speech-leveraging** distinction in unconstitutional conditions doctrine: the government may specify what it funds (permissible) but may not use funding to compel recipients to adopt and publicly espouse a belief as their own (unconstitutional).
- The decision distinguishes **Rust v. Sullivan** (1991), which upheld a condition barring use of Title X funds for abortion counseling. AOSI holds the key difference is that the Policy Requirement extended to the organization's entire identity and all activities, not just the funded program.
- The **unconstitutional conditions doctrine** operates even when the recipient has no entitlement to the benefit: the government cannot attach conditions to a voluntary grant that would abridge the recipient's constitutional rights.
- Roberts rejected the government's **affiliate workaround**: if the affiliate is truly distinct, the recipient cannot express divergent views through it; if the affiliate is closely identified with the recipient, the "independent" expression is hollow.
- AOSI is significant for **NGOs, nonprofits, and advocacy organizations** that receive federal grants: conditions that require them to adopt official stances on contested ideological issues are presumptively unconstitutional.
