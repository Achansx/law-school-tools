# Van Orden v Perry (2005)

## Memory Jogger

> A Ten Commandments monument had stood on the Texas state capitol grounds for 40 years among 17 other monuments and 21 historical markers; the Court upheld it 5-4 because, on Breyer's controlling concurrence, the monument's longstanding, passive, civic context reflected acknowledgment of religious heritage rather than an establishment of religion -- the key fifth vote turned on context and history, not any unified doctrinal test.

---

## Facts

A six-foot granite monument inscribed with the Ten Commandments stood on the grounds of the Texas State Capitol in Austin. The monument was donated in 1961 by the Fraternal Order of Eagles and accepted by the state. It was situated among 17 other monuments and 21 historical markers commemorating the "people, ideals, and events that compose Texan identity." The text on the monument consisted of the Ten Commandments framed with religious symbols, including two Stars of David and the Chi Rho symbol of Christ.

Thomas Van Orden, a homeless man who had previously practiced law, spent much of his time in the park-like setting of the capitol grounds. He brought suit against Governor Rick Perry and other officials, asserting that the monument violated the Establishment Clause of the First Amendment.

---

## Procedural History

The District Court for the Western District of Texas upheld the monument, applying the Lemon test and finding that it had a secular purpose, did not have the primary effect of advancing religion, and did not create excessive entanglement between government and religion. The Fifth Circuit affirmed. Van Orden petitioned for certiorari. The Supreme Court granted certiorari and affirmed in a fractured 5-4 decision with no majority opinion.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Plurality (4):** Rehnquist, C.J. (author), joined by Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas — uphold monument; Lemon inappropriate for passive displays
- **Concurrence (plurality):** Scalia — joins the plurality and writes separately to endorse a "historical acknowledgment" approach
- **Concurrence (plurality):** Thomas — joins the plurality and writes separately urging that the Establishment Clause should not be incorporated against the states, and that the clause in any event reaches only legal coercion, not passive displays
- **Concurrence in Judgment (5th vote, controlling):** Breyer — uphold monument; declines to apply Lemon or any single test; relies on 40-year history, civic donor, setting among other monuments, and passive character
- **Dissent:** Stevens (joined by Ginsburg)
- **Dissent:** O'Connor (in an opinion concurring in part and dissenting in part in McCreary County, which the parties and commentators read as her Van Orden dissent)
- **Dissent:** Souter (joined by Stevens and Ginsburg)

---

## Holding

The Ten Commandments monument on the Texas state capitol grounds does not violate the Establishment Clause. Because no majority opinion commanded five votes, the holding is controlled by Justice Breyer's concurrence in the judgment. Breyer concluded that the monument's 40-year history without controversy, its physical setting among other civic monuments, and its character as a passive acknowledgment of the religious heritage underlying American law placed it on the constitutional side of the Establishment Clause line. Removing such longstanding, passive monuments would project government hostility to religion rather than neutrality.

---

## Analysis

### Plurality Opinion (Rehnquist)

Chief Justice Rehnquist argued that the Lemon test is an inappropriate framework for evaluating passive monuments and longstanding government acknowledgments of religion. Drawing on the concept of "tolerable acknowledgment of beliefs widely held among the people of this country," Rehnquist traced a tradition of government recognition of religious texts and figures -- from the Declaration of Independence's invocation of the Creator to the Court's own chamber friezes depicting Moses. The Ten Commandments have a dual significance: they are foundational religious text for millions of Americans, but they are also a recognized code of law that has influenced Western legal tradition. The monument's presence on state grounds alongside secular monuments reflects this dual character.

### Breyer Concurrence (Controlling)

Justice Breyer's concurrence controlled the outcome but defied easy doctrinal categorization. He explicitly declined to apply any single test, instead asking whether the monument, in its specific context, had the kind of predominantly religious purpose or effect that the Establishment Clause prohibits. He identified several features weighing in favor of constitutionality: (1) the monument had stood for 40 years, suggesting it had not provoked a sense of government endorsement of religion; (2) it was donated by a civic organization (Fraternal Order of Eagles) to emphasize a civic rather than purely religious message; (3) it was one of 17 monuments in a park-like setting celebrating Texas history; and (4) it was a passive display -- a monument to be walked past, not a prayer or instruction to believe. Against this backdrop, Breyer concluded that upholding the monument was less likely to promote conflict than striking it down, which would require identifying and removing longstanding religious acknowledgments throughout civic life.

### McCreary County Contrast (Same-Day Companion)

Van Orden is impossible to read without its same-day companion, McCreary County v ACLU of Kentucky, 545 U.S. 844 (2005). Souter wrote for a five-Justice majority in McCreary (joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg, and Breyer) striking down county-courthouse displays of the Ten Commandments. The same nine Justices heard both cases and the same Justice — Breyer — cast the decisive vote for opposite results. The difference lies in the factors Breyer found dispositive in Van Orden and absent in McCreary: the Texas monument had stood 40 years without provoking suit and had been placed among 16 other secular monuments; the Kentucky courthouse displays were newly erected, repeatedly modified in reaction to litigation, and accompanied by legislative resolutions explicitly endorsing their religious purpose. The Lemon "purpose prong" and its endorsement-test cousin drove the result in McCreary; Breyer's holistic contextual analysis drove the result in Van Orden. Read together, the two decisions announce that Establishment Clause analysis of religious displays depends on totality-of-circumstances facts — longevity, provenance, surrounding context, purpose statements — rather than on any doctrinal test capable of ex ante application.

### Stevens Dissent

Stevens, joined by Ginsburg, argued that the majority's "acknowledgment" framing misdescribes what the Ten Commandments are. The first four commandments are explicitly religious directives (no other gods, no graven images, sabbath observance, taking the name of the Lord in vain). The government's adoption of a six-foot monument bearing them "sends the message that the State endorses the divine code" — the exact message the Establishment Clause's neutrality principle forbids. Stevens's methodological move is to reject the passive/active distinction as irrelevant: neutrality is violated by endorsement of a particular religious message, full stop, and the government cannot cure that violation by pointing to the length of time the endorsement has been in place.

### Souter Dissent

Souter, joined by Stevens and Ginsburg, emphasized that Breyer's contextual analysis, while internally coherent, is unadministrable in lower courts and encourages a post-hoc rationalization of whatever result a court finds culturally comfortable. He argued the monument is a straightforward religious text and that the Establishment Clause requires the government either to acknowledge all religions equally or to refrain from acknowledging any — the Texas display does neither.

### Thomas Concurrence

Thomas's concurrence, though joining the plurality on the outcome, argued two broader points that would transform the case if they commanded a majority: (1) the Establishment Clause as incorporated against the states is a category mistake because the Clause was originally a federalism provision protecting state religious establishments against federal interference, and (2) even if the Clause applies to the states, its prohibition reaches only legal coercion (forcing religious observance by law) and not mere acknowledgment. Under Thomas's view, the Van Orden question would be easy in the State's favor without resort to any balancing at all.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

1. A 50-year-old nativity scene donated by a local civic association occupies one small portion of a large public park that also contains statues of civic figures, a monument to fallen soldiers, and a seasonal menorah. A new suit challenges the nativity. **Why:** All four Breyer factors are present — longevity, civic donor, surrounding secular and non-Christian religious displays, passive character. Lynch v Donnelly (1984) already upheld a context-rich nativity, and Van Orden's reasoning extends the same contextual analysis to a one-religion display that is physically and symbolically situated among non-religious and non-Christian counterparts. The case is on-all-fours.

2. A stone monument inscribed with excerpts from the Declaration of Independence — including the Creator reference in the preamble — is donated by a veterans' organization and placed among 20 other civic monuments at a state capitol grounds. A taxpayer sues alleging Establishment Clause violation. **Why:** The Declaration's Creator reference is the foundational civic-religious text of the American state, and the monument's placement among other civic markers makes the "tolerable acknowledgment" analysis straightforward. Breyer's Van Orden framework controls, and the historical-acknowledgment line of Marsh v Chambers (1983) reinforces the result.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

3. A state legislature in 2026 passes a statute mandating new Ten Commandments displays in every county courthouse, accompanied by a legislative preamble stating that "the Ten Commandments are the law of God and the foundation of our civil law." **Why:** This is McCreary County as it would return today, not Van Orden. The explicit religious purpose statement, the courthouse setting, and the newly-erected character all point the Breyer vote the other way. American Legion v American Humanist Ass'n (2019) further refined the rule to create a presumption of constitutionality for "longstanding" monuments, but that presumption is rebutted by clear evidence of a religious purpose and does not attach to new erections.

4. A state places a single Ten Commandments monument, newly donated, at the entrance of a Department of Motor Vehicles office with no other monuments, signage, or contextual framing. **Why:** None of Breyer's four factors is present. The monument is new, not donated in a civic-organization setting that would diversify its message, has no contextualizing companions, and is placed at a government service entrance where the endorsement inference is direct. Under Breyer's contextual approach, this case tips to the Stevens-Souter side.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

5. A state removes a 40-year-old Ten Commandments monument in response to litigation, enters into a stipulated judgment on narrow grounds, and then — after the judgment — the legislature re-erects the same monument in the same location with a new legislative finding that it is being preserved "for historical purposes." **Why:** The historical continuity Breyer treated as central was that the monument had stood unchallenged for 40 years; the fence-sitter tests whether removing it and re-erecting it breaks that continuity. One reading: the original 40 years of acceptance cannot be erased by a brief gap, and the monument's longstanding prior presence remains the dispositive fact. The opposite reading: Breyer's concern was that removal would signal hostility to religion, but a monument that was in fact removed in response to litigation and then re-erected with a new religious-purpose statement more closely resembles McCreary's pattern of reactive re-enactment than Van Orden's passive continuity. The case is a genuine coin-flip; no subsequent decision resolves it cleanly, though American Legion's presumption of constitutionality for aged monuments likely tips the balance toward preservation if the monument is old enough.

## Critique

### Progressive / Strict-Neutrality Angle

The strongest progressive critique is that Breyer's controlling concurrence sells out the Establishment Clause's neutrality principle in exchange for a totality-of-circumstances test that cannot be administered without judicial thumbs on the scale. If the government's 40-year acquiescence can itself cleanse an otherwise-religious display, then Establishment Clause protection atrophies over time: any display that survives the first decade of challenge gains constitutional cover simply by persisting. Stevens's dissent makes this structural point pointedly: the passive/active distinction is not in the Establishment Clause's text, and the length-of-time factor substitutes historical momentum for principled review. Douglas Laycock and others have argued that the Van Orden/McCreary pair effectively makes Establishment Clause analysis of passive displays unreviewable, because any circuit panel can pick factors to reach either result. This angle is reinforced by the fact that the religious message on the monument is unambiguous: the first four Commandments are theological directives addressed to Jews and Christians, not civic-history markers.

### Originalist / Historical-Acknowledgment Angle

The opposing originalist position, articulated by Scalia's concurrence and reinforced by American Legion v American Humanist Ass'n (2019), is that the Establishment Clause was never understood to forbid passive acknowledgments of the religious heritage that the framing generation self-consciously built into American civic life — from the House and Senate chaplains to the "In God We Trust" motto to the oath of office. On this view, the Lemon "purpose prong" was a mid-twentieth-century invention that generates absurd results when applied to monuments the founding era would have taken for granted. Thomas's concurrence pushes the argument further, suggesting the Establishment Clause is a federalism rule that never should have been incorporated against the states in the first place. The originalist angle concedes that Breyer's "factors" test is imprecise but denies that imprecision is the real problem; the real problem, on this view, is the mid-twentieth-century strict-neutrality experiment the Clause was never meant to conduct.

### Doctrinal Architecture Critique

Whatever one's view of the result, Van Orden does a well-documented harm to lower-court administration. After 2005, Circuit panels divided over whether to apply Lemon/endorsement or Breyer's factors test, producing a doctrinal mess that the Court did not partially resolve until American Legion (2019), which pulled in the direction of a presumption of constitutionality for "longstanding" monuments. Even after American Legion, Kennedy v Bremerton School District (2022) announced that the Lemon test is effectively discarded in favor of an "historical practices and understandings" inquiry. Van Orden is the case at the center of that transition: it was the first post-Lemon decision where a majority-equivalent of Justices openly declined to apply the test, and the subsequent decade of lower-court confusion is a case study in the cost of deciding hard constitutional questions through 5-4 fractured results with no majority opinion.

## Key Points

- Van Orden's **holding** -- if it can be called one -- is controlled by **Breyer's concurrence**: passive, historically situated, contextually embedded religious symbols do not necessarily violate the Establishment Clause.
- The decision resists reduction to a general rule. Breyer explicitly rejected applying the **Lemon test** or any other single framework, opting for a contextual balancing approach unique to the facts.
- **McCreary County v. ACLU** (2005), decided the same day, struck down Ten Commandments courthouse displays in Kentucky. The difference turned on context and history: the courthouse displays were newly erected in explicitly religious circumstances; the Texas monument had stood for 40 years in a civic secular setting.
- The **Rehnquist plurality** argued for a "historical acknowledgment" approach that would permit longstanding government recognitions of religious heritage without requiring strict neutrality, but this view commanded only four votes.
- Van Orden is a leading example of the **fractured Court** problem in Establishment Clause jurisprudence: because no majority agreed on a test, the decision offers limited precedential guidance beyond its specific facts.

This table tracks how each case in the lineage handled the three-prong Lemon test (purpose, primary effect, excessive entanglement) and what supplementary or replacement framework it deployed. | [[Cases/Van Orden v Perry (2005)\|Van Orden v Perry (2005)]] (Breyer controlling concurrence)           | No                  | "Legal judgment" totality-of-circumstances (1 Justice) | Texas monument upheld on 40-year undisturbed-history grounds |

| American Legion v American Humanist Assn (2019) (not yet briefed)                                    | No (plurality)      | "Presumption of constitutionality" for longstanding monuments (Alito plurality) | Bladensburg Peace Cross upheld |

The Van Orden row is doctrinally pivotal: it is the first case in the sequence where a Justice with a controlling vote (Breyer) openly declined to apply Lemon, but it does so without a majority of the Court agreeing on what should replace Lemon. American Legion's plurality narrows that gap; Kennedy v Bremerton closes it by replacing Lemon's three prongs with a single history-and-tradition inquiry as the controlling Establishment-Clause framework.
