# Taylor v United States (2016)

## Memory Jogger

> Robbing or attempting to rob a drug dealer of drugs or drug proceeds automatically satisfies the Hobbs Act's commerce element, because the national drug market is interstate commerce under the Raich aggregation principle.

---

## Facts

David Anthony Taylor was a member of a gang in Roanoke, Virginia, that targeted drug dealers for home-invasion robberies. On two occasions he and his associates broke into the Roanoke homes of suspected marijuana dealers. The first robbery netted only small sums of cash, a cell phone, and some jewelry; the second produced nothing because the dealer was not home and the house was empty. Taylor was charged under the Hobbs Act (18 U.S.C. section 1951), which criminalizes robbery or extortion that "obstructs, delays, or affects commerce or the movement of any article or commodity in commerce."

At trial the Government offered evidence that drug dealing is an inherently interstate economic activity, but did not attempt to prove that the particular drugs or proceeds Taylor targeted had themselves traveled in interstate commerce or were destined to do so. The District Court excluded Taylor's proffered evidence that the targeted dealers dealt only in locally grown marijuana, ruling that it was irrelevant to the commerce element.

---

## Procedural History

The jury convicted Taylor on two Hobbs Act counts at a second trial (the first ended in a mistrial). The Fourth Circuit affirmed, holding that the Government satisfies the Hobbs Act's commerce element when it proves the defendant robbed or attempted to rob a drug dealer of drugs or drug proceeds, without needing to prove an individualized interstate nexus. The Supreme Court granted certiorari to resolve a circuit disagreement about the quantum of Commerce Clause proof required under the Hobbs Act in drug-dealer-robbery prosecutions.

The Court affirmed Taylor's convictions.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority:** Alito (joined by Roberts, C.J., Kennedy, Ginsburg, Breyer, Sotomayor, Kagan) — 7-1
- **Dissent:** Thomas

## Holding

In a Hobbs Act prosecution for robbery or attempted robbery of a drug dealer, the Government satisfies the statutory commerce element simply by proving that the defendant knowingly stole or attempted to steal drugs or drug proceeds. The Government need not prove that the specific drugs at issue traveled or were destined to travel across state lines. Because Congress has the constitutional power under the Commerce Clause to regulate the national market for illegal drugs (including purely intrastate production, possession, and sale) based on aggregate effects, Gonzales v Raich controls, and any individual robbery of a drug dealer falls within the regulated class of activities.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion (Alito)

Justice Alito treated the case as controlled by Gonzales v Raich (2005). The Commerce Clause authorizes Congress to regulate the national market for illegal drugs, including purely local production and possession, because in the aggregate such local activity substantially affects interstate commerce. Because robbery of drug dealers is economic activity directed at that regulated class, Congress may proscribe it without any further showing of an individualized interstate nexus. Alito distinguished the Hobbs Act's general commerce language from more restrictive statutory commerce elements; the Act reaches "all commerce over which the United States has jurisdiction," language the Court has long read as extending to the full reach of Congress's Commerce Clause power. Proof beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant targeted drugs or drug proceeds is therefore sufficient, and evidence that the targeted dealer operated in a purely local market is legally irrelevant. Alito emphasized that this ruling is confined to robberies of drug dealers and does not resolve the commerce-element analysis for robberies of other businesses.

## Key Points

- Directly applies Gonzales v Raich (2005) to a new statutory context, confirming that the aggregation principle has force outside the Controlled Substances Act itself.
- Largely moots the Lopez/Morrison framework for Hobbs Act prosecutions by eliminating the need for a case-specific commerce showing when the target is a drug dealer.
- Confines its holding to the drug-market context; does not resolve analogous questions for robberies of banks, retailers, or other business types.
- Thomas's solo dissent signals continuing friction on the Court over the reach of Wickard-Raich aggregation theory.
- Exemplifies the doctrinal bridge between criminal jurisdiction and Commerce Clause theory in contemporary practice.
