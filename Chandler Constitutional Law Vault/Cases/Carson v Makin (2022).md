# Carson v Makin (2022)

## Memory Jogger

> Maine's tuition assistance program paid private school tuition for children in rural districts without public secondary schools -- but only at "nonsectarian" schools; the Court struck down the exclusion because once a state decides to fund private education it cannot withhold those funds solely because a school is religious, and the attempt to distinguish religious "status" from religious "use" was rejected.

---

## Facts

Maine has 260 school administrative units (SAUs) but many are too small and rural to operate secondary schools. Under Maine's tuition assistance program, when a SAU does not operate a public secondary school, it must pay tuition for resident students to attend either a public school in another district or an approved private school. Since 1981, Maine has required that participating private schools be "nonsectarian," effectively barring any school that integrates religious instruction or has a sectarian character. The program did not require that schools be non-religious in their administration but focused on their educational mission.

David and Amy Carson, and Alan and Judy Gillis, wanted to use the tuition assistance payments at Bangor Christian Schools and Temple Academy respectively -- two private schools with explicitly religious missions that integrate faith throughout their curricula. Both schools were excluded under the nonsectarian requirement. The plaintiffs challenged the exclusion as a violation of the Free Exercise Clause of the First Amendment.

---

## Procedural History

The District Court for the District of Maine granted summary judgment for the state. The First Circuit affirmed, applying the Supreme Court's then-recent decision in Espinoza v. Montana Department of Revenue (2020) but distinguishing between exclusions based on a school's religious "status" (prohibited) and exclusions based on religious "use" of funds (permissible). The Supreme Court granted certiorari and reversed.

## Holding

Maine's "nonsectarian" requirement for otherwise generally available tuition assistance payments violates the Free Exercise Clause of the First Amendment. A State may not exclude religious organizations from a generally available public benefit program solely on the basis of their religious character. The First Circuit's distinction between status-based discrimination (barring schools because they are religious) and use-based discrimination (barring schools because they would use the funds for religious purposes) was rejected: the prohibition on status-based discrimination is not a permission to engage in use-based discrimination.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Chief Justice Roberts traced the doctrinal line from Trinity Lutheran Church of Columbia, Inc. v. Comer (2017) through Espinoza v. Montana Department of Revenue (2020) to Carson. In Trinity Lutheran, the Court held that denying a church a generally available playground-resurfacing grant violated the Free Exercise Clause because the denial rested solely on religious status. In Espinoza, the Court applied the same rule to scholarship funds and held that a Montana constitutional provision barring aid to religiously controlled schools was unconstitutional. Maine argued that Carson was different because the nonsectarian requirement went not to the school's religious "status" but to its religious "use" of the funds -- the schools would use the money for explicitly religious instruction. Roberts rejected this distinction as formalistic: a law conditioning a benefit on how the recipient must not use it (i.e., not religiously) is still a law discriminating on the basis of religion. The prohibition on use-based discrimination logically followed from the prohibition on status-based discrimination.

Roberts also rejected Maine's reliance on Locke v. Davey (2004), which upheld a state scholarship program that excluded vocational training for the ministry. Roberts explained that Locke turned on a "historic and substantial" state interest against supporting church leaders, and that no comparable historical tradition exists against the general funding of religious schools.

## Key Points

- Carson extends the **Trinity Lutheran-Espinoza** line to hold that states cannot condition otherwise available education subsidies on a school's being "nonsectarian" -- the exclusion is religious discrimination regardless of whether it is framed as status-based or use-based.
- The **status-use distinction** attempted by the First Circuit -- allowing use-based exclusions while prohibiting status-based ones -- was definitively rejected. A state cannot avoid Free Exercise scrutiny by characterizing its religious exclusion as targeting how funds will be spent.
- **Locke v. Davey** survives but is confined to its narrow facts: a state's "historic and substantial" interest in not funding ministerial training. No comparable tradition bars funding of religious K-12 schools.
- Carson creates tension with state antidiscrimination laws because participating religious schools often claim religious exemptions from LGBTQ and other hiring and admissions requirements -- a question Roberts left open.
- The ruling has significant implications for **school choice** programs: states operating voucher or tuition assistance programs almost certainly cannot exclude religious schools from participation.
