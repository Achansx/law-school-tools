# Slaughter-House Cases (1873)

## Memory Jogger

> The first Supreme Court reading of the Fourteenth Amendment stripped the Privileges or Immunities Clause of independent force by splitting citizenship in two, leaving national privileges narrow and state privileges untouched.

---

## Facts

In 1869 the Louisiana legislature granted the Crescent City Live-Stock Landing and Slaughter-House Company a 25-year exclusive monopoly to operate slaughterhouses within a defined three-parish district surrounding New Orleans. The statute obligated Crescent City to accept any butcher's animals for a regulated fee, but it forbade every other butcher in the district from maintaining an independent slaughterhouse. The stated justification was public health: concentrating offal, waste, and animal carcasses in a single facility downstream of the city's water supply addressed cholera outbreaks that had swept the region.

Hundreds of Louisiana butchers, organized as the Butchers' Benevolent Association, sued under the newly ratified Thirteenth and Fourteenth Amendments. They argued that the monopoly reduced independent butchers to something close to forced labor, abridged their privileges and immunities as citizens of the United States, denied them equal protection, and deprived them of property in their trade without due process.

The case arrived only five years after ratification of the Fourteenth Amendment, making it the first major opportunity for the Supreme Court to interpret the amendment's substantive reach.

---

## Procedural History

Six consolidated cases from Louisiana state courts challenged the monopoly on state and federal grounds. The Louisiana Supreme Court upheld the statute in every proceeding, and the butchers sought review in the Supreme Court of the United States on writs of error. Argument took place twice, the second time because of continuing internal disagreement and a vacancy on the Court.

A sharply divided Court affirmed 5-4. Justice Miller wrote for the majority; Justice Field authored the principal dissent, joined by Chief Justice Chase and Justices Swayne and Bradley. Justice Bradley and Justice Swayne wrote separate dissents.

## Holding

The Privileges or Immunities Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment protects only a narrow set of privileges and immunities that owe their existence to the Federal Government, its national character, its Constitution, or its laws; it does not transfer the great body of civil rights traditionally protected by the states to federal supervision. A Louisiana statute granting an exclusive slaughterhouse monopoly is a valid exercise of the police power and does not violate the Thirteenth or Fourteenth Amendments.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Justice Miller treated the amendment against the backdrop of its Reconstruction purpose, which he read as primarily remedial for the freedmen rather than as a wholesale restructuring of the federal-state balance. Section 1, he insisted, distinguishes citizenship of the United States from citizenship of a state, and the Privileges or Immunities Clause reaches only the former. The privileges owed to national citizenship are a limited enumerated set: access to the seat of government, use of navigable waters, protection on the high seas, the writ of habeas corpus, and similar incidents of the federal relationship. The broad civil rights catalogued by Justice Washington in Corfield v Coryell remain attributes of state citizenship and therefore beyond the new amendment's reach. Reading the clause more broadly, Miller warned, would make the Court "a perpetual censor upon all legislation of the States" and "fetter and degrade the State governments." On that reading, the Louisiana monopoly was a routine health and safety regulation of the sort states had always enacted.

### Dissent (Field)

Justice Field, joined by Chief Justice Chase and Justices Bradley and Swayne, argued that the Privileges or Immunities Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment protected a broad category of natural and common-law rights -- including the right of every American citizen to pursue a lawful calling -- against state interference. Drawing on Justice Washington's circuit opinion in Corfield v. Coryell (1823), which catalogued the fundamental rights of citizenship recognized by the free states, Field argued that "among the privileges and immunities of citizens" were rights that "belong, of right, to the citizens of all free governments." These included the right to pursue any trade, occupation, or profession not requiring special skill or training -- the right to engage in ordinary labor. A monopoly that excluded hundreds of licensed butchers from their chosen trade for the exclusive benefit of a private corporation was not a reasonable regulation of a calling but the extirpation of it: the state cannot under the guise of health regulation grant one group of private citizens the exclusive privilege to engage in an entire industry while forbidding all others. Field viewed the majority's narrow reading of the Clause as rendering it a nullity; it protected only a handful of federally created rights that would have been legally cognizable even without the amendment. The dissent foreshadowed the Lochner era: the Fourteenth Amendment's liberty-of-contract doctrine that dominated from the 1890s through 1937 drew directly on Field's framework, substituting substantive due process for the P or I Clause after Slaughter-House made the latter unavailable.

### Dissent (Bradley)

Justice Bradley's separate dissent pressed the Due Process Clause as an independent basis for striking down the monopoly, developing the inherent-rights framework that would eventually become substantive due process. Bradley argued that every citizen possesses certain natural and inherent rights that government cannot abolish -- including the right to pursue a lawful vocation as a means of livelihood. A "calling" or trade is "property" in the constitutional sense: depriving a person of the right to pursue their occupation is as much a deprivation of property as seizing their physical goods. The Due Process Clause's requirement that no person be deprived of property without due process operates as a substantive limit, not merely a procedural one: legislation that destroys a trade without compensation or legitimate police-power justification violates the Clause regardless of the process provided. Bradley thus developed, independently of Field's P or I argument, the foundation for substantive due process review: the idea that certain deprivations of economic liberty are constitutionally impermissible regardless of the procedures attending them. Field's P or I route was closed by the majority's holding; Bradley's substantive due process route remained available and eventually became the doctrinal vehicle for economic liberty claims in Lochner v. New York (1905) and the line of cases it spawned.

### Dissent (Swayne)

Justice Swayne's brief dissent emphasized the transformative character of the Reconstruction Amendments and argued the majority's narrow construction was inconsistent with the framers' intent. The Thirteenth, Fourteenth, and Fifteenth Amendments together worked a fundamental restructuring of American federalism: they placed individual rights under federal protection against state interference, reversing the antebellum assumption that the states were the primary guarantors of civil liberties. To read the Privileges or Immunities Clause as protecting only a thin list of federally derived entitlements -- as Miller's majority did -- was to turn "what was meant for bread into a stone." Swayne argued the majority's construction was "too narrow" and "too timid" given the constitutional revolution Congress and the people had enacted between 1865 and 1868. The amendment's purpose was not merely to confirm a limited set of federal-citizenship rights already known to exist, but to guarantee that the broad category of civil rights traditionally associated with American citizenship would be enforceable against state deprivation in federal court. Swayne's critique, though the briefest of the three dissents, captures the textualist-historicist objection: the majority cannot reconcile its narrow reading with either the text ("privileges or immunities of citizens of the United States") or the historical context of a nation that had just fought a war over the meaning of citizenship.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

1. A state legislature grants a single corporation an exclusive 30-year franchise to operate all water supply infrastructure in a metropolitan region. A competing water company and individual citizens challenge the grant under the Privileges or Immunities Clause, arguing it deprives them of the right to enter the water-supply business. Under Slaughter-House's holding, the Privileges or Immunities Clause protects only federal-citizenship rights -- access to federal offices and protections, use of the navigable waters, etc. -- not the general right to pursue a trade or business. The right to operate a utility and to compete in commercial markets is, on Miller's reading, an attribute of state citizenship subject to state regulation. The monopoly franchise is within the state's police power to regulate and control local commercial enterprises. Comes out the same way.

2. A state enacts a comprehensive licensing regime for electricians that requires a three-year apprenticeship, a written examination, and a $5,000 license fee, effectively limiting entry into the trade. A new entrant challenges the regime as a deprivation of her right to pursue a lawful trade under the Privileges or Immunities Clause. Slaughter-House controls: the right to pursue an ordinary trade is an attribute of state, not national, citizenship, and the state's police power to license and regulate trades is broad. Miller's majority explicitly declined to make the Court "a perpetual censor upon all legislation of the States" by federalizing common civil rights. The licensing regime comes out the same way -- it is the kind of routine commercial regulation the state has always been free to enact.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

3. A state law makes it a crime to file a petition with a federal agency or to hire a lawyer to represent a petitioner before a federal department, ostensibly to prevent "frivolous regulatory interference." A regulated business challenges the law under the Privileges or Immunities Clause. On Slaughter-House's narrow enumeration, this comes out differently. Miller's majority listed among the genuine privileges of national citizenship the right to "seek its [the federal government's] protection," the right "to use the navigable waters of the United States," and the right to "come to the seat of government to assert any claim." The right to petition federal agencies for redress falls within this narrow national-citizenship core. Slaughter-House's P or I Clause is not dead -- it protects rights that "owe their existence to the Federal Government, its national character, its Constitution, or its laws." A state law directly interfering with the federal petition right triggers that core.

4. A citizen challenges a state civil forfeiture law -- which seizes private property without criminal conviction -- under the Privileges or Immunities Clause, arguing it deprives her of a nationally recognized right to property. Under Slaughter-House, property rights are state-citizenship rights beyond the Clause's reach; they are attributes of state law. But the modern doctrinal work once attributed to P or I is now done through the Due Process Clause and incorporation. Timbs v. Indiana (2019) applied the Eighth Amendment's Excessive Fines Clause against the states via the Due Process Clause, specifically declining to re-examine whether P or I was the better vehicle. McDonald v. City of Chicago (2010) incorporated the Second Amendment through Due Process over Thomas's P or I concurrence. The takeaway: in modern practice, litigants seeking to apply Bill of Rights provisions against states invoke incorporation via Due Process rather than P or I, precisely because Slaughter-House foreclosed the latter as a live theory. This hypo would come out differently than Slaughter-House because the modern doctrine (incorporation via DP) does what Field's P or I theory was designed to do -- but it uses a different clause.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

5. A city enacts a strict handgun ban. A resident challenges it under the Second Amendment as incorporated against the states, asking the Court to use the Privileges or Immunities Clause rather than the Due Process Clause as the incorporation vehicle. This is the fence-sitter because it squarely raises whether Slaughter-House should be overruled. McDonald v. City of Chicago (2010) presented exactly this question: a plurality incorporated the Second Amendment via Due Process, while Justice Thomas concurred on the ground that the Privileges or Immunities Clause is the textually correct mechanism for incorporation and Slaughter-House should be reconsidered. The plurality refused to revisit Slaughter-House. The fence-sitter tension: Slaughter-House is formally good law and its narrow reading is reaffirmed each time incorporation is done through Due Process rather than P or I. But Thomas's McDonald concurrence, along with the academic critique developed by Akhil Amar and others, provides serious grounds for revisiting it. A future Court persuaded by Thomas's position would overrule Slaughter-House as to the incorporation question while perhaps preserving Miller's holding on the specific facts (state police-power monopoly). The constitutional outcome of the gun-ban challenge does not change -- the Second Amendment applies -- but the doctrinal rationale would shift.

---

## Critique

Slaughter-House is the most consequential interpretive decision in the history of the Reconstruction Amendments, and both its progressive critics and its originalist critics converge on the same bottom line: Miller got it wrong. The progressive critique, most forcefully developed by Akhil Amar and Charles Fairman's revisers, is that Miller gutted the central structural provision of the Fourteenth Amendment. Section 1's Privileges or Immunities Clause was understood by the drafters of the amendment -- Congressman John Bingham and Senator Jacob Howard, whose floor speeches are the primary legislative record -- as protecting the civil rights enumerated in the Civil Rights Act of 1866 (to make contracts, sue, hold property, etc.) against state deprivation. Miller's dual-citizenship bifurcation reduced the Clause to a list of preexisting federal incidentals that added nothing to what the Constitution already protected. By routing civil rights protection through the Due Process and Equal Protection Clauses instead, the majority displaced the constitutional work onto provisions that were not designed to carry it and whose text less clearly supports the result: substantive rights derived from "due process of law" require a doctrinal gyration that the P or I Clause does not.

The originalist critique runs in the same direction from a different starting point. Justice Thomas's concurrence in McDonald v. City of Chicago (2010) argued that Miller's reading of the P or I Clause is inconsistent with the text, the Framers' intent, and the antebellum understanding of "privileges and immunities" as a term of art encompassing fundamental civil rights. John Harrison's contrary originalist reading -- that P or I was a non-discrimination provision, not a substantive-rights provision -- remains the academic minority view. The stronger originalist position supports Field and Howard's reading: the Clause was intended to nationalize the civil rights of all citizens, not merely to confirm a thin list of federally derived entitlements. If that is correct, Slaughter-House was wrong on its own terms, and the century of incorporation doctrine built on Due Process rather than P or I represents a doctrinal detour around a misread constitutional text. The 5-4 vote, Justice Bradley's immediate recognition of the constitutional stakes, and the dissents' force all suggest the case was closer than its eventual doctrinal dominance implies.

## Key Points

- First Supreme Court interpretation of the Fourteenth Amendment; effectively neutralizes the Privileges or Immunities Clause as an independent civil-rights provision.
- Vote: 5-4. Miller majority; Field principal dissent, joined by Chase, Bradley, and Swayne.
- Forces later incorporation and civil-rights doctrine to flow through the Due Process and Equal Protection Clauses rather than Privileges or Immunities.
- Revisited but not overruled in McDonald v City of Chicago (2010), where Thomas's concurrence urges revival of the Privileges or Immunities Clause.

Field's Slaughter-House dissent becomes the lead Bradwell dissent; Bradley's Slaughter-House dissent on fundamental rights yields to the Bradwell "separate spheres" concurrence. Teaching the Miller narrowing without Bradwell's worked example drops the doctrinal punch line.
