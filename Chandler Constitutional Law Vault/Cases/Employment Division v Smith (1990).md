# Employment Division v Smith (1990)

## Memory Jogger

**The Peyote Case.** Native American Church members were fired for using peyote as a sacrament, then denied unemployment benefits because their termination was for "misconduct." The Court (Scalia) used this case to dramatically narrow Free Exercise: if a law is *neutral* and *generally applicable*, it survives Free Exercise even without satisfying strict scrutiny, regardless of how substantially it burdens religious practice. The Sherbert compelling-interest test is largely relegated to unemployment compensation cases and laws targeting religion. Congress responded with RFRA (later struck down in *Boerne* as applied to states).

---

## Facts

- Alfred Smith and Galen Black were members of the Native American Church; they used peyote as a sacrament in religious ceremonies
- Both were fired from their jobs as drug rehabilitation counselors because their employer discovered they had used peyote
- Smith and Black applied for unemployment compensation from Oregon's Employment Division
- Oregon denied benefits, concluding they were terminated for "misconduct" — specifically, their illegal use of a controlled substance (peyote was prohibited under Oregon law with no religious exemption)
- Smith and Black argued that denying benefits based on religiously motivated peyote use violated the Free Exercise Clause

---

## Procedural History

- Oregon Supreme Court (first time): reversed denial of benefits; Free Exercise required an exemption from the criminal prohibition as applied to sacramental use
- Supreme Court (first time, 1988): vacated and remanded to determine whether Oregon's criminal law actually prohibited sacramental peyote use
- Oregon Supreme Court (second time): Oregon law did prohibit sacramental peyote; nonetheless, the Free Exercise Clause prohibited denial of benefits
- Supreme Court (second time, this decision): reversed Oregon Supreme Court; Free Exercise Clause does not require an exemption from a neutral, generally applicable law

---

## Judicial Votes

- **6-3 decision** reversing Oregon Supreme Court
- **Majority**: Scalia (joined by Rehnquist CJ, White, Stevens, Kennedy) — neutral generally applicable laws do not require religious exemptions
- **Concurrence**: O'Connor (joined by Brennan, Marshall, Blackmun in part) — agrees in the judgment but rejects Scalia's reasoning; would apply compelling-interest test and still uphold Oregon's law
- **Dissent**: Blackmun (joined by Brennan, Marshall) — applies Sherbert; Oregon's interest not compelling enough to justify denying an exemption

---

## Holding

The Free Exercise Clause does not require the government to provide religious exemptions from neutral, generally applicable laws. *Sherbert v. Verner* compelling-interest review applies when a law is not neutral or not generally applicable — including laws that operate through an individualized-assessment mechanism in which secular reasons justify non-compliance but religious reasons do not. *Sherbert*'s own unemployment-benefits facts are the paradigm case of individualized assessment (a state officer evaluates each claim for "good cause"), not an independent unemployment-only carve-out. Where a law is neutral and generally applicable, by contrast, it need only survive rational basis review — even if the law substantially burdens religious practice.

If a law specifically targets religion or is not generally applicable (i.e., it creates a "system of individual exemptions" in which secular reasons justify non-compliance but religious reasons do not), then strict scrutiny applies under *Church of Lukumi Babalu Aye v. Hialeah* (1993).

---

## Analysis

### Majority (Scalia)

Scalia's opinion opens with the textual move that drives everything else: the Free Exercise Clause protects the "exercise of religion" against laws "prohibiting" it, but the word "prohibiting" is doing lighter work than a modern reader expects. The First Amendment absolutely forbids government from regulating religious *belief* or punishing *religious status*. But when the government regulates *conduct* through a neutral, generally applicable law, the burden on religious practice is an incidental effect of a lawful regulation, not a constitutional violation. Scalia treats this as the default position of free exercise jurisprudence as it existed before Sherbert -- Reynolds v. United States (1878) (polygamy), Jacobson v. Massachusetts (1905) (compulsory vaccination), United States v. Lee (1982) (Amish Social Security taxes), Braunfeld v. Brown (1961) (Sunday closing laws) -- and presents Sherbert's compelling-interest rule as an aberration that applied only to unemployment compensation schemes, where the government operates a discretionary benefits system in which individual exemptions are routine.

The heart of the majority opinion is the *anomaly* argument. Applying strict scrutiny to every neutral law that burdens religion would, Scalia writes, "open the prospect of constitutionally required religious exemptions from civic obligations of almost every conceivable kind" -- military service, taxation, manslaughter laws, compulsory vaccination, drug laws, traffic laws, minimum wage laws, child labor laws, equal-employment laws. The catalog of examples is meant to show that a robust compelling-interest regime would be either unadministrable (the court would be in the business of continually weighing "severe impact" on religious practice against state interests) or destabilizing (judicially required exemptions would privilege religion relative to other constitutional interests). Scalia turns free exercise into an equal-protection-adjacent claim: the Clause protects against *targeting* of religion, not against incidental burden.

Scalia then handles the Sherbert precedent with a two-track distinction. First, Sherbert applies only to "individualized governmental assessment of the reasons for the relevant conduct," a narrow category that encompasses unemployment-compensation cases and analogous discretionary benefits schemes. (This is the carve-out that Lukumi and later Fulton will exploit: any system of individualized exemptions triggers strict scrutiny.) Second, the "hybrid rights" exception: Wisconsin v. Yoder (1972) and Pierce v. Society of Sisters (1925) involved free exercise plus another constitutional right (parental control of education, in Yoder's case), and hybrid rights claims continue to receive heightened scrutiny. Scalia does not commit to what counts as a "hybrid rights" claim or how much additional constitutional weight the second right must carry -- a notorious under-theorization that has generated three decades of doctrinal instability.

The centrality move is the opinion's most politically sharp. Scalia refuses to let courts decide whether a particular religious practice is "central" to an adherent's faith because doing so would require judges to evaluate the relative importance of religious beliefs -- a category of inquiry the Establishment Clause forbids. This is Scalia responding to the dissenters' proposed middle ground (Sherbert but only when the burden is "substantial" or the practice "central") and rejecting it as unworkable. The rule that emerges is categorical: no strict scrutiny, no exemption, no case-by-case weighing, unless the law fails facial neutrality, general applicability, or falls within the Sherbert/hybrid-rights carve-outs.

### Concurrence (O'Connor)

O'Connor's concurrence -- joined by Brennan, Marshall, and Blackmun in the portions rejecting Scalia's rule -- is a sharp methodological dissent disguised as a concurrence in the judgment. She agrees that Oregon's criminal prohibition of peyote survives the Free Exercise Clause, but she reaches that conclusion through the pre-Smith framework: government bears the burden of showing a compelling interest narrowly tailored to restrict religiously motivated conduct, and Oregon's interest in uniform enforcement of controlled substance laws is compelling enough to satisfy that standard as applied to peyote.

Her opinion rests on three methodological moves. First, she reads the Free Exercise Clause as *textually protective* of religiously motivated conduct, not merely of belief: "A law that prohibits certain conduct -- conduct that happens to be an act of worship for someone -- manifestly does prohibit that person's free exercise of his religion." The First Amendment does not distinguish between laws that target religion and laws that are generally applicable; the operative question is whether religious exercise is burdened, not whether the government intended the burden. Second, she treats Sherbert as a general principle, not an unemployment-compensation-only carve-out, and frames the compelling-interest test as the ordinary framework courts should apply whenever government substantially burdens religious exercise. Third, she presents the "parade of horribles" as overstated: courts have been applying the compelling-interest test for decades and have "been quite capable of . . . striking sensible balances between religious liberty and competing state interests," typically resolving the question in the government's favor after genuine scrutiny.

On application, O'Connor concludes Oregon wins because controlled-substance enforcement is a state interest of the highest order, peyote cannot easily be distinguished from other prohibited substances, and the state's concerns about administrability and enforcement uniformity are substantial. The concurrence is sometimes taught as an object lesson: even under the Sherbert framework, most religious exemption claims against criminal laws fail. The point is that applying strict scrutiny does not mean granting exemptions; it means requiring the government to justify its position.

The opinion is also a placeholder for the Smith-skeptical coalition that would later pass RFRA. O'Connor is the core of the "apply Sherbert, apply it seriously" camp; her opinion gives Congress the analytical framework it would adopt in RFRA (substantial burden + compelling interest + least restrictive means) almost verbatim.

### Concurrence-in-part / Dissent (Blackmun, Brennan, Marshall)

Blackmun's dissent agrees with O'Connor's methodological points -- Sherbert's compelling-interest test is the correct framework -- but reaches the opposite result on application: Oregon's interest is not compelling enough to justify denying unemployment benefits to sacramental peyote users. Blackmun emphasizes that peyote is not a "popular drug," that its sacramental use is tightly circumscribed by the Native American Church's liturgy, and that federal law and many states already exempt religious peyote use without administrative collapse. The "flood of exemption claims" that Scalia fears has not materialized in jurisdictions that already grant the exemption, making the speculative nature of the state's enforcement concerns dispositive.

Blackmun also mounts a critique of Scalia's historical analysis, arguing that the Founders understood the Free Exercise Clause as protecting conduct and not merely belief, and that Jefferson's and Madison's writings on religious liberty point toward the substantive protection Sherbert provides. The dissent closes with the practical observation that Smith's rule systematically disadvantages minority religious practices that do not benefit from secular exemptions negotiated by majority religions through the ordinary legislative process. This is the structural critique that McConnell, Laycock, and the RFRA coalition would carry forward.

### Dissent (Blackmun, Brennan, Marshall) — see merged treatment above

Blackmun's dissent is analyzed with the O'Connor concurrence, since the two opinions share a methodological framework (apply Sherbert) and split only on application.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way under Smith)

**(1) A neutral workplace drug policy enforced against a Rastafarian employee terminated for sacramental cannabis use in a state where cannabis remains Schedule I.** Employer's policy prohibits any illegal drug use, with no individualized exemptions; a Rastafarian is fired after failing a random drug test and denied unemployment benefits for misconduct.
*Why: The policy is neutral (no religious targeting) and generally applicable (no secular exemptions). Smith's rule bars any free exercise claim for an exemption. The employee would need to locate an individualized-assessment carve-out -- e.g., if the employer routinely excuses positive tests for prescription drugs, Lukumi/Fulton analysis might re-enter -- or invoke RLUIPA/state RFRA. Without such a trigger, Smith controls and the state wins.*

**(2) Compulsory school attendance law applied to a Hindu family seeking to homeschool for religious training.** State mandates school attendance until age 16 with no religious exemption; a Hindu family challenges the law as burdening the parents' religious duty to provide Vedic education.
*Why: The law is neutral and generally applicable. Scalia's opinion specifically distinguished Yoder as a hybrid-rights case (free exercise + parental rights + a claim rooted in a specific Amish lifestyle preserved for 300 years). Absent the hybrid-rights framing that Yoder's facts support, the Hindu family's claim fails under Smith; the law need only survive rational basis review.*

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently under Smith)

**(1) Zoning ordinance that generally prohibits any building over 35 feet but exempts commercial structures with historic-district variance grants available on a case-by-case basis, applied to a mosque's proposed minaret.** Although the ordinance is facially neutral, the case-by-case variance scheme creates individualized assessments.
*Why: The individualized-exemptions carve-out from Smith (which Lukumi and later Fulton sharpen) triggers strict scrutiny because the variance scheme provides "a mechanism for individualized exemptions" (Smith, 494 U.S. at 884), and a government cannot categorically refuse to extend such a system to religious hardship without a compelling reason. The case would come out for the mosque under Lukumi/Fulton, not under Smith.*

**(2) Municipal ordinance banning "unnecessary" killing of animals, with exemptions for hunting, fishing, euthanasia, pest control, and kosher slaughter, applied to Santeria animal sacrifice.** The ordinance is not generally applicable because secular animal-killing conduct is exempted while religiously motivated killing is prohibited.
*Why: This is Lukumi. Strict scrutiny applies, and the city's underinclusive law cannot satisfy it. The example illustrates Smith's other carve-out: when a neutral-seeming law systematically exempts comparable secular conduct, Smith's rational basis floor does not apply.*

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear under Smith)

**Federal prison regulation requiring all inmates to be clean-shaven for security reasons, challenged by a Muslim inmate whose faith requires a beard of at least one fist in length.** The regulation is neutral and generally applicable on its face, but the prison grants medical exemptions to inmates with dermatological conditions.
*Why: Under Smith alone, the regulation is neutral and generally applicable, so the inmate loses. But the medical-exemption practice creates a Lukumi/Fulton individualized-exemptions trigger that may require strict scrutiny. Holt v. Hobbs (2015) avoided the question by applying RLUIPA (which imports compelling-interest review statutorily). Whether the regulation survives *constitutional* scrutiny depends on how broadly the individualized-exemptions doctrine reads the carve-out -- an open question under current Fulton/Tandon doctrine.*

## Critique

**Progressive/pro-accommodation critique.** Michael McConnell (The Origins and Historical Understanding of Free Exercise, 103 Harv. L. Rev. 1409 (1990)) and Douglas Laycock have argued that Smith reads the Free Exercise Clause far more narrowly than text, history, and early republic practice support. McConnell documents multiple state constitutional free-exercise provisions that were drafted with explicit exemption language and argues the federal Clause was understood in the same protective vein. On the structural point, Smith systematically disadvantages minority religions: majority religious practices already fit into the background legal rules because those rules were drafted with majority practices in mind (Sunday closing laws align with Christian sabbath; zoning rules accommodate common Christian architecture; food safety and workplace laws leave room for mainstream Christian and Jewish practice but not for peyote, santeria, or minority Jewish practices). The Sherbert framework, for all its administrative costs, at least forced government to justify burdens on minority practices.

**Originalist critique of Scalia's history.** Though Smith reached an originalist-leaning result (no judicially crafted exemptions absent targeting), Scalia's historical analysis has been contested from within originalism. Michael McConnell's originalist account argues that the founding generation expected religiously motivated conduct to receive constitutional protection beyond mere belief, citing state constitutional debates and colonial practice. A more modern originalist line (Vincent Phillip Muñoz) reads the Clause as narrowly procedural rather than substantively protective. The originalist debate is unresolved, and Scalia's confident dismissal of the Sherbert framework as a "parade of horribles" sidesteps rather than engages the historical ambiguity.

**Administrability critique.** Scalia's "constitutional anomaly" argument assumes that courts would grant exemptions in most cases if they applied Sherbert. O'Connor's concurrence, and four decades of pre-Smith practice, suggest otherwise: courts applying Sherbert routinely found state interests compelling and denied exemptions. The administrative burden is the case-by-case inquiry itself, but administrative burden is a strange metric for defining constitutional protection -- equal protection and free speech doctrine also require courts to weigh compelling interests, and no one argues those rights should be narrowed for administrability.

**The Smith-Lukumi-Fulton trajectory.** Ironically, Smith has been progressively undermined by its own carve-outs. The "individualized exemptions" door Scalia left open in the Sherbert unemployment context has become, through Lukumi and especially Fulton (2021), a near-general restoration of strict scrutiny: a law is not generally applicable, and Smith does not apply, when government "'invite[s]' the government to consider the particular reasons for a person's conduct by providing 'a mechanism for individualized exemptions'" (Fulton, quoting Smith, 494 U.S. at 884). Many scholars argue Smith is functionally overruled in all but name, and a majority of the Court appears ready to overrule it outright (Fulton's concurrences by Alito, Thomas, and Gorsuch openly said so).

**Legislative reversal and the federalism backlash.** Congress responded to Smith with RFRA (1993), restoring the compelling-interest test by statute. The political coalition behind RFRA was extraordinary -- ACLU, National Association of Evangelicals, and the Church of Scientology all supported it -- which itself supports the critics' position that Smith's rule was out of step with the American religious-liberty tradition. [[Cases/City of Boerne v Flores (1997)|Boerne]] struck RFRA down as applied to the states on Section 5 grounds, producing the current patchwork where federal RFRA binds federal actors, state RFRAs (or state constitutional parallels) bind state actors in some jurisdictions, and the Smith constitutional floor applies elsewhere.

**Minority-religion disparate impact.** Empirical studies (Stephanie Barclay, Mark Rienzi) document that Smith-era free exercise claims succeed at lower rates for minority religions (Muslim, Sikh, Native American, Santeria) than for Christian claimants, precisely because legislative exemptions and Lukumi/Fulton triggers more reliably protect practices that majority religions have negotiated into background law. The critique is not that Smith is facially discriminatory but that its formal-equality framework conceals substantive inequality -- a standard progressive critique of "color-blind" and "religion-blind" doctrines.

## Key Points

- **The Smith test**: Free Exercise Clause requires only that laws burdening religion be *neutral* (not targeting religion on its face) and *generally applicable* (not creating a system of individual exemptions favoring secular over religious)
- **If neutral and generally applicable**: rational basis review only, regardless of how substantial the burden on religious practice
- **If not neutral or not generally applicable** (Lukumi): strict scrutiny applies — law must be justified by a compelling governmental interest and narrowly tailored
- **Sherbert test (pre-Smith)**: required compelling interest + least restrictive means whenever government substantially burdened religious exercise; Smith limits Sherbert to the unemployment compensation context
- **Hybrid rights**: Scalia notes that if Free Exercise is combined with another constitutional right (e.g., free speech, parental rights), heightened scrutiny may apply — but the scope of this exception remains uncertain
- **Legislative response**: Congress passed RFRA in 1993 to restore the Sherbert test; RFRA is valid as applied to the federal government (*Gonzales v. O Centro*) but was struck down as applied to states in *Boerne*
- **RFRA state analogs**: many states enacted state RFRAs or state courts interpret state constitutions to require compelling interest test even after Smith

## Connections

- [[Cases/Reynolds v United States (1879)|Reynolds v United States (1879)]] — the belief-conduct foundation that Smith's neutral-and-generally-applicable rule modernizes; Scalia's majority treats Reynolds as the original authority for the proposition that religious motivation does not excuse compliance with a valid criminal law of general application, and rebuilds the doctrine on that footing after the Sherbert detour.
- [[Cases/Sherbert v Verner (1963)]] — the unemployment compensation case that established the compelling-interest test Smith limits; still good law in the unemployment context
- [[Wisconsin v Yoder (1972)]] — Scalia treats Yoder as a hybrid-rights case (Free Exercise + parental rights), distinguishing it from Smith's neutral law rule
- [[Cases/Church of Lukumi Babalu Aye v City of Hialeah (1993)]] — applying strict scrutiny when a law specifically targets religious practices (animal sacrifices); companion to Smith
- [[Cases/City of Boerne v Flores (1997)]] — Congress enacted RFRA to override Smith; Boerne struck down RFRA as applied to states; the Smith standard remains the constitutional baseline
- [[Cases/Fulton v City of Philadelphia (2021)]] — Court unanimously struck down a city anti-discrimination policy as applied to a Catholic foster care agency under Smith's "individual exemptions" carve-out, without overruling Smith
- [[Topics/Free Exercise of Religion]] → lecture that situates Smith within the broader Free Exercise doctrine arc
- [[Cases/Lemon v Kurtzman (1971)|Lemon v Kurtzman (1971)]] — parallel Religion-Clause doctrinal shift toward neutrality; together with Lemon's eventual abandonment in Kennedy v Bremerton (2022), Smith's rational-basis rule marks a Religion-Clause movement away from the compelling-interest framework that dominated the 1960s-70s across both the Free Exercise and Establishment contexts
- [[Cases/Christian Legal Society v Martinez (2010)|Christian Legal Society v Martinez (2010)]] — Reciprocal of Martinez's Smith entry. Ginsburg's CLS majority applies Smith's neutral-and-generally-applicable rule to a public university's all-comers nondiscrimination policy and finds it satisfied. Martinez extends Smith's logic from criminal laws into the limited-public-forum and government-benefit-conditioning contexts, and is now the controlling vehicle for applying Smith's neutrality principle to viewpoint-neutral access policies.
