# Timbs v Indiana (2019)

## Memory Jogger

> Timbs is the capstone modern incorporation case. A unanimous Court (Ginsburg, J.) incorporates the Eighth Amendment's Excessive Fines Clause against the states, holding that there is no daylight between federal and state conduct on this guarantee. The case matters doctrinally for three reasons: it restates the modern incorporation test (fundamental to our scheme of ordered liberty, or deeply rooted in history and tradition); it confirms that civil in rem forfeiture that is at least partially punitive falls within the Clause; and the Thomas and Gorsuch concurrences press the case for routing incorporation through the Privileges or Immunities Clause, keeping the Slaughter-House revival debate alive.

---

## Facts

Tyson Timbs pleaded guilty in Indiana state court to one count of dealing in a controlled substance (heroin) and one count of conspiracy to commit theft. The state trial court sentenced him to six years, with five suspended to probation, and ordered him to pay $1,203 in fees and costs. The maximum statutory monetary fine for Timbs's offenses was $10,000. Timbs had bought a Land Rover LR2 for approximately $42,000, using money he had received from his father's life insurance policy. He used the vehicle to transport heroin. At the time of the arrest, the Land Rover was worth roughly $42,000.

The State initiated a civil forfeiture action against the Land Rover under Indiana law. The trial court denied forfeiture, holding that forfeiture of the vehicle would be grossly disproportionate to the gravity of the offense given that the maximum statutory fine was $10,000 and that Timbs had purchased the vehicle with lawfully inherited funds rather than with drug proceeds. The Indiana Court of Appeals affirmed. The Indiana Supreme Court reversed, holding that the Excessive Fines Clause had never been incorporated against the states and therefore Indiana's forfeiture was not subject to an excessiveness limit under the federal Constitution.

---

## Procedural History

Forfeiture denied by the Indiana trial court as excessive. Affirmed by the Indiana Court of Appeals. Reversed by the Indiana Supreme Court on the ground that the Excessive Fines Clause does not bind the states. The United States Supreme Court granted certiorari to decide whether the Eighth Amendment's Excessive Fines Clause is incorporated against the states through the Fourteenth Amendment.

## Holding

The Eighth Amendment's Excessive Fines Clause is an incorporated protection applicable to the States under the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause. A Bill of Rights guarantee is incorporated when it is fundamental to our scheme of ordered liberty or deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition. The protection against excessive fines has been a constant shield against abuses of governmental punitive or revenue-producing power from Magna Carta through the Founding, the Reconstruction Amendments, and every State constitution. The Clause covers civil in rem forfeitures to the extent they are at least partially punitive, as Austin v. United States established. The Indiana Supreme Court's contrary ruling is vacated.

---

## Analysis

### Majority (Opinion of the Court, Justice Ginsburg)

The opinion moves in three steps. First, it restates the modern incorporation standard: a protection is incorporated if it is fundamental to our scheme of ordered liberty or deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition, applying with equal strength against the States as against the federal government. Second, it marshals the historical case: the Excessive Fines Clause traces to Chapter 39 of Magna Carta through the 1689 English Bill of Rights and into the constitutions of the several States, both at the Founding and at Reconstruction; Blackstone, state-constitutional text, and Reconstruction-era debates confirm its deep roots. Third, it applies McDonald's principle that incorporated rights apply with full federal force, meaning there is no daylight between federal and state conduct. The Court rejects Indiana's argument that the Clause applies only to in personam criminal fines, not civil in rem forfeiture, and relies on Austin v. United States (1993) to confirm that partially punitive forfeitures are subject to the Clause. The inquiry focuses on the right guaranteed in general, not on each particular application.

### Concurrence

**Gorsuch, J.** A brief two-paragraph concurrence joining the majority in full but flagging one reservation: "As an original matter, I acknowledge, the appropriate vehicle for incorporation may well be the Fourteenth Amendment's Privileges or Immunities Clause, rather than, as this Court has long assumed, the Due Process Clause." Gorsuch does not elaborate because the outcome is unaffected by the clause-selection question, since the Excessive Fines Clause is incorporated either way. The concurrence functions as a bookmark: Gorsuch signals willingness to revisit the clause-selection question in a future case where the choice of vehicle would drive a different result, and invites litigants to present the P&I argument when it matters. This aligns him with Thomas's longer-running position while preserving the unanimity of Ginsburg's opinion on the operative question.

**Thomas, J., concurring in the judgment.** Thomas's concurrence is a sustained originalist brief for routing incorporation through the Privileges or Immunities Clause. He agrees that the Excessive Fines Clause binds the states but insists the doctrinal vehicle is wrong. Three moves. First, "the right to be free from excessive fines is one of the 'privileges or immunities of citizens of the United States' protected by the Fourteenth Amendment," with roots in Magna Carta, the English Bill of Rights, and state constitutions that establish it as a fundamental right of American citizenship at Reconstruction. Second, Slaughter-House (1873) misread the Privileges or Immunities Clause by defining the rights it covered "quite narrowly," leaving later litigants to seek "an alternative fount of such rights" in substantive due process. Third, substantive due process is textually incoherent: "process" describes procedure, and using the clause to protect substantive rights "encompass[es] a substantive right that has nothing to do with 'process.'" Thomas's concurrence extends his McDonald v City of Chicago (2010) concurrence, which made the same argument for Second Amendment incorporation. Timbs's Thomas concurrence is more developed than McDonald's on the historical case for the Excessive Fines right specifically and on the consequences of Slaughter-House's error for the modern incorporation enterprise.

### Dissent

No dissent on the record. The judgment was unanimous (9-0) to vacate and remand. Thomas's concurrence in the judgment is methodologically a sharp break from the majority but is not a dissent: he agrees on the outcome (incorporation) and on the scope of the Excessive Fines right. The doctrinal divergence is entirely over which constitutional clause carries the incorporation load.

## Hypothetical Applications

**Hypothetical 1 (same-side, disproportionate home forfeiture).** State seeks to forfeit a defendant's $425,000 family home after a single-sale marijuana distribution conviction. The statutory maximum fine for the underlying offense is $25,000, and the defendant acquired the home with lawful wages before the offense. Excessive Fines Clause applies under Timbs. On the proportionality question left for remand in Timbs itself, the seventeen-to-one ratio of forfeiture to statutory fine, combined with the home's lawful-source funding, makes this a strong Austin-style punitive forfeiture claim. Result: forfeiture fails the Excessive Fines analysis.

**Hypothetical 2 (same-side, wealth-calibrated fine).** A state adopts a traffic statute calibrating fines to defendant's adjusted gross income, capped at 5% of AGI. A defendant with $2 million in annual income is fined $100,000 for a speeding infraction that carries a base fine of $500 for everyone below the calibration threshold. Timbs's incorporation of the Clause reaches state fine regimes. The two-hundred-to-one ratio of the calibrated fine to the baseline statutory fine for the same conduct, without a corresponding escalation in culpability or harm, is grossly disproportionate in the Austin sense. Result: as-applied challenge succeeds.

**Hypothetical 3 (opposite-side, pure proceeds forfeiture).** State forfeits $75,000 in cash seized from defendant's residence that defendant stipulates were the proceeds of heroin sales, with no overlap with any lawful source. The Excessive Fines Clause applies under Timbs, but Austin and its progeny confirm that forfeiture of actual proceeds of the offense is remedial rather than punitive: the state is recovering ill-gotten gains, not exacting a punitive levy. Result: forfeiture survives because the action is not within the Clause's operative scope.

**Hypothetical 4 (opposite-side, fine inside statutory range).** A defendant is fined $8,500 for an offense carrying a $10,000 statutory maximum, following a conviction for conduct at the typical severity level the legislature anticipated when setting the range. The Clause applies, but the fine is within the range the legislature established, tracks the typical severity of the offense, and bears no signal of arbitrariness or revenue-extraction motive. Result: fine survives.

**Hypothetical 5 (fence-sitter, mixed administrative penalty).** EPA-equivalent state environmental agency imposes a $400,000 civil penalty on a small business for repeated permit violations. The agency labels the penalty "remedial" because it tracks estimated damage to public waterways and avoided-compliance costs, but the penalty also exceeds estimated damages by a factor of three and is pitched explicitly at deterrence. Austin asks whether the exaction is at least partially punitive. The three-to-one ratio above estimated damages, combined with the deterrence rationale the agency itself invokes, makes a strong case for partial punitive character. If the Clause applies, the proportionality question turns on whether the deterrence increment is grossly disproportionate to the gravity of the violations. Result is contingent on fact-specific proportionality analysis; Timbs opens the door, but Austin's partially-punitive line and the as-applied proportionality inquiry do not resolve automatically.

## Critique

**Progressive critique.** Timbs is an easy incorporation case. The historical record, from Magna Carta through state constitutions at Reconstruction, leaves no plausible room for the argument that the Excessive Fines Clause is not fundamental or deeply rooted, and the unanimous vote reflects that. The more consequential doctrinal work was deferred: the Court said the Clause applies and Austin governs, but it left proportionality for remand and said nothing about the outer limits of partially punitive civil in rem forfeiture. Post-Timbs litigation has accordingly focused on whether specific forfeiture regimes count as at least partially punitive, and on what counts as grossly disproportionate. A second progressive angle: Timbs is also a quiet indictment of modern fines-and-fees regimes that function as regressive revenue generation, and the Ginsburg opinion's framing of the Clause as a constant shield against revenue-producing power signals that the Court is aware of that broader concern even if it did not reach it.

**Originalist critique.** The Thomas and Gorsuch concurrences confirm that the Court continues to route incorporation through the Due Process Clause rather than the Privileges or Immunities Clause even when the historical record lines up especially well with the P&I path. Thomas's concurrence extends his McDonald position and is more developed on the substantive-due-process critique: the textual objection that "process" cannot bear substantive weight is not answered by any opinion in Timbs, and the majority's silence on clause-selection arguably papers over the tension. Gorsuch's shorter concurrence functions as a bookmark inviting litigants to tee up the P&I question in a case where clause-selection would actually change the outcome. The remaining unincorporated Bill of Rights protections (grand jury indictment under Hurtado, civil jury under Minneapolis & St. Louis R. Co. v. Bombolis, and in some readings excessive bail) are now the likely venues for that argument.

**Scholarly angle.** Timbs completes the modern selective incorporation arc for criminal-process protections with unusual doctrinal thinness: because the answer is overdetermined, Ginsburg's opinion needs to say relatively little to reach the result. The cost is that the opinion leaves the harder doctrinal work (the scope of Austin, the contours of as-applied proportionality, and the clause-selection debate) for another day.

## Key Points

- Incorporates the Eighth Amendment's Excessive Fines Clause against the States through the Fourteenth Amendment Due Process Clause.
- Restates the modern incorporation test: a right is incorporated if it is fundamental to our scheme of ordered liberty or deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition.
- Confirms McDonald's no-daylight principle: incorporated rights apply with full federal force against the States.
- The inquiry is whether the right guaranteed, not each particular application of that right, is fundamental or deeply rooted; this keeps open as-applied questions about civil forfeiture while settling the incorporation question.
- Austin v United States (1993) governs: civil in rem forfeiture falls within the Clause when at least partially punitive.
- Thomas and Gorsuch concurrences press the Privileges or Immunities alternative, continuing the McDonald-era debate and keeping the Slaughter-House revival question alive.
- Functionally completes the modern incorporation arc for criminal-process protections; the remaining unincorporated Bill of Rights protections (grand jury indictment, civil jury, excessive bail in some readings, Third Amendment quartering) are a short list.

## Connections

**Doctrinal families:** [[Topics/Incorporation Doctrine|Incorporation Doctrine]], [[Topics/Selective Incorporation]], [[Excessive Fines Clause]], [[Eighth Amendment]], [[Fourteenth Amendment Due Process]], [[Topics/Privileges or Immunities|Privileges or Immunities]], [[Topics/Substantive Due Process|Substantive Due Process]]. The [[Topics/Privileges or Immunities|P or I topic page]] treats the Thomas and Gorsuch concurrences here as the most developed contemporary revival briefs; the [[Topics/Substantive Due Process|SDP topic page]] frames Timbs as the unanimous modern application of the Duncan test.

**Prior cases:** [[Cases/Barron v Baltimore (1833)|Barron v Baltimore (1833)]] is the antebellum baseline the whole Timbs exercise presupposes: without Marshall's 1833 holding that the Bill of Rights does not bind the States, there would be no incorporation question to answer. Thomas's and Gorsuch's Privileges or Immunities concurrences frame Timbs as the latest chapter in the Reconstruction-era project to overturn Barron through the right clause. [[Cases/Palko v Connecticut (1937)]] supplied the original fundamental-to-ordered-liberty formulation. [[Cases/Duncan v Louisiana (1968)]] refined the test into the modern fundamental-in-the-American-scheme phrasing that Ginsburg's opinion tracks. [[Cases/McDonald v City of Chicago (2010)]] is the direct methodological predecessor and supplies the no-daylight principle Ginsburg quotes. [[Austin v United States (1993)]] supplies the partially-punitive test for civil in rem forfeiture that Timbs imports wholesale.

**Contrast on clause-selection.** Ginsburg's majority routes incorporation through the Due Process Clause, following McDonald and every Palko-Duncan descendant. Thomas's concurrence in the judgment re-presses the argument that Privileges or Immunities is the correct vehicle, that Slaughter-House (1873) misread the Clause, and that the substantive-due-process edifice built on top of that misreading is textually incoherent. Gorsuch's shorter concurrence flags the same reservation as a bookmark. The clause-selection debate is doctrinally dormant so long as Due Process and P&I produce the same incorporation result, but it remains live for any protection where the two paths would diverge.

**Contrast on scope-of-the-right.** Indiana argued that even if the Clause is incorporated, it covers only in personam criminal fines, not civil in rem forfeiture. Ginsburg rejected that reading by relying on Austin and by framing the incorporation inquiry as whether the right guaranteed, not each particular application of that right, is fundamental or deeply rooted. This is the same right-guaranteed-in-general framing that runs from Duncan through McDonald; Timbs continues the line.

**Forward connections.** Timbs supplies the analytic template for any future incorporation question still on the table (grand jury indictment, civil jury, Third Amendment quartering). It also controls the state-level civil-forfeiture docket, since the remand disposition makes Indiana the first test case for post-Timbs as-applied proportionality review.

**Privileges or Immunities revival thread.** [[Cases/Saenz v Roe (1999)|Saenz v Roe (1999)]] is the upstream substantive P or I holding that Thomas and Gorsuch implicitly rely on when they argue the Clause remains doctrinally alive enough to carry incorporation. Saenz used the Clause to strike down California's durational residency scheme twenty years before Timbs and showed that the Court will reach for the Clause for a narrow, well-defined national-citizenship right without purporting to overrule Slaughter-House. Timbs completes the revival arc: Thomas's concurrence here is the most developed originalist argument that Slaughter-House should be overruled outright, and it draws on the same Reconstruction-era historical record Stevens partially credited in Saenz. The three cases together chart the P or I Revival Pattern catalogued on the [[Topics/Privileges or Immunities|Privileges or Immunities]] page: Slaughter-House's narrow 1873 reading, Saenz's 1999 inside-the-framework exception, and the Timbs-and-McDonald Thomas-Gorsuch revival briefs that target the framework itself.
