# Ashcroft v Free Speech Coalition (2002)

## Memory Jogger

> The CPPA's prohibition on virtual child pornography is facially unconstitutional because it bans a substantial amount of protected expression that neither depicts real children being abused nor meets the Miller obscenity test.

---

## Facts

The Child Pornography Prevention Act of 1996 (CPPA) extended federal child pornography prohibitions beyond material produced using real children to cover: (1) § 2256(8)(B) — any visual depiction that "is, or appears to be, of a minor engaging in sexually explicit conduct," capturing both "youthful adult pornography" and purely computer-generated "virtual child pornography" produced without actual children; and (2) § 2256(8)(D) — material that "conveys the impression" it depicts a minor engaging in sexually explicit conduct. The Free Speech Coalition, a trade association of the adult entertainment industry, along with a photographer, a painter, and an author, filed a pre-enforcement facial challenge, arguing the CPPA criminalized a substantial amount of protected speech because it extended far beyond material produced by actually exploiting children.

---

## Procedural History

The district court granted the government's motion for summary judgment, upholding the CPPA. The Ninth Circuit reversed in part, holding that § 2256(8)(B) and § 2256(8)(D) were facially overbroad. The government petitioned for certiorari, which the Supreme Court granted to resolve a circuit split on the constitutionality of the "appears to be" and "conveys the impression" provisions.

## Holding

Sections 2256(8)(B) and 2256(8)(D) of the CPPA are facially unconstitutional. The "appears to be" provision is overbroad because it prohibits a substantial amount of speech — including literary adaptations, mainstream films, and adult films using adult actors — that records no crime, creates no victims by its production, and is not necessarily obscene under *Miller*. Virtual child pornography is not "intrinsically related" to the sexual abuse of children in the way actual child pornography is under *Ferber*. The government may not suppress lawful speech as a means to suppress unlawful speech, and protected speech does not become unprotected merely because it resembles unprotected speech.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion (Kennedy, J.)

The majority grounds the decision in two pillars. First, the *Ferber* exception to First Amendment protection rests on a direct link between the speech and the criminal abuse of real children: the "intrinsic relation" between the material and harm exists because the pornography is the record of the crime. Virtual pornography that records no crime and creates no victims lacks that intrinsic relation; the causal link to actual abuse is contingent and indirect. Second, the CPPA fails the *Miller* obscenity test independently because it prohibits works regardless of whether they have serious literary, artistic, political, or scientific value — a well-known film depicting teenage sexuality or a literary adaptation of *Romeo and Juliet* could fall within the statute's reach. Under First Amendment doctrine, "the Government may not suppress lawful speech as the means to suppress unlawful speech," and redeeming value must be judged by considering the work as a whole. The § 2256(8)(D) "conveys the impression" provision is separately invalid because it turns on how speech is marketed rather than what it depicts, making it substantially overbroad beyond even Ginzburg's pandering rationale.

## Key Points

- The *Ferber* exception is grounded in the direct relationship between the material and actual child abuse; where no real children are exploited, *Ferber*'s categorical exception does not apply.
- The government's argument that virtual pornography "whets the appetite" of pedophiles is rejected as "the mere tendency of speech to encourage unlawful acts," which is an insufficient basis for suppression under Brandenburg.
- The "appears to be" formulation is a proxy for content, not conduct, and sweeps in mainstream Hollywood films and literary adaptations.
- *Ashcroft* is subsequently narrowed by the Court's upholding of the PROTECT Act's pandering provisions in *United States v. Williams* (2008), which criminalizes offers to provide child pornography regardless of whether the material is real.
