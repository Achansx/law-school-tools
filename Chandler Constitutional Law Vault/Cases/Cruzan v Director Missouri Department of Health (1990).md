# Cruzan v Director Missouri Department of Health (1990)

## Memory Jogger

> The Court assumed without deciding that the Due Process Clause protects a competent person's liberty interest in refusing unwanted medical treatment, but upheld Missouri's requirement of clear and convincing evidence of an incompetent patient's prior wishes before authorizing withdrawal of life-sustaining nutrition and hydration -- the first significant "right to die" ruling.

---

## Facts

Nancy Cruzan was rendered permanently unconscious following a 1983 automobile accident. She was maintained in a persistent vegetative state by artificial nutrition and hydration at a Missouri state hospital. Her parents, as co-guardians, sought judicial authorization to terminate the artificial feeding, which would result in Nancy's death. The hospital refused to honor the request without court approval. The Cruzans presented evidence that Nancy had told a housemate she would not want to continue living in a vegetative state, but Missouri courts required clear and convincing evidence of her specific prior wishes. The Missouri Supreme Court held that the evidence of Nancy's preferences was insufficient under the state's evidentiary standard and denied the petition. The case raised the question whether the Due Process Clause or any constitutional provision gave the Cruzans the right to terminate their daughter's medical treatment over the state's objection.

---

## Procedural History

The Missouri trial court initially authorized withdrawal of nutrition and hydration, finding by clear and convincing evidence that Cruzan would have chosen to remove life support. The Missouri Supreme Court reversed, holding that an incompetent's right to refuse treatment could be exercised by a surrogate only upon clear and convincing evidence of the patient's specific prior wishes, and that the trial court's finding was insufficient. The U.S. Supreme Court affirmed the Missouri Supreme Court, upholding the clear-and-convincing-evidence requirement, by a 5-4 vote.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority (5):** Rehnquist, C.J. (author), joined by White, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy
- **Concurrence:** O'Connor (writes separately to emphasize that artificially delivered nutrition is medical treatment; open to surrogate decision-making)
- **Concurrence:** Scalia (would avoid any constitutional right; states should resolve these questions)
- **Dissent:** Brennan (joined by Marshall, Blackmun) (Missouri's standard impermissibly overrides the individual's liberty interest)
- **Dissent:** Stevens (state interest in "life" cannot override deeply personal liberty interests)

---

## Holding

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment protects a liberty interest in refusing unwanted medical treatment -- the Court assumed this without definitively deciding it. Even so, Missouri's requirement of clear and convincing evidence of an incompetent patient's prior wishes before a surrogate may authorize withdrawal of life-sustaining nutrition and hydration is constitutionally permissible. A state may legitimately err on the side of life when a patient cannot speak for herself. Missouri's evidentiary standard is reasonably designed to protect incompetent patients from erroneous decisions made by surrogates whose interests may conflict with the patient's own.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion (Rehnquist, C.J.)

Chief Justice Rehnquist grounded the majority's analysis in the common-law tradition of informed consent, which has long recognized that a competent person has the right to refuse even life-saving medical treatment. Extending that principle, the majority assumed -- without definitively holding -- that the Due Process Clause embodies a liberty interest in declining unwanted treatment even when life-sustaining. The crucial pivot, however, was from the competent patient to the incompetent one. Missouri could not know what Nancy herself would have chosen, and the state's interest in protecting incompetent persons from decisions that do not reflect their actual wishes justified its clear-and-convincing-evidence requirement. Rehnquist emphasized that the state's interest in preserving human life is substantial and that the Constitution does not compel states to adopt an any-evidence or substituted-judgment standard rather than a demanding evidentiary threshold.

## Key Points

- First major Supreme Court case to directly address the "right to die"; the Court assumed (but did not definitively hold) a liberty interest in refusing unwanted medical treatment
- The critical doctrinal move is the competent/incompetent distinction: a state may impose demanding evidentiary requirements on surrogate decision-making even if it may not override a competent patient's refusal
- Missouri's clear-and-convincing-evidence standard is the minimum the state may require; states may adopt more permissive standards without constitutional compulsion
- O'Connor's concurrence is often read as the operative statement on surrogacy: states may be required to give effect to some forms of prior competent expression
- Directly precedes Glucksberg: the assumed right to refuse treatment is the closest analog to a right to die, but Glucksberg held the extension to assisted suicide does not follow
