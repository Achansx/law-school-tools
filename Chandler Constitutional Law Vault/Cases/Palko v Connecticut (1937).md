# Palko v Connecticut (1937)

## Memory Jogger

> Cardozo's opinion supplies the theoretical scaffolding for selective incorporation. A Bill of Rights guarantee binds the states only if it is implicit in the concept of ordered liberty. On that test double jeopardy protection fails (per 1937 doctrine) and Connecticut may retry Palko after a successful prosecution appeal. Duncan later reformulates the test and Benton v Maryland (1969) later overrules the specific holding, but the framework Cardozo announces drives incorporation analysis for the next three decades.

---

## Facts

Frank Palka (the reporter's misspelling gave the case its name) was charged with first-degree murder for killing two police officers in Fairfield, Connecticut, in September 1935. The jury returned a verdict of second-degree murder, which under Connecticut law carried life imprisonment rather than the death penalty. Connecticut's 1886 statute permitted the State, with the trial court's permission, to appeal criminal judgments on the same terms as a defendant. The State appealed, arguing that the trial judge's evidentiary rulings and jury instructions were legal errors. The Connecticut Supreme Court of Errors agreed, set aside the conviction, and ordered a new trial. At the second trial Palka was convicted of first-degree murder and sentenced to death.

Palka challenged the statute on federal constitutional grounds. If prosecuted in federal court, the Fifth Amendment's Double Jeopardy Clause would have barred a second trial on the same charge. Palka argued that the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause extended the same protection to state prosecutions. The Connecticut Supreme Court affirmed. The United States Supreme Court granted review to decide whether the Fourteenth Amendment incorporates the double jeopardy protection of the Fifth Amendment.

---

## Procedural History

Convicted of second-degree murder in Connecticut Superior Court. State appealed under a 1886 Connecticut statute. The Connecticut Supreme Court of Errors reversed and ordered a new trial. Retrial resulted in a first-degree murder conviction and a death sentence, affirmed by the Connecticut Supreme Court. The United States Supreme Court granted certiorari to address the incorporation question.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority:** Cardozo, J., joined by Hughes, C.J., Brandeis, Stone, Roberts, Sutherland, McReynolds, and Black, JJ.
- **Concurrence:** None
- **Dissent:** Butler, J. (without opinion)

**Judgment:** 8-1 affirming; double jeopardy not incorporated. (Overruled on the specific double jeopardy question by Benton v Maryland, 395 U.S. 784 (1969).)

---

## Holding

The Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause does not automatically incorporate all guarantees of the Bill of Rights against the states. It absorbs only those immunities that are implicit in the concept of ordered liberty, meaning principles of justice so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental. Under that test, the Fifth Amendment's protection against double jeopardy is not fundamental as applied to Connecticut's limited prosecutorial appeal, because the State is not subjecting the accused to a multitude of cases with accumulated trials but merely seeking a trial free from substantial legal error. Connecticut's statute therefore does not violate due process, and Palka's conviction stands.

---

## Analysis

### Majority (Justice Cardozo)

Cardozo frames the question as which elements of the Bill of Rights, originally directed at the federal government, are absorbed by the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause. He answers with a gradient. Some rights (freedom of thought, speech, and religion, the matrix of other freedoms) have been recognized as fundamental and therefore binding on the states. Others (trial by jury, grand jury indictment, the privilege against self-incrimination) have not, because a fair and enlightened system of justice can exist without them. The organizing principle is whether the right is implicit in the concept of ordered liberty or a principle so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental. Applying this test, Cardozo concludes that double jeopardy protection, at least as against Connecticut's narrow right to appeal for substantial legal error, is not fundamental. Palka is not being worn out by a multitude of prosecutions; he is being given one trial free from error.

### Concurrence

No concurring opinion was filed. Midpage confirms the judgment was announced as a single opinion of the Court with Butler noted as a silent dissent.

### Dissent

Justice Butler dissented without opinion. The only record is the terse line "Mr. Justice Butler dissents" following the judgment. The substance must be inferred from context. Butler was one of the Four Horsemen, consistently the most protective of individualized due process against state procedural experimentation in the 1930s (compare his opinion for the Court in Morehead v New York ex rel Tipaldo the preceding Term). A fair reconstruction is that Butler read the Connecticut statute as permitting precisely the kind of cumulative prosecutorial pressure the Double Jeopardy Clause was meant to forbid, and would have applied a fundamental-fairness floor regardless of whether the Clause itself was incorporated. Later dissents framing the majority's outcome critically (for example, Black's Adamson v California (1947) dissent and the Court's own 1969 opinion in Benton v Maryland overruling Palko on the specific double jeopardy point) vindicated Butler's intuition without relying on his unrecorded reasoning.

## Hypothetical Applications

**Same-side 1: Free exercise of religion in a 1938 state prosecution.** A state charges a Jehovah's Witness door-canvasser under a municipal peddling ordinance that a neighboring jurisdiction has applied only against religious solicitors. Under Palko, free exercise sits squarely inside what Cardozo calls the matrix of nearly every other form of freedom; Cantwell v Connecticut (1940) would formally incorporate the Free Exercise Clause two years later on exactly this reasoning. Same-side: the right passes the ordered-liberty test because freedom of conscience is implicit in our constitutional tradition.

**Same-side 2: Coerced confession obtained by state police through whipping.** A state trial admits a confession obtained by physical torture. Even in 1937, this would fail Palko's test and bind the states through due process. Brown v Mississippi (1936), decided the previous Term, treated such confessions as violating due process without any need for incorporation of the Fifth Amendment; Palko's ordered-liberty framework confirms that the right against coerced confessions is so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental.

**Opposite-side 1: Grand jury indictment for a state felony.** A state prosecutes a defendant for felony burglary by information rather than grand jury indictment. Hurtado v California (1884) had already held that grand jury indictment is not required of the states, and Palko expressly endorses that result. Under Cardozo's list, grand jury indictment is a feature of Anglo-American practice but is not implicit in the concept of ordered liberty; a fair and enlightened system of justice can exist without it. Fails the test.

**Opposite-side 2: Self-incrimination privilege in a civil regulatory proceeding.** A state administrative agency compels a witness to answer questions on pain of contempt. In 1937, Twining v New Jersey (1908) had squarely held that the Fifth Amendment's self-incrimination privilege did not bind the states, and Palko uses Twining to illustrate the other side of the ordered-liberty line. The right fails until Malloy v Hogan (1964) overrules Twining and incorporates the privilege. Palko's framework categorizes self-incrimination with grand jury indictment as procedural detail rather than fundamental right.

**Fence-sitter: Modern right to present a complete defense.** A state trial judge excludes defense expert testimony on eyewitness-identification unreliability under a strict state evidence rule. Is the right to present a defense implicit in the concept of ordered liberty? Cardozo's framing is indeterminate. The general right to be heard sits near the matrix; the specific right to present any expert testimony looks more like procedural detail. A modern court would likely incorporate it through Chambers v Mississippi (1973) and the broader due-process-as-fair-trial tradition, but Palko alone does not resolve the specific application and its ordered-liberty test invites exactly this kind of judicial discretion (a chief critique below).

---

## Critique

**Progressive critique.** The ordered-liberty test is doctrinally circular and democratically unaccountable. Cardozo's gradient asks whether a right is fundamental, but supplies no criterion beyond the Court's own intuition about what a fair and enlightened system of justice requires. The result is a rights-expansion pace the Court controls by itself, allowing antebellum federalism to outlive the Reconstruction Amendments by three decades longer than total incorporation would have tolerated. Benton v Maryland (1969) confirmed the critique by overruling Palko's specific holding on the ground that double jeopardy is in fact fundamental: the 1937 Court simply misapplied its own test. The deeper progressive point is that the test's subjectivity made the interwar Court's preferences look like constitutional reasoning, and the same move later produced Lochner-era substantive due process when the gradient cut the other way. See Akhil Reed Amar, The Bill of Rights: Creation and Reconstruction (1998) (arguing for refined incorporation rooted in original meaning rather than judicial intuition).

**Originalist critique.** Cardozo's framework compounds the Slaughter-House error by routing incorporation through Due Process rather than Privileges or Immunities. That move was already criticized when Palko was decided (Justice Harlan the elder had dissented in Hurtado along these lines) and becomes Justice Thomas's central thesis in McDonald v City of Chicago (2010) and Timbs v Indiana (2019). Originalist objection runs in two steps: (1) the Fourteenth Amendment's framers intended the Privileges or Immunities Clause to be the operative incorporation vehicle, and (2) substantive due process is a textually incoherent category because "process" describes procedure, not substance. Palko's selective Due Process incorporation is therefore doubly wrong: it picks and chooses among rights the framers intended to apply wholesale, and it does so through a clause that cannot support substantive protection. Cardozo's gradient also entrenches the substantive-due-process tradition that later produces Griswold, Roe, and Obergefell, which originalists see as the predictable consequence of the 1937 wrong turn.

## Key Points

- Establishes the selective-incorporation framework that governed Fourteenth Amendment doctrine from 1937 until Duncan reformulated the test in 1968.
- The test: is the right implicit in the concept of ordered liberty, or a principle of justice so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental?
- First Amendment freedoms (speech, press, religion, assembly) pass; procedural criminal rights like jury trial, grand jury indictment, and self-incrimination fail the 1937 analysis.
- Double jeopardy is held not fundamental on these facts because Connecticut seeks only one trial free from substantial legal error, not wearing down by repeated prosecution.
- Overruled on the specific double-jeopardy question by Benton v Maryland (1969), which incorporated the Double Jeopardy Clause; Palko's incorporation framework itself persists until refined by Duncan.
- Clause-shift significance: Cardozo routes incorporation through Due Process rather than Privileges or Immunities, silently cementing the Slaughter-House workaround and seeding the substantive-due-process tradition.

Malloy v Hogan (1964) overrules Adamson on the Fifth Amendment question, and Duncan (1968) replaces Palko's "implicit in the concept of ordered liberty" formulation with "fundamental to the American scheme of justice," but the doctrinal debate begins here, in the Black-Frankfurter duel that Adamson stages.
