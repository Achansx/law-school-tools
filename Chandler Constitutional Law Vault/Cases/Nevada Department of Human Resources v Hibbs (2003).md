# Nevada Department of Human Resources v Hibbs (2003)

## Memory Jogger

> FMLA's family-care leave provision validly abrogates state sovereign immunity because sex discrimination triggers heightened scrutiny — giving Congress more room to legislate prophylactically — and the legislative record documented a pervasive pattern of states perpetuating gender-based stereotypes about caregiving roles.

---

## Facts

William Hibbs was employed by the Nevada Department of Human Resources. His wife suffered a serious medical condition requiring care, and Hibbs requested intermittent leave under the Family and Medical Leave Act of 1993 (FMLA). The FMLA requires covered employers, including states, to grant eligible employees up to twelve weeks of unpaid leave per year for family care, including care of a spouse with a serious health condition. Nevada initially approved Hibbs's leave requests but eventually threatened termination when Hibbs did not return to work, and ultimately fired him.

Hibbs sued in federal court seeking money damages under the FMLA. The FMLA expressly provides that states are "employers" for purposes of the Act and that state employees may sue for violations. Nevada asserted Eleventh Amendment immunity. The central constitutional question was whether Congress had validly abrogated state sovereign immunity by enacting the FMLA's family-care provisions under Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment.

The FMLA's legislative history documented that states had historically offered generous leave to female employees for childbirth and childcare but minimal leave to male employees, thus reinforcing gender stereotypes and entrenching women in domestic roles while disadvantaging them in the workforce.

---

## Procedural History

The district court dismissed Hibbs's claims, holding Congress lacked power to abrogate Nevada's sovereign immunity under the FMLA. The Ninth Circuit reversed, finding a valid Section 5 abrogation. The Supreme Court granted certiorari to resolve the question, given the circuit conflict and the importance of the FMLA's scope.

## Holding

State employees may recover money damages in federal court against their state employers for violations of the FMLA's family-care provision. The provision constitutes a valid exercise of Congress's power under Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment to abrogate state sovereign immunity, because sex discrimination is subject to heightened scrutiny, Congress documented a pattern of state-perpetuated gender discrimination in family-leave policies, and the FMLA's family-leave requirement is congruent and proportional to that constitutional harm.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Chief Justice Rehnquist — the same author who had limited congressional enforcement power in City of Boerne, Florida Prepaid, and Garrett — wrote for the majority upholding the FMLA abrogation. The key distinguishing factor was the level of constitutional scrutiny. Unlike disability (rational basis, as in Garrett), sex classifications trigger intermediate scrutiny, meaning the constitutional floor that Congress enforces is higher, and Congress therefore has broader prophylactic authority under Section 5.

The majority reviewed the FMLA's legislative record and found it sufficient. Congress had heard extensive testimony documenting that states routinely offered paid maternity leave to women but denied equivalent family-care leave to men, reinforcing the stereotype that women are the primary caregivers for family members. This conduct, when undertaken by states, was itself constitutionally suspect under the Equal Protection Clause's prohibition on sex stereotyping. Because the FMLA's family-care provision targeted conduct that could itself violate the Equal Protection Clause, the Act was congruent and proportional to the identified constitutional harm. The majority also noted that prophylactic legislation is permissible when enforcing provisions subject to heightened scrutiny.
