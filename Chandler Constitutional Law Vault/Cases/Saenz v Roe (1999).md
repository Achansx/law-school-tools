# Saenz v Roe (1999)

## Memory Jogger

> California could not limit welfare benefits for new residents to the amount they would have received in their prior state, because the Privileges or Immunities Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment protects the right of newly arrived citizens to be treated like other citizens of the state and does not allow degrees of citizenship based on length of residence.

---

## Facts

In 1992, California enacted Welfare and Institutions Code section 11450.03, a two-tier welfare scheme. Families new to California who had lived in the state for less than twelve months would receive AFDC (later TANF) benefits calibrated to the level of benefits available in their former state of residence, rather than the standard California payment. The purpose was to deter welfare migration and produce fiscal savings. Implementation was delayed by federal waiver litigation until Congress enacted the Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act of 1996 (PRWORA), 42 U.S.C. section 604(c), which expressly authorized states to condition TANF benefits on duration of residence. California then activated section 11450.03 in April 1997.

Three plaintiffs challenged the scheme. Brenda Roe had moved to California from Oklahoma in 1997 and was pregnant; under the challenged statute she would receive the Oklahoma rate of 307 dollars rather than the California rate of 456 dollars per month. Anna Doe and Thomas Doe had relocated from Louisiana and Washington, respectively. They represented a class of new California residents whose benefits would be reduced because of their prior state of residence.

---

## Procedural History

The District Court for the Eastern District of California issued a preliminary injunction barring enforcement of the statute, concluding that the duration-based classification likely violated the right to travel recognized in Shapiro v Thompson (1969) and its progeny. The Ninth Circuit affirmed. After PRWORA was enacted, California argued that Congress's express authorization cured any constitutional defect. The Supreme Court granted certiorari and affirmed the injunction, 7-2.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority:** Stevens (joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer) — 7-2
- **Dissent:** Rehnquist; Thomas (joined by Rehnquist)

---

## Holding

California's durational-residency limitation on welfare benefits violates the Privileges or Immunities Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. The right to travel protects at least three components: (1) the right to enter and leave another state, (2) the right to be treated as a welcome visitor rather than an unfriendly alien when temporarily present in a second state, and (3) the right of a person who elects to become a permanent resident of a new state to be treated like other citizens of that state. The third component is secured specifically by the Privileges or Immunities Clause, and it does not yield even when Congress expressly authorizes the state to enact the discriminatory scheme.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Justice Stevens wrote for the Court. The right to travel has long been recognized as a component of federal citizenship, although it has been traced to various constitutional provisions. Stevens parsed the right into three distinct components and located each in a different textual source. The right to cross state lines stems from the structure of the Union itself. The right to be treated as a welcome visitor when temporarily in another state is protected by the Privileges and Immunities Clause of Article IV. The third right, which controlled the case, is the right of the newly arrived citizen to the same privileges and immunities enjoyed by other citizens of the same state, and that right flows from the Citizenship and Privileges or Immunities Clauses of the Fourteenth Amendment, which equate citizenship with residence and do not permit degrees of citizenship based on length of residence.

California's scheme directly subordinated this third component. Section 11450.03 discriminated among California citizens based solely on where they had lived during the prior twelve months, creating what Stevens called a hierarchy of forty-five subclasses of similarly situated citizens. The state's fiscal justification could not survive strict or near-strict review: the classification was not tailored to bona fide residence verification, and California had other tools (residency verification and anti-fraud procedures) to police genuine establishment of domicile.

Most importantly, the Court rejected California's argument that PRWORA cured the defect. Congress has no Section 5 power to authorize states to violate the Fourteenth Amendment. Section 5 empowers Congress to enforce the Amendment's guarantees, not to dilute or abrogate them. The Amendment's limits on state power are self-executing as against Congress just as against the states.

The doctrinal significance of Stevens's opinion runs deeper than the welfare-benefits result. Since the Slaughter-House Cases (1873) read the Fourteenth Amendment's Privileges or Immunities Clause to protect only a narrow set of federal-citizenship rights (access to seaports, protection on the high seas, petition of the federal government), the Clause had been a constitutional dead letter for over 125 years. Stevens resurrected it, holding that the right of a newly arrived citizen to be treated the same as other citizens of her new state is among the privileges or immunities of national citizenship that the Clause protects from state abridgment, with state residence (secured by the Fourteenth Amendment's Citizenship Clause) serving as the trigger for the new arrival's entitlement. As Stevens put it, the right "is protected not only by the new arrival's status as a state citizen, but also by her status as a citizen of the United States." This is the first majority opinion since Slaughter-House to give the Clause meaningful work, and the opinion is careful not to overrule Slaughter-House or to expand the Clause to swallow other incorporated rights — it simply locates the right to travel's third component there rather than in the Equal Protection Clause.

That locational move matters. Shapiro v Thompson (1969) struck down a similar one-year residency requirement for welfare benefits under equal-protection strict scrutiny, treating the right to travel as a fundamental right that triggered heightened review of classifications penalizing its exercise. Stevens did not disavow Shapiro's result, but he reframed the doctrinal basis: rather than asking whether durational-residency classifications survive strict scrutiny as applied to a fundamental right, the Privileges or Immunities Clause directly forbids degrees of citizenship based on length of residence. That means the relevant analysis is categorical, not tiered. Stevens conspicuously declined to apply any labeled standard of review, instead holding that the classification "is not a permissible classification" at all because the Constitution itself denies states the authority to create subclasses of citizens. This pivot anticipates the doctrinal argument that resurfaces in McDonald v Chicago (2010), where Justice Thomas (concurring) urges the Privileges or Immunities Clause as the proper home for incorporation and substantive constitutional rights.

### Dissent (Rehnquist)

Chief Justice Rehnquist, writing for himself, dissented primarily on doctrinal-history grounds. He argued the Court had misread the Privileges or Immunities Clause: the Clause as historically understood protects federal-citizenship rights, not an undifferentiated right of "new" state citizens to demand immediate equality with long-term residents on every matter the state chooses to distribute. Durational residency requirements, he observed, have been a familiar and tolerated feature of American law across welfare (Starns v Malkerson, 1971, summarily affirmed), in-state tuition (Sturgis v Washington, 1973, summarily affirmed), divorce jurisdiction (Sosna v Iowa, 1975), and voter registration (Rosario v Rockefeller, 1973). A state has a legitimate interest in ensuring that its benefits flow to citizens who have meaningfully attached to the state, not to those who arrive for the benefits and then leave. The California scheme, Rehnquist emphasized, does not deny benefits to new residents at all: it gives them whatever their prior state paid. That distinguishes Shapiro v Thompson (1969), where the Connecticut statute denied welfare entirely for the first year. Rehnquist also warned that the majority's reasoning threatens to convert the Privileges or Immunities Clause into a constitutionalized affirmative welfare right, because any cost-saving state classification that touches newcomers will now trigger a categorical no-subclasses rule not tethered to any recognizable tier of scrutiny. That framework, he argued, is neither constitutionally warranted nor institutionally manageable.

### Dissent (Thomas, joined by Rehnquist)

Justice Thomas, joined by Chief Justice Rehnquist, wrote a separate dissent that simultaneously welcomes the Clause's revival and rejects the majority's use of it. Thomas agreed that the Court's Privileges or Immunities jurisprudence had been "all but read out of the Constitution" by Slaughter-House, and that reinvigorating it is a legitimate project. But the project should be executed according to the Clause's original public meaning as the 39th Congress and the ratifiers understood it in 1868. Thomas drew on Corfield v Coryell (C.C.E.D. Pa. 1823), Justice Washington's antebellum Article IV opinion enumerating fundamental rights (protection by government, personal security, property, contract, access to courts), and on Senator Jacob Howard's May 23, 1866 floor speech introducing the Fourteenth Amendment to the Senate, which treated the Privileges or Immunities Clause as constitutionalizing the Corfield catalog of fundamental civil rights. In Thomas's reading, the Clause protects recognizable common-law fundamental rights — not positive entitlements like graduated welfare payments that are creatures of legislation. He therefore argued the majority invoked the Clause opportunistically, leveraging its textual authority without committing to any principled account of its content. The larger warning: reinvigorating Privileges or Immunities without a defined methodology risks recreating substantive due process under a new textual label, with the same judicial discretion and the same objection that the Clause becomes whatever a majority of Justices happens to find implicit in "citizenship." Thomas's dissent is thus programmatic — he wants the Clause back, but he wants it back with originalist discipline, and he resurfaces the same argument in his McDonald v Chicago (2010) concurrence eleven years later.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

1. **Hypo:** Colorado enacts a state earned-income tax credit that phases in over three years: new residents receive one-third of the full credit in year one, two-thirds in year two, and the full amount in year three. The state's stated rationale is "encouraging long-term residency" and "fiscal prudence." **Why:** Same result as Saenz. The phase-in operates exactly like California's tiered-benefit scheme — citizens are sorted into subclasses based on length of residence, with the recently arrived receiving less favorable treatment than the long-settled despite identical domicile. Stevens's opinion treats "encouraging long-term residency" and fiscal conservation as constitutionally insufficient justifications, and no bona fide residence verification is at stake (Colorado has already confirmed these are bona fide Colorado citizens). The scheme violates the third component of the right to travel.
2. **Hypo:** Virginia conditions its in-state tuition rate at public universities on five years of continuous domicile in Virginia immediately preceding enrollment, as applied to citizens who have unquestionably established Virginia domicile but have resided in-state for less than five years. **Why:** Same result. Saenz expressly reserves "portable benefits" and bona fide residence verification, but this scheme is neither: once the state concedes the student is a bona fide Virginia domiciliary, the five-year clock functions exactly as California's twelve-month clock did — sorting citizens of Virginia into subclasses. The tuition benefit is not portable in the relevant sense (the student cannot take it with her on leaving the state). Under the "no degrees of citizenship" rule, the five-year condition cannot stand as applied to already-domiciled citizens.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

3. **Hypo:** Arizona requires voter registrants to sign an affidavit of domicile (stating they reside in Arizona and intend to remain indefinitely), to provide an Arizona address, and to have lived in the state for at least 29 days prior to the election. The 29-day rule exists so election officials can process registrations and maintain accurate rolls, not to filter out recent movers. **Why:** Different result. Saenz expressly preserves bona fide residence verification and the administrative mechanisms that make it workable. A 29-day rule genuinely aimed at processing registrations is not a "durational residency" classification sorting citizens into subclasses; it is an administrative filter that closes once the voter is registered. Dunn v Blumstein (1972) had already drawn this line in the voting context, and Stevens reaffirms that the majority's holding does not disturb it.
4. **Hypo:** Nevada requires six weeks of residence before permitting a petitioner to file for divorce in Nevada courts. A newly arrived Nevada resident challenges the requirement under Saenz. **Why:** Different result. Divorce jurisdiction is the paradigm "portable benefit" Saenz reserves: the decree the newcomer seeks travels with her (it is a judgment valid nationwide under the Full Faith and Credit Clause), so the state has an unusually strong interest in assuring itself that the petitioner has meaningfully attached to Nevada before issuing a decree that binds other states. Sosna v Iowa (1975) upheld a one-year divorce-residency requirement precisely on this reasoning, and footnote 13 of Saenz preserves Sosna. The portability and out-of-state-binding character of a divorce decree distinguishes it from welfare payments, which are local, non-portable, and bind only the issuing state.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

5. **Hypo:** Georgia creates a merit-based scholarship (HOPE-style) funded from state lottery revenue, with eligibility limited to students who graduated from a Georgia high school. A family that moves to Georgia during senior year challenges the scholarship exclusion after the graduating student is denied eligibility. **Why:** Genuinely hard. On one framing, graduating from a Georgia high school is an achievement-based criterion that merely happens to correlate with residency — the scholarship is conditioned on a prior action (completing a Georgia public-education career), not on present citizenship status. On that framing, Saenz does not apply because no sorting of current citizens into subclasses takes place. On the other framing, the criterion functions as a durational-residency proxy: de facto, only students whose families have lived in Georgia for the four years of high school qualify, and the state cannot do by proxy what Saenz forbids directly. The scholarship's formal neutrality (the criterion is the high school diploma, not the duration of residency) points one way; its predictable incidence (newcomers cannot qualify) points the other. Courts addressing HOPE-type programs have generally upheld them on the achievement framing, but the Saenz-proxy argument remains live where the program's design and legislative history reveal a purpose to reward long-term residency.

## Critique

**Doctrinal critique (Clause revival without methodology).** The most common academic criticism of Saenz is that Stevens resurrected the Privileges or Immunities Clause without building a coherent framework for its future deployment. The opinion announces that the Clause protects the right of newly arrived citizens to equal treatment but says nothing about what other rights the Clause protects, how to identify them, or why this right rather than any other. The silence matters because when McDonald v Chicago (2010) squarely presented the question whether the Privileges or Immunities Clause is the proper home for incorporation of the Bill of Rights against the states, only Justice Thomas was prepared to answer yes, and the plurality stuck with substantive due process to avoid the methodological vacuum Saenz left behind. Commentators including Akhil Amar and Kurt Lash have argued that Saenz wasted a generational opportunity to rebuild the Clause on principled originalist or structural foundations.

**Laurence Tribe's appreciation.** Tribe and other liberal scholars have praised Saenz precisely for the doctrinal move the originalists criticize: it supplies a textually grounded Fourteenth Amendment protection for fundamental rights without relying on the contested substantive-due-process methodology that has drawn sustained originalist attack since Roe v Wade. On this reading, Saenz points the way toward a more textually defensible liberal constitutionalism — one where cases like Obergefell might someday rest on Citizenship/Privileges or Immunities rather than on Fourteenth Amendment liberty. The unrealized potential of this project is part of what makes Saenz a continuing touchstone.

**Rehnquist/Thomas critique (Section 5 should be read more capaciously).** The dissenters argued, though it is not their central ground, that the majority underread Congress's Section 5 enforcement power. On their view, Congress made a considered policy judgment in PRWORA to permit states to experiment with graduated benefits to newcomers as part of comprehensive welfare reform, and that judgment deserves deference akin to what Katzenbach v Morgan (1966) had given Congress. Saenz effectively establishes the opposite proposition — that Section 5 cannot authorize states to do what the Amendment forbids them to do on their own — and it does so categorically. Post-Saenz, the force of Katzenbach v Morgan is reduced to cases where Congress enforces rights against the states rather than authorizing state action that touches those rights, a line Shelby County v Holder (2013) continued to police.

**Federalism / policy critique.** A federalism-sympathetic critique presses that welfare migration was a genuine fiscal problem the majority brushed aside. California's original enactment (1992) was a response to evidence that high-benefit states were receiving disproportionate inflows from low-benefit states, straining budgets and creating race-to-the-bottom pressure on the high-benefit states. The majority treats this concern as rhetorically insufficient, but the empirical literature on welfare migration in the 1990s was contested, and the Court foreclosed a tool (graduated benefits capped to the prior state's level) that avoided the Shapiro problem of denying benefits entirely. Critics have argued that Saenz's categorical rule leaves states with only the crude choice of uniformly generous benefits or uniformly miserly ones, and that constitutionalizing that choice was an overreach the Court did not justify.

## Key Points

- First robust reliance on the Privileges or Immunities Clause since the Slaughter-House Cases (1873) effectively neutered it.
- Disaggregates the right to travel into three textually distinct components, each protected by a different constitutional source.
- Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment does not authorize Congress to validate state laws that would otherwise violate the Amendment. Compare and contrast with the scope of Section 5 discussed in City of Boerne v Flores (1997).
- Reserves bona fide residency requirements and certain portable benefits (divorce jurisdiction, university tuition) from the holding.
- Exam emphasis: this case rewards students who can articulate the three-component framework and locate each component in the right constitutional clause.
