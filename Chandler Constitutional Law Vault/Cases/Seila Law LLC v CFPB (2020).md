# Seila Law LLC v CFPB (2020)

## Memory Jogger

> The single-director Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, with its head removable by the President only for inefficiency, neglect, or malfeasance, violates the separation of powers because a principal officer wielding significant executive authority must remain accountable to the President at will.

---

## Facts

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau was created by Title X of the Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act of 2010 in the aftermath of the 2008 financial crisis. The CFPB is an independent regulatory agency empowered to implement and enforce nineteen federal consumer-protection statutes covering banking, credit, debt collection, mortgage lending, and related industries. It wields broad rulemaking, enforcement, and adjudicative authority, and it is funded not through annual appropriations but through transfers from the Federal Reserve.

Unlike traditional independent regulatory commissions, which are headed by multimember bipartisan boards, the CFPB is led by a single Director. The Director is appointed by the President with the advice and consent of the Senate for a five-year term and, under 12 U.S.C. section 5491(c)(3), may be removed by the President only for "inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance in office."

Seila Law is a California-based law firm that provides debt-relief services. In 2017, the CFPB issued a civil investigative demand requiring Seila Law to produce information and documents in connection with an investigation into potentially unfair, deceptive, or abusive acts by providers of debt-relief services. Seila Law refused to comply, arguing the CFPB's structure violated the Constitution's separation of powers.

---

## Procedural History

The CFPB filed a petition in the U.S. District Court for the Central District of California to enforce the CID. The District Court enforced the demand, rejecting the separation-of-powers challenge. The Ninth Circuit affirmed, relying on Humphrey's Executor v United States (1935) and Morrison v Olson (1988) to uphold the for-cause removal protection. The Supreme Court granted certiorari on the constitutional question and separately asked the parties to brief severability. Because both Seila Law and the CFPB agreed the statute was unconstitutional, the Court appointed former Solicitor General Paul Clement as amicus to defend the judgment below.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority (on constitutional holding):** Roberts (joined by Thomas, Alito, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh) — 5-4
- **Majority (on severability):** Roberts (joined by Alito, Kavanaugh, and Kagan, Ginsburg, Breyer, Sotomayor concurring in the judgment on severability) — 7-2
- **Concurrence/Dissent in part:** Thomas (joined by Gorsuch) — would overrule Humphrey's Executor
- **Dissent:** Kagan (joined by Ginsburg, Breyer, Sotomayor) — would uphold the for-cause provision

---

## Holding

The CFPB's leadership by a single Director removable only for inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance violates the separation of powers. The President's removal power, rooted in Article II's vesting of "the executive Power" in the President, is subject to only two narrow exceptions: (1) for-cause protection for multimember bodies of experts who do not exercise purely executive power (Humphrey's Executor) and (2) tenure protections for certain inferior officers with narrowly defined duties (United States v Perkins; Morrison v Olson). A single principal officer wielding significant executive power falls outside both exceptions. The unconstitutional removal restriction is severable from the rest of the Dodd-Frank Act, so the CFPB may continue to operate with its Director removable at will.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Chief Justice Roberts began from the constitutional baseline that Article II vests "the executive Power" in the President and charges him to "take Care that the Laws be faithfully executed." Because the President cannot personally execute the laws, he must rely on subordinates; the power to remove those subordinates is what renders them accountable to him and, through him, to the electorate. The First Congress settled that principle in the Decision of 1789, and Myers v United States (1926) confirmed it.

Roberts framed the Court's precedents as recognizing only two exceptions to this unrestricted removal power. Humphrey's Executor (1935) upheld a for-cause removal restriction for the five-member Federal Trade Commission, which the Court there described as a body of experts exercising quasi-legislative and quasi-judicial power. Morrison v Olson (1988), along with the older Perkins case, permitted tenure protections for certain inferior officers with narrowly defined duties (there, an independent counsel).

The CFPB fits neither exception. It is led by a single Director, not a multimember body, and that Director wields core executive power: enforcement, rulemaking, and adjudication over a substantial sector of the economy. Giving one insulated official such authority, Roberts reasoned, concentrates executive power in a manner the Framers rejected. The Court declined to construe the statutory removal grounds broadly under constitutional avoidance because doing so would rewrite Congress's work without a foothold in the text.

On severability, the Court held the for-cause provision could be excised from the remainder of Title X without invalidating the agency's other functions. A severability clause and a strong presumption in favor of preserving enacted statutes produced a 7-2 coalition for keeping the CFPB in operation with a Director now removable at will.

Roberts grounds the analysis in three overlapping sources of authority that he treats as dispositive before the case ever reaches modern doctrine. First, the Decision of 1789 (the First Congress's debate over whether the President could remove the Secretary of Foreign Affairs without Senate consent) is treated as a "contemporaneous and weighty evidence" liquidation of constitutional meaning. James Madison's floor speeches, which concluded that "if any power whatsoever is in its nature Executive, it is the power of appointing, overseeing, and controlling those who execute the laws," supply the textual baseline. Second, *Myers v United States* (1926) synthesized the Decision of 1789 with Article II's Vesting Clause and Take Care Clause to hold that the President has a general removal power. Third, *Free Enterprise Fund v PCAOB* (2010) is the precedent Roberts treats as immediate parent: it held that two layers of for-cause removal protection (a PCAOB member removable only for cause by the SEC, whose commissioners were themselves removable only for cause) unconstitutionally insulated executive officers from presidential accountability. Crucially for Seila Law, Free Enterprise Fund articulated the "since 1789" framing and described Humphrey's Executor and Morrison v Olson as recognized exceptions to a general rule of unrestricted removal, not as components of a functional balancing test.

Roberts's analytical move is to characterize this framework as two exceptions to a rule rather than three overlapping precedents. *Humphrey's Executor* (1935) upheld for-cause removal of FTC commissioners, but only because the FTC was a multimember body of experts balanced along partisan lines, performing quasi-legislative and quasi-judicial functions, said not to wield any executive power. *Morrison v Olson* (1988) upheld for-cause protection for the independent counsel, but only for an inferior officer with limited jurisdiction and tenure. The CFPB Director, Roberts concludes, fits neither exception: the Director is a single principal officer wielding vast executive authority, with no partisan-balance requirement, no staggered term, no limited jurisdiction. The majority declines amicus Paul Clement's invitation to expand Humphrey's Executor into a general permission slip for Congress to structure removal protections however it wishes, stating that "we decline to elevate [Humphrey's Executor] into a freestanding invitation for Congress to impose additional restrictions on the President's removal authority."

On standing, Roberts addresses the CFPB's argument that Seila Law suffered no cognizable injury from the removal provision itself because even a Director removable at will would likely have issued the same CID. The majority rejects this counterfactual requirement: when an agency official acts under an unconstitutional tenure structure, the agency's action itself is the injury, and the challenger need not show that a differently-structured agency would have made a different decision. The analysis parallels *Lucia v SEC* (2018) and Free Enterprise Fund on this point and closes off a common defensive move in separation-of-powers cases — arguing that structural flaws are harmless because the same substantive outcome would obtain.

### Concurrence in Part / Dissent in Part (Thomas, joined by Gorsuch)

Justice Thomas, joined by Justice Gorsuch, agreed with the majority's constitutional bottom line but pushed the analysis significantly further. His separate opinion is a direct assault on *Humphrey's Executor* as a surviving precedent. The core problem, Thomas argues, is that the 1935 case rested on a conceptual distinction the Constitution does not draw. Article II vests "the executive Power" in the President; it does not partition executive authority into "purely executive" functions (removable at will) versus "quasi-legislative" and "quasi-judicial" functions (permissibly insulated). The FTC's power to promulgate rules, bring enforcement actions, and adjudicate disputes is executive power in the only constitutionally significant sense: it is the power to enforce the laws of the United States.

Thomas's historical argument is that the "quasi-legislative/quasi-judicial" categories were invented by the *Humphrey's Executor* Court to justify an outcome politically congenial to the New Deal consolidation of administrative authority. Morrison v Olson's "purely executive" gloss compounded the error by suggesting the relevant question is how purely executive the function is, which, again, is a judicial invention rather than a constitutional standard. Thomas would therefore formally overrule Humphrey's Executor and read Morrison as either overruled or confined to its specific facts. On his account, every for-cause removal provision protecting an officer who wields significant executive power is unconstitutional regardless of agency structure. Single-director agencies like the CFPB and multimember commissions like the FTC both violate Article II in precisely the same way; the single-versus-plural distinction the majority adopts is, in Thomas's view, a pragmatic compromise rather than a principled constitutional line.

The concurrence also targets the majority's severability holding. Thomas argues that the CFPB as constitutionally reconstituted (Director removable at will) was not the agency Congress created, and that the Court's severance operation amounted to judicial rewriting of the statute. His preferred remedy would be to deny enforcement of the CID in this case and leave it to Congress to restructure the agency. The methodological disagreement here is important for administrative law doctrine: Thomas and Gorsuch reject the Court's recent pattern of curing structural constitutional defects through severance rather than requiring Congress to reenact. The Thomas-Gorsuch position has since found a wider hearing in *Collins v Yellen* (2021) and the Court's evolving treatment of remedies for separation-of-powers violations.

### Dissent (Kagan, joined by Ginsburg, Breyer, Sotomayor)

Justice Kagan's dissent, joined by Justices Ginsburg, Breyer, and Sotomayor, is built on three overlapping moves: the Necessary-and-Proper anchor, the historical-practice record, and the argument that the single-versus-plural line has no constitutional foundation. The dissent opens with the observation that the Constitution contains no express removal provision; what the Constitution does contain is Article I's Necessary and Proper Clause, which authorizes Congress to "compose the government." Kagan argues that decisions about how to structure administrative agencies — including whether to insulate a single officer or a multimember body — are precisely the sort of structural judgments the Constitution commits to the political branches rather than to the Court. By treating the removal power as a constitutionally mandated rule rather than a default subject to congressional variation, the majority "appropriate[s]" the power delegated to Congress under the Necessary and Proper Clause.

The historical practice section is the dissent's longest. Kagan traces independent-agency structures back to the Second Bank of the United States (1816), the Comptroller of the Currency (1863), the Interstate Commerce Commission (1887), the Federal Reserve (1913), the FTC (1914), and continuing through the New Deal and post-New Deal administrative state. She reads *Myers* not as establishing an unrestricted removal power but as invalidating a specific postmaster statute on narrow grounds that Chief Justice Taft himself modified in later opinions. She cites Madison's principle that "a regular course of practice" can "liquidate" constitutional meaning to argue that the long tradition of for-cause removal protections has settled the constitutional question against the majority's Article II reading. The dissent notes that in 1816, fifteen of twenty-five directors of the Second Bank could not be removed by the President — a far more aggressive insulation than the CFPB Director enjoys.

The dissent's sharpest critique targets the single-versus-plural distinction the majority adopts. Kagan argues the distinction is a "Rinse and Repeat Clause" invention: the Court takes a feature shared by most (but not all) independent agencies and elevates it into a constitutional rule. But the single-versus-plural difference has no Article II pedigree. A multimember commission concentrates authority in a majority of commissioners who can block presidential priorities just as effectively as a single insulated director. Conversely, a single director who needs cooperation from a career civil service, congressional appropriators, and state partners may be more, not less, responsive to presidential preferences than a multimember body. Kagan concludes: "The Constitution does not distinguish between single-director and multimember independent agencies. It instructs Congress, not this Court, to decide on agency design."

The dissent also reads Humphrey's Executor on its own terms as a broad approval of agency independence. Kagan notes that the *Humphrey's Executor* Court's quasi-legislative/quasi-judicial language was expository rather than limiting: the FTC was described that way to distinguish it from the postmaster in *Myers*, not to establish a new category of officers whose insulation is constitutionally permissible. Modern agencies mix executive, legislative, and judicial functions in precisely the way Humphrey's Executor contemplated, and confining the decision to its exact facts (1930s FTC commissioners) amounts to overruling it sub silentio while pretending to preserve it.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

1. **The single-director Consumer Product Safety Commission rewrite.** Congress restructures the CPSC from a five-member commission into a single-director agency headed by an officer removable only for "inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance in office," wielding rulemaking, civil-penalty, and recall authority over a substantial sector of the consumer economy. *Why: the structural features match Seila Law exactly — single principal officer, for-cause removal, significant executive power. Roberts's rule applies directly without any doctrinal extension.*

2. **Single-director FHFA (the actual Collins v Yellen fact pattern).** The Federal Housing Finance Agency is led by a single Director removable only for cause; the FHFA oversees Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac and has broad conservatorship authority over mortgage finance. *Why: *Collins v Yellen* (2021) confirmed this is a straightforward Seila Law application. The FHFA is a single-director agency with executive authority comparable to the CFPB. Any argument that housing finance is somehow "different" from consumer finance fails under Roberts's framework because the relevant feature is the structural combination (single director + for-cause + substantial executive power), not the regulatory subject matter.*

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

3. **Reconstituted CFPB as multimember commission.** Congress amends Dodd-Frank to replace the single Director with a five-member bipartisan commission, staggered terms, partisan-balance requirement, removable only for cause. *Why: the restructured agency fits squarely within *Humphrey's Executor*. Roberts's majority carefully preserved the multimember-body exception, and the dissent's historical analysis would reinforce the outcome. This is precisely the "alternative response" Roberts invites at page 36 of the slip opinion.*

4. **Independent counsel-style inferior officer.** Congress creates a Special Counsel for Cybersecurity, appointed by the Attorney General, with limited jurisdiction (one agency's cyber-crime docket), for a two-year term, removable only for cause. *Why: Morrison v Olson's exception for inferior officers with narrowly defined duties survives Seila Law. Roberts's majority treated Morrison as a preserved precedent for this category of officer. Thomas's concurrence would dissent, but the majority's two-exceptions framework controls.*

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

5. **Single-head agency with only adjudicative functions.** Congress creates a single-director "Consumer Complaints Tribunal" with for-cause removal, authorized only to adjudicate consumer complaints referred by other agencies; no rulemaking, no enforcement initiation, no civil-penalty authority. *Why unclear: (a) the single-director structure satisfies the first Seila Law red flag, but (b) the office lacks the "significant executive power" the majority emphasized. A defender would argue Humphrey's Executor's "quasi-judicial" language applies; a challenger would argue Thomas's concurrence rejects the quasi-judicial exception and that even a single adjudicator exercises executive authority by binding the United States. The outcome probably depends on whether the current Court reads Seila Law's emphasis on "significant" executive power as a threshold requirement (permitting the agency) or whether the single-director/for-cause combination alone triggers invalidity (condemning it).*

## Critique

**Formalist / unitary-executive critique (Calabresi, Prakash, Yoo; Thomas's concurrence as internal critique).** From this vantage, Seila Law was too cautious. The unitary executive thesis holds that Article II's Vesting Clause, read in light of the Decision of 1789 and *Myers*, commits "the executive Power" to the President in a way that forbids any for-cause insulation of officers wielding executive authority. Under this view, the majority's preservation of Humphrey's Executor as a narrow exception is a judicial compromise without constitutional foundation. The formalist critique predicts — and broadly welcomes — a future decision overruling Humphrey's Executor outright, which is what Thomas and Gorsuch ask for here. Calabresi and Yoo's work treats the "quasi-legislative/quasi-judicial" taxonomy as incoherent, because every administrative function is ultimately the execution of statutes and therefore "executive" within Article II. Seila Law's single-director line is, on this account, an administrable proxy but not the real constitutional principle.

**Functionalist / Kagan critique (Strauss; Kagan's academic work; Gillian Metzger).** The counterweight reads Article II's Vesting Clause as a grant of authority to the President within a constitutional scheme that also authorizes Congress to structure the administrative state under the Necessary and Proper Clause. On this view, the Court has no principled basis for identifying single-director-plus-for-cause as the constitutional tripwire; the feature combination is a policy preference, not a textual command. Kagan's 2001 *Harvard Law Review* article "Presidential Administration" (written well before her elevation to the bench) actually defends presidential control of agencies on functional grounds, but her Seila Law dissent treats her earlier position as a policy argument Congress is entitled to weigh, not a constitutional rule. Metzger argues that the majority's categorical approach ignores how actual agency independence operates: for-cause removal is just one of many tools Congress uses to calibrate presidential oversight, and the Court's focus on the removal provision alone produces a distorted picture of agency accountability.

**Historical-practice critique (Manning; Mascott).** Both the majority and dissent invoke historical practice, but they read the same record differently. The formalist camp treats the 1789 debate and Myers as establishing a strong default, with *Humphrey's Executor* as an outlier the Court should not extend. The functionalist camp treats the long unbroken line of independent agencies (Second Bank through the modern regulatory state) as liquidating constitutional meaning in favor of congressional latitude. Manning's "Means of Constitutional Power" framework suggests both camps over-read the historical record: early banks and the Comptroller of the Currency operated in very different constitutional and administrative contexts, and extrapolating from them to modern regulatory agencies requires interpretive work the constitutional text does not dictate. The historical-practice debate thus becomes a proxy for a deeper disagreement about how much weight to give evolving institutional norms in constitutional interpretation.

**Post-Seila doctrinal trajectory.** *Collins v Yellen* (2021) extended Seila Law to the Federal Housing Finance Agency, confirming the rule's reach beyond Dodd-Frank-specific facts. Collins also addressed a remedial question Seila Law left open: whether actions taken by officers subject to unconstitutional removal protection are voidable. The Court answered no, provided the President had not attempted (and been prevented from) removing the officer. This makes the structural flaw largely retrospective rather than ratification-undoing, which reduces the practical bite of the Seila Law rule. *Arthrex v Smith & Nephew* (2021) applied related reasoning to administrative patent judges, using severance to make them at-will removable. The cumulative effect is a Court-driven rebalancing of the administrative state's structural defaults toward presidential control, without the upheaval that overruling Humphrey's Executor would produce. Thomas's dissatisfaction with severance remedies remains a marker of intra-Court disagreement about how much further the project should go.

## Key Points

- Reaffirms but narrows Humphrey's Executor and Morrison v Olson by categorizing them as two discrete exceptions rather than components of a general functionalist balancing test.
- Introduces a structural rule: a single principal officer wielding significant executive power cannot be insulated from presidential removal.
- Severability holding preserves the CFPB's operational existence while restructuring its relationship to the President.
- Sets the doctrinal stage for Collins v Yellen (2021), which extended Seila Law to the Federal Housing Finance Agency and addressed remedial questions about actions taken before severance.
- Exam emphasis: compare and contrast with Humphrey's Executor, Morrison v Olson, Free Enterprise Fund v PCAOB, and Myers v United States.
