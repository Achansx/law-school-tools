# Regents of the University of California v Bakke (1978)

## Memory Jogger

**The Fractured Affirmative Action Decision.** Allan Bakke, a white applicant, was twice rejected from UC Davis Medical School, which reserved 16 of 100 seats for minority applicants. No majority opinion — six separate opinions, two coalitions of four. Justice Powell holds the controlling position alone. His two key holdings: (1) a rigid racial quota is unconstitutional; (2) diversity in higher education is a constitutionally permissible compelling interest that can justify considering race as a "plus" factor in holistic admissions. The Harvard Plan is Powell's model. *Bakke* launched the "diversity rationale" that governed affirmative action doctrine until *Students for Fair Admissions* (2023).

## Facts

- UC Davis Medical School operated a dual admissions program: 84 seats filled through regular admissions process; 16 seats reserved exclusively for minority applicants (defined as Black, Chicano, Asian, or Native American)
- Allan Bakke, a white applicant, was rejected in both 1973 and 1974; his benchmark score significantly exceeded those of minority applicants admitted under the special program
- Bakke sued, alleging the special admissions program violated the Equal Protection Clause and Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964
- California Supreme Court ordered Bakke's admission and struck down the program; UC Regents appealed

## Procedural History

- California trial court: both parties won on different grounds; no order for Bakke's admission; program not struck down
- California Supreme Court: special admissions program violated Equal Protection; ordered Bakke admitted; struck down the program
- U.S. Supreme Court: affirmed in part, reversed in part — Bakke must be admitted; but the state court's holding that race can never be considered in admissions is reversed; race may be considered as one factor in holistic review

## Judicial Votes

- **No majority opinion** — four separate coalitions
- **Stevens bloc (4)** (Stevens, Stewart, Rehnquist, Burger CJ): would decide solely on Title VI; Title VI prohibits the program; would not reach the constitutional question; would affirm Bakke's admission
- **Brennan bloc (4)** (Brennan, White, Marshall, Blackmun): race-conscious admissions to remedy societal discrimination are constitutional under intermediate scrutiny; would reverse the California Supreme Court entirely; the program is valid
- **Powell (1)**: controls the outcome; holds (1) quota system is unconstitutional; (2) diversity is a compelling interest; (3) race may be considered as one "plus" factor in holistic review; affirms Bakke's admission (joined by Stevens bloc); reverses the blanket prohibition on race-conscious admissions (joined by Brennan bloc)
- Net result: Bakke admitted (5-4, Powell + Stevens bloc); racial quota struck down (5-4, Powell + Stevens bloc); race may be a factor (5-4, Powell + Brennan bloc)

## Holding

**(Powell's controlling opinion):**
All racial and ethnic distinctions are inherently suspect and subject to the most exacting judicial scrutiny. The UC Davis special admissions program is unconstitutional because it operates as a rigid racial quota that forecloses all individual competition for 16 seats based solely on race and ethnicity.

However, a university's interest in a diverse student body is a constitutionally sufficient compelling interest to justify the consideration of race as one factor among many in a genuinely individualized, holistic admissions process. Race may be a "plus" in a particular applicant's file, but it may not insulate the applicant from comparison with all other candidates. The "Harvard Plan" — which considers race as a positive factor without setting aside specific seats — is a constitutional model.

Title VI of the Civil Rights Act proscribes only those racial classifications that would violate the Equal Protection Clause, so the constitutional and statutory analyses are coextensive.

## Key Points

- **No majority opinion**: Powell controls the outcome as the swing vote; his opinion sets the governing standard even though no other Justice joined it in full
- **Quotas unconstitutional**: a rigid set-aside of seats available only to minorities forecloses individual competition and violates equal protection (5-4: Powell + Stevens bloc)
- **Diversity is a compelling interest**: the educational benefits of a diverse student body justify race-conscious admissions in a holistic, individualized process (5-4: Powell + Brennan bloc)
- **The Harvard Plan model**: race as a "plus" factor in individualized review; no specific percentage targets; race as one element of a genuinely individualized decision
- **Title VI coextensive with Equal Protection**: Title VI proscribes what the Equal Protection Clause forbids and no more
- **Subsequent history**: *Grutter v. Bollinger* (2003) adopted Powell's diversity rationale for law school admissions; *Gratz v. Bollinger* (2003) struck down Michigan undergrad point system as too mechanical; *Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard* (2023) overruled *Grutter* and effectively ended race-conscious admissions in higher education

## Connections

- [[Cases/Adarand Constructors Inc v Pena (1995)]] — established strict scrutiny for all racial classifications; Bakke's framework for evaluating racial classifications is incorporated into the Adarand/Croson framework
- [[Cases/Grutter v Bollinger (2003)]] — adopted Powell's diversity rationale for law school; held that narrowly tailored race-conscious admissions survive strict scrutiny; overruled by *Students for Fair Admissions* (2023)
- [[Cases/Gratz v Bollinger (2003)]] — struck down Michigan undergrad admissions system that assigned 20 points to minority status; distinguished from Grutter's holistic review
- [[Cases/Students for Fair Admissions v Harvard (2023)]] — overruled Grutter; held race-conscious admissions at Harvard and UNC unconstitutional; effectively repudiated the diversity rationale as a sufficient compelling interest in this context
- [[Richmond v JA Croson Co (1989)]] — established that racial classifications by state/local governments require strict scrutiny; applied separately from Bakke's educational context
