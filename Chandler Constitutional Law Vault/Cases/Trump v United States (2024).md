# Trump v United States (2024)

## Memory Jogger

> A former President has absolute immunity from criminal prosecution for core constitutional acts, at least presumptive immunity for other official acts, and no immunity for unofficial acts.

---

## Facts

Special Counsel Jack Smith obtained a federal grand jury indictment of former President Donald J. Trump in the District of Columbia in August 2023. The four-count indictment alleged that Trump engaged in a criminal conspiracy to overturn the 2020 presidential election results. The charged conduct included pressuring the Department of Justice to investigate unsubstantiated election-fraud claims and to send letters to state officials suggesting fraud, organizing slates of fraudulent electors in swing states, pressuring Vice President Mike Pence to reject or delay the certification of electoral votes on January 6, 2021, and using his public addresses to shape the conduct of the crowd that marched on the Capitol that day.

Trump moved to dismiss the indictment, asserting absolute immunity from criminal prosecution for acts taken while he was President. The question reached the Supreme Court while trial preparations were stayed, presenting whether and to what extent a former President enjoys immunity from federal criminal prosecution for conduct during his term.

---

## Procedural History

The District Court (Chutkan, J.) denied Trump's motion to dismiss, rejecting the claim of presidential immunity from criminal liability. The D.C. Circuit affirmed, holding that whatever immunities a sitting President enjoyed, a former President had no structural immunity from the federal criminal charges alleged in the indictment.

The Supreme Court granted certiorari before final judgment on an expedited schedule. The Court heard argument in April 2024 and issued its decision on July 1, 2024. Chief Justice Roberts wrote for a 6-3 majority, remanding for further proceedings consistent with a newly articulated three-tier immunity framework.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority:** Roberts, C.J. (joined by Thomas, Alito, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh, Barrett) — 6-3
- **Concurrence:** Thomas; Barrett (in part)
- **Dissent:** Sotomayor (joined by Kagan, Jackson); Jackson

---

## Holding

Under the constitutional structure of separated powers, a former President enjoys absolute immunity from federal criminal prosecution for actions within his "conclusive and preclusive constitutional authority." He enjoys at least presumptive immunity from prosecution for all other "official acts," rebuttable only by a showing that prosecution poses no danger of intrusion on executive authority. There is no immunity for "unofficial acts." Evidence of immune official acts may not be admitted at a criminal trial even when the charged conduct is unofficial. Courts may not inquire into presidential motives in distinguishing official from unofficial conduct.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Chief Justice Roberts grounded the immunity framework in Nixon v Fitzgerald's civil-immunity logic and in structural separation-of-powers reasoning. The President stands alone as the sole head of a coordinate branch, so criminal prosecution for official conduct raises unique risks of chilling vigorous execution of the laws and of encouraging politically motivated prosecutions. The majority drew a tripartite framework. First, for acts within the President's "conclusive and preclusive" constitutional powers, such as control over federal prosecutors and the pardon power, immunity is absolute because Congress cannot regulate these functions and courts cannot examine their exercise. Second, for all other official acts, immunity is at least presumptive, with the government bearing the burden to show prosecution would not threaten executive authority. Third, unofficial acts enjoy no immunity. Critically, courts cannot probe motive when classifying conduct, and evidence of immune official acts is barred even in trials of unofficial conduct.

### Concurrence (Thomas)

Justice Thomas joined the majority in full but wrote separately to question whether Special Counsel Jack Smith was lawfully appointed at all. His concurrence developed a textualist Appointments Clause argument: any office wielding the power of a "Hea[d] of Department" or otherwise exercising significant federal authority must be "established by Law," meaning by statute, before the President or a department head can appoint anyone to fill it. Thomas marshaled Lucia v SEC and his own prior writings to argue that Congress has never enacted a statute creating the Special Counsel's office; the Attorney General's regulatory creation cannot substitute for statutory authorization. The concurrence does not decide the question (it was not before the Court) but signals that a future Edmond / Arthrex-style challenge to the Special Counsel's authority is open, providing a roadmap that Judge Cannon would later cite in dismissing the Mar-a-Lago documents prosecution. Thomas's separate writing thus performs a distinct function from the majority's structural-immunity reasoning: it tees up a parallel separation-of-powers vehicle that could moot the immunity question entirely.

### Concurrence (Barrett, in part)

Justice Barrett joined the Court's opinion except for Part III-C, the categorical evidentiary-immunity rule. Her separate writing reframed the Court's "immunity" terminology as shorthand for two narrower propositions: (1) the President can challenge a criminal statute as applied to official acts on as-applied constitutional grounds, and (2) he can obtain interlocutory review of a denial. On the evidentiary question, she pointedly disagreed: excluding "any mention" of the official act associated with a bribe, she argued, "would hamstring the prosecution" because proving the corrupt bargain typically requires showing the act that was bartered. The majority responded directly to her position in footnote 3, conceding that the public record of an official act may be referenced but insisting that the President's own deliberations and motives remain off-limits. Barrett's narrower framing matters analytically: it preserves a path for as-applied prosecution of bribery and similar offenses that the majority's evidentiary bar arguably forecloses, and it rejects the majority's elevation of an evidentiary privilege to a structural component of immunity.

### Dissent (Sotomayor)

Justice Sotomayor, joined by Kagan and Jackson, delivered a sharp dissent built around a single accusation: the majority "has replaced a presumption of equality before the law with a presumption that the President is above the law for all of his official acts." She argued that the tripartite framework operates in practice as near-categorical immunity for any conduct that touches the office. The pleadings-stage exclusion of motive evidence, the categorical evidentiary bar of Part III-C, and the absolute-immunity zone for "conclusive and preclusive" powers (which she read as encompassing any DOJ-related conduct, including the indictment's core allegations) combine to remove every realistic prosecutorial pathway. She illustrated the consequences with hypotheticals that drew widespread attention, including the textbook "law-free zone" image and the example of a President who publicly announces an intent to stop a political rival "no matter what it takes" and then retains a private hitman: under the majority's evidentiary rule, the public statement of premeditated intent could not be introduced. She closed with the line that has since defined the dissent: "With fear for our democracy, I dissent."

### Dissent (Jackson)

Justice Jackson wrote separately to dissect what she called the majority's "paradigm shift" in Presidential accountability. Her opening move was historical: immunity from criminal prosecution, in its purest form, reduces to the maxim "[t]he King can do no wrong," a notion "firmly rejected at the birth of [our] Republic," quoting Clinton v Jones and Blackstone. She built from that anchor a structural argument that conferring immunity does not merely create a defense but exempts a person from the operation of law itself, "creat[ing] a privileged class free from liability for wrongs inflicted or injuries threatened" (quoting Hopkins v Clemson). The accountability the Founders designed, she argued, ran through the criminal law applied evenly to officeholders and citizens alike. The majority's three-tier framework, in her view, repeals that design without text or precedent and replaces it with categorical exemptions calibrated by the Court rather than by Congress. Jackson's contribution is thus less about specific applications than about naming the structural change: she insists the question is not whether the President can be prosecuted for any specific act but whether the criminal law applies to him at all, and she answers that the Constitution's settled answer was yes until July 1, 2024.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

1. **Obstruction charge built on Attorney General consultations.** A former President is indicted for obstruction of an official proceeding based on private discussions with the Attorney General urging that certain political opponents be investigated. Under Trump, this falls inside the "conclusive and preclusive" zone: supervision and direction of federal prosecutors is a core Article II power that Congress cannot regulate and courts cannot examine. Absolute immunity attaches without further factual development, and the evidentiary bar of Part III-C bars the government from introducing the contents of those discussions even in trials of unrelated unofficial conduct. The Trump majority itself directs dismissal of the indictment's DOJ-pressure allegations on this exact reasoning.

2. **Bribery charge tied to a pardon.** A former President is indicted for accepting a private payment in exchange for issuing a pardon. The pardon power is textually committed to the President alone (Art. II, § 2, cl. 1), placing it in the conclusive-and-preclusive bucket. Trump's no-motive-inquiry rule arguably forecloses the prosecution because proving bribery requires probing why the act was taken; Barrett's separate concurrence flags this as the doctrinal cost of the majority's evidentiary rule. Under the controlling Roberts opinion, absolute immunity blocks the prosecution. Under Barrett's framing the result might differ, but her view did not command a majority. Same-side outcome under Trump as written.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

3. **Tax fraud on pre-Presidency business income.** A former President is indicted for falsifying business records and underreporting tax liability for transactions that occurred before he took office. Trump itself confirms the disposition: "there is no immunity" for unofficial acts, and conduct that predates the inauguration cannot plausibly be characterized as official. The evidentiary bar does not apply because no immune official act is implicated. The prosecution proceeds without immunity-based interference and looks no different from any other defendant's tax case.

4. **Personal assault during a private dinner.** While in office, the President assaults a guest at a personal social event held at a private residence. The act is not an exercise of any Article II power, occurs in a personal capacity, and bears no functional relationship to the office. Under Trump's tripartite framework this is unofficial conduct entitled to no immunity, and the evidentiary bar does not extend to the underlying facts because they were never an official act. The prosecution proceeds on ordinary state-law assault charges.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

5. **Charges arising from a campaign rally speech delivered while in office.** The indictment alleges that the President's January 6 Ellipse speech incited the crowd that marched on the Capitol. The Trump majority expressly declined to classify this category, remanding for the District Court to determine whether the speech was made in the President's official capacity (communication with the public on matters of national concern, presumptively official) or in his capacity as a candidate (electioneering, presumptively unofficial). The Court signaled the inquiry is "fact-specific" and "objective" and forbade motive analysis. Lower courts will have to develop factors (audience, venue, funding, content) without the analytical guidance Trump withheld; reasonable judges may classify the same speech differently.

## Critique

**Progressive / textualist critique.** The most persistent attack on the opinion is that the Constitution's text says nothing about Presidential criminal immunity. Article II, Section 4 contemplates impeachment "and Conviction of Treason, Bribery, or other high Crimes and Misdemeanors," and Section 3 expressly subjects the President to the laws he is to "take Care" are faithfully executed. Sotomayor's dissent presses this point alongside the historical record: the Founders rejected royal immunity at the Convention, and no statutory or precedential basis predates Nixon v Fitzgerald's narrower civil-immunity holding. Critics argue that the majority took a function-grounded civil immunity from Fitzgerald and inflated it into a categorical criminal immunity through structural speculation. The cost, on this account, is rule-of-law: the criminal law no longer applies evenhandedly to officeholders, and prosecutors lose the evidentiary tools needed to charge corrupt official conduct (the bribery hypothetical Barrett deploys is the canonical case). A related democratic-theory critique adds that the immunity functions ratchet-style: the more aggressively a President exercises power, the harder it becomes to prove the abuse was unofficial, so the framework rewards bolder conduct.

**Originalist / structural critique from within the majority.** Thomas's separate concurrence signals that even the Court's expansive immunity does not reach what he sees as the deeper Article II infirmity: the Special Counsel was never authorized by statute and so cannot exercise federal prosecutorial power at all. That position would moot the entire immunity inquiry by removing the prosecutor. From a different originalist angle, scholars sympathetic to a robust unitary executive note that the majority's "presumptive immunity" tier is doctrinally awkward, importing a balancing test into an area Article II arguably commits to plenary executive control. On this view, the Court should have either embraced absolute immunity for all official acts (the cleaner separation-of-powers position) or relied on case-by-case as-applied challenges (Barrett's narrower path), rather than inventing a tiered framework whose middle category invites the very intrusive judicial review the majority claims to avoid.

**Comparative-law critique.** Most constitutional democracies do not recognize a similar criminal immunity for former heads of government. France permits prosecution of a former President once the office is vacated; Israel, Italy, South Korea, and Brazil have all prosecuted former leaders for in-office conduct. Comparative critics treat the U.S. position as an outlier and an experiment whose costs (in deterrence and accountability) the majority did not adequately weigh against the chilling-effect concerns it foregrounded.

## Key Points

- Announces a three-tier framework: absolute (core powers), presumptive (official acts), none (unofficial).
- Vote: 6-3 along ideological lines. Sotomayor's dissent becomes widely cited outside the opinion.
- Extends Nixon v Fitzgerald's civil-immunity reasoning into the criminal context for the first time.
- Creates significant evidentiary-immunity rule barring use of protected official-act evidence even at trials of unofficial conduct.
