# Bowsher v Synar (1986)

## Memory Jogger

> Congress Can't Control What It Creates. The Gramm-Rudman-Hollings Act assigned deficit-reduction execution duties to the Comptroller General, an officer removable by Congress. The Court held that Congress cannot reserve removal power over an officer who executes the laws -- placing such an officer under congressional control would be the functional equivalent of a legislative veto over the execution of the law, which Chadha already forbade.

---

## Facts

- In 1985 Congress enacted the Balanced Budget and Emergency Deficit Control Act ("Gramm-Rudman-Hollings") in response to mounting federal deficits; the Act required automatic across-the-board spending cuts ("sequestration") if projected deficits exceeded statutory targets
- Under § 251, the Comptroller General was assigned the final step in the sequestration process: reviewing budget estimates from the OMB and CBO, resolving discrepancies, and issuing a binding report to the President specifying the required spending reductions, which the President was obligated to implement
- The Comptroller General is appointed by the President (from a list recommended by congressional leaders) and confirmed by the Senate, but is removable by Congress -- either by impeachment or by joint resolution on specified grounds including inefficiency, neglect of duty, and malfeasance (Budget and Accounting Act of 1921, 31 U.S.C. § 703(e)(1))
- Members of Congress challenged the Act on separation-of-powers grounds, arguing that the assignment of sequestration functions to a congressionally removable officer was unconstitutional
- The Act included a fallback provision (§ 274(f)) restoring the sequestration role to a joint committee of Congress if the Comptroller General's role was invalidated

---

## Procedural History

- A three-judge district court for the District of Columbia held the assignment of sequestration functions to the Comptroller General unconstitutional under the separation of powers, relying on Myers v. United States (1926) and INS v. Chadha (1983)
- Direct appeal to the Supreme Court under the Act's expedited-review provision
- Supreme Court affirmed, 7-2 in judgment (5 in majority, 2 concurring in judgment), June 26, 1986

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority (5):** Burger, C.J. (joined by Brennan, Powell, Rehnquist, and O'Connor, JJ.)
- **Concurrence in judgment (2):** Stevens, J. (joined by Marshall, J.) -- agreed the Act was unconstitutional but on different grounds: the problem was that Congress itself was wielding executive power through the sequestration report, not specifically the removal provision
- **Dissent:** White, J. -- argued the removal provision did not actually threaten the President's executive authority because the statutory removal grounds were politically neutral and unlikely to create real subservience
- **Dissent:** Blackmun, J. -- agreed with White that the majority's reasoning was unsound, and argued that if there was a constitutional infirmity the proper remedy was to sever the removal provision of the 1921 Act rather than invalidate the sequestration mechanism

---

## Holding

Congress cannot reserve for itself the power of removal of an officer charged with the execution of the laws, except through the constitutionally specified mechanism of impeachment. An officer who is subject to congressional removal cannot constitutionally be vested with the power to execute the laws, because allowing Congress to remove such an officer for "inefficiency" or "neglect" in executing those laws would amount to a prohibited congressional veto over the execution of statutes. The Comptroller General's sequestration functions under § 251 of Gramm-Rudman-Hollings were accordingly unconstitutional.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Burger's majority grounded its analysis in the structural principle that all three branches must operate through their respective constitutionally assigned channels. The Comptroller General was assigned executive functions -- interpreting the deficit-reduction mandate, making factual determinations about agency spending, and issuing binding directives -- that plainly constituted "execution" of the law. Yet the Comptroller General remained removable by Congress via joint resolution for reasons including inefficiency and neglect. Citing Myers v. United States (1926), the majority reasoned that the power to remove an officer who executes the laws is itself executive in character, and Congress retains it only through impeachment. Allowing Congress to remove the Comptroller for executing the law in ways Congress disliked would effectively revive the legislative veto condemned in INS v. Chadha (1983): Congress could simply threaten removal to coerce compliance with its preferred policy choices. The Court declined to sever the 1921 Act's removal provision, instead relying on the Act's own fallback mechanism in § 274(f).

## Key Points

- Establishes that Congress may not retain removal authority over any officer who exercises executive power -- the only permissible congressional role in removing executive officers is impeachment
- Closely paired with Seila Law LLC v. CFPB (2020) and Morrison v. Olson (1988): Bowsher addresses legislative-branch removal; Morrison addresses executive-branch for-cause removal; Seila Law addresses single-director independent agencies
- The Midpage "Caution" treatment reflects that Bowsher's strict formalism has been softened by later cases tolerating for-cause removal in other contexts
- Stevens's concurrence offers an alternative, arguably sharper structural framing: the problem is not which branch holds the removal key, but whether the branch that creates a statute is also executing it
- The fallback provision (§ 274(f)) saved the rest of Gramm-Rudman-Hollings; deficit sequestration was transferred to a congressional joint committee, which later raised its own constitutional doubts
