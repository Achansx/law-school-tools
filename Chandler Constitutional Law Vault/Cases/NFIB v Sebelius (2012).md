# NFIB v Sebelius (2012)

## Memory Jogger

> The Affordable Care Act's individual mandate fails under the Commerce Clause because Congress cannot compel commerce to exist, but survives as a tax; the Medicaid expansion fails because Congress cannot threaten states with the loss of existing funds to extract agreement to a new program.

---

## Facts

The Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act (ACA), enacted in 2010, restructured the American health-insurance market through two provisions challenged here. The individual mandate, codified at 26 U.S.C. § 5000A, required most Americans to maintain minimum essential health coverage or pay a "shared responsibility payment" to the Internal Revenue Service. The payment was collected as part of the annual federal tax return and calibrated to income, capped at the cost of available insurance, and backed by no criminal penalty.

The ACA also expanded Medicaid eligibility to all adults under 65 earning up to 133% of the federal poverty line, roughly sixteen million additional people. The statute directed the Secretary of Health and Human Services to withhold all Medicaid funds, not merely the incremental expansion dollars, from any state that refused to adopt the new eligibility rules. For many states Medicaid participation accounted for more than ten percent of the state budget.

Twenty-six states, several private plaintiffs, and the National Federation of Independent Business sued the federal government, arguing that the mandate exceeded the Commerce Clause and the Taxing Power and that the Medicaid condition was unconstitutionally coercive.

---

## Procedural History

The District Court for the Northern District of Florida struck down the individual mandate and held it not severable, invalidating the entire ACA. The Eleventh Circuit affirmed as to the mandate but held the mandate severable and sustained the rest of the ACA, including the Medicaid expansion.

The Supreme Court granted certiorari on three questions: the constitutionality of the individual mandate, the Medicaid expansion, and severability. In a fractured decision, the Court upheld the mandate as a tax, invalidated the Medicaid expansion's enforcement mechanism, and otherwise left the ACA intact.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority (mandate, tax power):** Roberts, C.J. (joined by Ginsburg, Breyer, Sotomayor, Kagan) — 5-4 upholding mandate as tax
- **Majority (mandate, Commerce Clause rejection):** Roberts, C.J., Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Alito — 5-4 rejecting Commerce Clause justification
- **Majority (Medicaid coercion):** Roberts, C.J. (joined by Breyer, Kagan); Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Alito agreed on coercion — 7-2 on coercion finding
- **Remedy (Medicaid):** Roberts, Breyer, Kagan (joined by Ginsburg, Sotomayor on the sever-the-sanction remedy) — narrow remedy
- **Joint dissent:** Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Alito (would invalidate the entire ACA)
- **Dissent (Medicaid):** Ginsburg (joined by Sotomayor; Breyer and Kagan joined in part)

---

## Holding

The individual mandate cannot be justified under the Commerce Clause, which reaches existing commercial activity but does not authorize Congress to compel individuals to engage in commerce. The mandate also cannot be justified under the Necessary and Proper Clause, because it is not incidental to an enumerated power but creates the predicate for one. The mandate is nevertheless a constitutional exercise of the taxing power, because the shared responsibility payment functions as a tax: it is collected by the IRS, yields revenue, has no scienter requirement, and is not so punitive as to cease to be a revenue measure.

The Medicaid expansion violates the Spending Clause because threatening to withhold all existing Medicaid funding leaves states with no genuine choice and amounts to coercion. The remedy is not to strike the expansion but to forbid the Secretary from terminating existing Medicaid funds for states that decline the expansion.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion (Roberts)

Chief Justice Roberts organized the opinion around the canon of constitutional avoidance. On the Commerce Clause he held that "[t]he power to regulate commerce presupposes the existence of commercial activity to be regulated" ([NFIB lines=276](https://app.midpage.ai/document/national-federation-of-independent-business-809122?utm_source=mcp&lines=276)); compelling an individual who is not currently purchasing insurance to enter the market is categorically different from regulating an ongoing commercial activity. Extending the power to reach inactivity, Roberts reasoned, would leave no principled stopping point. He rejected the Necessary and Proper Clause argument for similar reasons: the clause supports means incidental to an enumerated power, not the manufacture of a predicate for that power. Roberts then turned to the Taxing Power. Because it was "fairly possible" to read the shared responsibility payment as a tax rather than a penalty, and because the payment bore the structural hallmarks of a tax, the mandate survived. On Medicaid, Roberts treated the threatened withdrawal of all existing funds as a gun to the head: Spending Clause legislation must leave states a genuine choice, and conditioning preexisting dollars on acceptance of a distinct program crossed the coercion line first gestured at in Dole.

### Joint Dissent (Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Alito)

The joint dissent proceeded on the premise that the mandate fails on every constitutional ground and, combined with the Medicaid expansion, renders the entire ACA invalid. On the tax question, the dissenters argued Congress cannot have it both ways: Congress deliberately labeled the shared responsibility payment a "penalty" rather than a "tax" and, elsewhere in the ACA, distinguished the two with precision. Allowing a court to re-read a "penalty" as a "tax" enables Congress to evade constitutional limits by strategic labeling. The dissenters pressed a deeper incoherence: Roberts held the payment is not a "tax" for Anti-Injunction Act purposes because Congress called it a "penalty," then reversed course to uphold it as a "tax" for constitutional purposes. This "tax for constitutional purposes but not for statutory purposes" distinction, the dissenters wrote, is without precedent and internally contradictory. On the Medicaid expansion, the joint dissent agreed the threatened withdrawal of all existing Medicaid funds was unconstitutionally coercive, but drew the opposite remedial conclusion: because Congress designed the mandate and the Medicaid expansion as interlocking components of a single regulatory scheme, neither could stand without the other, and the entire ACA should have been invalidated. The dissenters rejected the majority's surgical severance as legislative revision rather than statutory interpretation.

### Dissent (Ginsburg)

Justice Ginsburg (joined by Sotomayor; Breyer and Kagan joined in part) would have sustained both provisions. On the Commerce Clause, Ginsburg argued the health-care market is unique: virtually everyone will at some point demand medical care, creating an inevitable rather than speculative entry into commerce. The uninsured are not inactive; they are cost-shifting their certain future health-care expenses onto insurers, providers, and insured patients. Under the substantial-effects framework of Wickard v Filburn (1942) and Gonzales v Raich (2005), Congress had ample rational basis to find that the aggregate effect of uninsured individuals on the interstate insurance market was substantial. Roberts's activity-inactivity distinction, she argued, is entirely novel: no prior Commerce Clause case ever required that each regulated party be currently engaged in an active commercial exchange. The aggregation principle operates at the class level, not the individual level, and Wickard never asked whether Filburn was transacting at the moment of regulation. On the Medicaid expansion, Ginsburg would have found no constitutional violation. Every new Spending Clause program modifies existing conditions, and states have long understood Medicaid's terms could evolve. The threatened loss of incremental new funds for non-compliance, she argued, is functionally indistinguishable from prior conditional-spending programs the Court had upheld under Dole. The expansion's condition (new eligibility rules) is directly related to the new program (expanded funding), and no prior case had ever invalidated a Spending Clause condition on coercion grounds.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

1. Congress requires uninsured homeowners in designated flood zones to purchase flood insurance from a federally approved insurer or pay a $500 annual surcharge collected with their federal tax return, with no criminal penalty. Does the mandate survive under NFIB? The surcharge has the structural hallmarks Roberts identified: collected by the IRS, calibrated to circumstances, produces revenue, and carries no criminal sanction. On these facts it survives as a tax. The Commerce Clause argument separately fails because uninsured homeowners are not yet in the flood-insurance market, triggering the same activity-inactivity bar as the ACA mandate.

2. Congress enacts a Broadband Expansion Act offering states $40 billion in new broadband infrastructure grants, but conditions the offer on states' full compliance with sweeping new telecommunications regulations and on penalty of losing all existing federal highway funds if they refuse. The threat to withhold all highway funds as the price of refusing a distinct broadband program fails under the NFIB coercion doctrine. Highway funds are a pre-existing benefit unrelated to broadband; the threatened withdrawal constitutes "economic dragooning" of the kind Roberts prohibited, particularly where highway funding exceeds 10% of many state budgets.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

3. Congress enacts the Health Insurance Fairness Act prohibiting insurers from charging different premiums based on employer size and mandating community rating across large group plans. The insurers are already transacting in the interstate insurance market; Congress is regulating existing commercial activity, not compelling inactive individuals to enter commerce. Roberts's inactivity distinction is not triggered, and the substantial-effects analysis readily supports the regulation. This is Raich/Wickard territory: a comprehensive federal regulatory scheme governing an interstate market.

4. Congress extends Medicaid eligibility to children under 10 at households earning up to 150% of the poverty line and withholds only the incremental new child-eligibility funds if a state refuses to adopt the new criteria, leaving all existing Medicaid funding untouched. This survives. Dole permits Congress to attach conditions to new grant money where the condition relates to the program and the state retains a genuine choice. Withholding only the marginal program-related funds is the paradigm case Dole blessed; the coercion holding in NFIB turns specifically on the threat to pre-existing funds, which this hypothetical eliminates.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

5. Congress enacts a national carbon levy of $50 per ton on household fossil fuel consumption above a specified threshold, payable with the annual tax return, with a partial rebate for low-income households. The statute is labeled a "Carbon Reduction Incentive" and is administered jointly by the EPA and IRS. The levy generates substantial revenue, is calibrated to consumption levels, and carries no criminal penalty -- satisfying Roberts's structural-tax criteria. However, the regulatory label and EPA co-administration blur the Roberts line between taxes and regulatory penalties. The joint dissent's logic (label controls) would cut against it; Roberts's logic (structural features control) would likely sustain it. Genuinely unclear whether a primarily regulatory motive vitiates the tax characterization.

## Critique

Progressive critique: Roberts's activity-inactivity distinction is the decision's most contested structural feature. Scholars including Akhil Amar and Erwin Chemerinsky argued the distinction has no foundation in Commerce Clause precedent. Wickard and Raich both approved regulation of individuals who were not transacting commercially at the moment of regulation -- Filburn was growing wheat for home consumption, not selling it. The aggregation principle operates at the class level, asking whether the regulated class substantially affects interstate commerce, not whether each individual is currently active. Moreover, the line collapses into a framing question: characterizing the uninsured as "active" cost-shifters (as Ginsburg did) flips the analysis with equal logical force. A distinction this sensitive to characterization cannot supply a stable constitutional rule.

Originalist and textualist critique: Roberts's tax-power saving construction raises unresolved Direct Tax Clause concerns. Article I, section 9 requires direct taxes to be apportioned by population. If the shared responsibility payment is a tax on individuals calibrated to income and household size, it resembles a capitation more than an excise. Roberts disposed of the Direct Tax argument summarily without engaging its original meaning. Randy Barnett (respondents' counsel) argued additionally that the saving construction was itself a form of judicial legislating: Congress chose the word "penalty" deliberately, and the Court should not substitute its preferred label. The dissent's "Congress chose the wrong label" objection is, on this view, not a formalist quibble but a separation-of-powers point.

Spending Clause limiting principle: The Medicaid coercion holding is rhetorically vivid but analytically underdeveloped. Roberts offered no percentage-of-budget threshold, no test for what counts as a "new program" versus an "expansion" of an existing one, and no criteria distinguishing Medicaid from the highway-funding condition in South Dakota v Dole (1987). Lower courts applying the Medicaid holding have confronted exactly this gap: the line between "relatively mild encouragement" and "economic dragooning" remains unsettled more than a decade after NFIB.

## Key Points

- Commerce Clause contraction: reaffirms Lopez/Morrison limits and adds a new activity-inactivity gloss.
- Tax Power expansion: a regulatory mandate with a revenue-raising penalty is sustainable if it is fairly readable as a tax.
- Spending Clause coercion doctrine: gives teeth to the Dole fourth prong for the first time in practice.
- Vote is fractured. Roberts provides the decisive swing in an otherwise 4-4 Court on the mandate question.
- Led most Republican-led states to decline the Medicaid expansion, with downstream coverage effects that remain politically contested.

- [[Cases/Wickard v Filburn (1942)|Wickard v Filburn]]: Ginsburg invokes Wickard's aggregation principle to defend the mandate; Roberts distinguishes it on the ground that Wickard regulated existing wheat cultivation, not the decision not to grow wheat.

- [[Cases/United States v Lopez (1995)|United States v Lopez]]: Reaffirmed as establishing that Commerce Clause power does not reach noneconomic intrastate activity; the activity-inactivity distinction reinforces and extends Lopez's limiting logic.
- [[Cases/United States v Morrison (2000)|United States v Morrison]]: Cited alongside Lopez as the companion case establishing that noneconomic activity unrelated to a larger regulatory scheme falls outside Commerce Clause power.
- [[Cases/South Dakota v Dole (1987)|South Dakota v Dole]]: The Medicaid coercion holding operationalizes the coercion principle Dole had floated but not applied (Dole's fourth prong is the independent-bar requirement that no other constitutional provision must bar the condition; the coercion principle is a separate overlay that NFIB activates) for the first time in practice; Roberts cites Dole as establishing the "gun to the head" limit.
