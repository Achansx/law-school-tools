# District of Columbia v Heller (2008)

## Memory Jogger

> The case that established the Second Amendment protects an individual right to keep and bear arms for self-defense in the home, unconnected to militia service.

---

## Facts

The District of Columbia enacted one of the most restrictive firearms laws in the country. It generally prohibited the possession of handguns: it was a crime to carry an unregistered firearm, and the District refused to register handguns. The law also required that any lawfully owned firearm in the home (such as a registered long gun) be kept unloaded and disassembled or bound by a trigger lock at all times, unless located at a place of business or being used for lawful recreation.

Dick Heller was a D.C. special police officer authorized to carry a handgun while on duty guarding the Federal Judicial Center. He applied to register a handgun for use in his home. The District refused his application. Heller sued, arguing the D.C. handgun ban and trigger-lock requirement violated his Second Amendment right to keep and bear arms.

The constitutional provision at issue was the Second Amendment: "A well regulated Militia, being necessary to the security of a free State, the right of the people to keep and bear Arms, shall not be infringed." The central interpretive dispute was whether this protected only a collective right tied to militia service or an individual right encompassing self-defense.

---

## Procedural History

Heller initially filed suit in the District Court for the District of Columbia, which dismissed his complaint. The D.C. Circuit reversed, holding that the Second Amendment protects an individual right and striking down the handgun ban as unconstitutional. The Supreme Court granted certiorari to resolve the fundamental question of the Second Amendment's scope. This was the Court's first in-depth examination of the Amendment.

## Holding

The Court held 5-4 that the Second Amendment protects an individual right to possess and carry weapons in case of confrontation, unconnected with service in a militia. The D.C. handgun ban violated this right because it prohibited an entire class of arms overwhelmingly chosen by Americans for self-defense in the home. The trigger-lock requirement was also unconstitutional because it made it impossible for citizens to use firearms for the core lawful purpose of self-defense. The right is not unlimited, however, and the Court identified several categories of "presumptively lawful" regulations that remain valid.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion (Scalia)

Scalia employed an originalist, text-and-history methodology. He divided the Second Amendment into a prefatory clause ("A well regulated Militia, being necessary to the security of a free State") and an operative clause ("the right of the people to keep and bear Arms, shall not be infringed"), analyzing each separately before checking them for consistency.

**The Operative Clause.** Scalia began with "the right of the people," noting this phrase appears throughout the Constitution (First Amendment, Fourth Amendment) and consistently refers to individual rights exercised by all members of the political community. "Arms" meant weapons of offense or defense at the founding, not only military weapons. "Keep arms" simply meant "have weapons," as confirmed by Samuel Johnson's 1773 dictionary. "Bear arms" meant to carry weapons, not exclusively to serve in a military capacity -- the District's military-only reading was a "hybrid definition" no dictionary had ever adopted.

The operative clause's combined meaning: "the individual right to possess and carry weapons in case of confrontation."

**The Prefatory Clause.** Scalia treated the prefatory clause as announcing one purpose for codifying the right -- preventing elimination of the citizen militia -- without exhausting all reasons Americans valued it. Self-defense and hunting were also important. "Militia" at the founding meant all able-bodied male citizens capable of military service, not merely the organized or select militia. "Well-regulated" meant properly disciplined and trained, not government-controlled. The prefatory clause was a purpose statement, not a limitation on the operative clause's scope.

**Historical Confirmation.** The historical narrative ran from the English experience (Stuart Kings disarming political opponents, the 1689 English Bill of Rights) through the founding debates (Antifederalist fears of a disarmed populace, Federalist assurances that Congress had no power to abridge the right) to post-ratification commentary (St. George Tucker, William Rawle, Joseph Story all treating the right as individual).

**The Right Is Not Unlimited.** Scalia acknowledged that the right is not absolute. The Court identified "presumptively lawful" regulations: prohibitions on possession by felons and the mentally ill, laws forbidding firearms in sensitive places (schools, government buildings), and conditions on commercial sale. The Court also limited protection to weapons "in common use for lawful purposes," excluding "dangerous and unusual weapons."

**Application.** The handgun ban failed because handguns are "the most preferred firearm in the nation to keep and use for protection of one's home and family." A complete prohibition of an entire class of arms overwhelmingly chosen for self-defense was unconstitutional under any standard of scrutiny applied to enumerated rights. The trigger-lock requirement failed because it made firearms unusable for self-defense.

**Standard of Review.** The Court rejected rational-basis review and Breyer's proposed interest-balancing approach. "The very enumeration of the right takes out of the hands of government -- even the Third Branch -- the power to decide on a case-by-case basis whether the right is really worth insisting upon." But the Court declined to specify whether strict or intermediate scrutiny applied, leaving this for future cases.

### Dissent (Stevens)

Stevens argued the Second Amendment protects only a right to keep and bear arms in connection with militia service, not an individual right to civilian self-defense. His textual argument rested on three pillars.

First, the prefatory clause defines the right's scope, not merely announces a purpose. The Amendment's single-minded focus on militia service -- and its omission of any reference to hunting or self-defense, which Pennsylvania and Vermont's contemporaneous declarations did protect -- showed the Framers intended to protect only military-related arms bearing.

Second, "bear arms" is a military idiom. Unadorned by modifiers like "for the defense of themselves," the phrase "bear arms" most naturally refers to military service, as evidenced by dozens of founding-era texts.

Third, "the people" need not have identical scope in every constitutional provision. In the Second Amendment context, "the people" refers to the people collectively in their capacity as militia members, not as individuals exercising a personal right.

Stevens concluded that even if the text were ambiguous, the burden fell on those departing from settled law (the collective-right reading that had prevailed in lower courts for decades).

### Dissent (Breyer)

Breyer assumed arguendo that the Second Amendment protects an individual right but argued the D.C. handgun ban was still constitutional under an interest-balancing approach. He proposed asking whether a statute burdens a protected interest "in a way or to an extent that is out of proportion to the statute's salutary effects upon other important governmental interests." The D.C. law was proportionate because it addressed the severe problem of urban handgun violence, was limited in geographic scope, and had historical antecedents in founding-era firearms regulations.

Breyer also identified a tension in the majority's methodology: by blessing broad categories of existing regulations (felon-in-possession laws, sensitive-place restrictions) without explaining how they survived scrutiny, the majority was effectively engaging in the same interest-balancing it purported to reject.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

1. **Hypo:** A city bans all semi-automatic pistols, which constitute the majority of handguns sold in the United States, while permitting revolvers. **Why:** Under Heller, a ban on "the most preferred firearm in the nation" for self-defense fails. Semi-automatic pistols are the modern equivalent of what Heller describes -- arms overwhelmingly chosen for lawful self-defense. Banning an entire dominant class of handguns while permitting only a less common type still amounts to a prohibition on the preferred means of exercising the core right.

2. **Hypo:** A state requires all firearms in the home to be stored in a locked safe at all times, with no exception for immediate self-defense use. **Why:** This is functionally identical to the trigger-lock requirement Heller struck down -- it makes firearms unusable for the core lawful purpose of self-defense in the home.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

3. **Hypo:** A state prohibits convicted felons from possessing any firearms. **Why:** The Heller majority explicitly identified felon-in-possession laws as "presumptively lawful" longstanding regulations that the decision does not cast doubt upon.

4. **Hypo:** A city bans the private possession of machine guns and other fully automatic weapons. **Why:** Heller limits Second Amendment protection to weapons "in common use for lawful purposes" and excludes "dangerous and unusual weapons." Machine guns have been heavily regulated since 1934 and are not in common civilian use.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

5. **Hypo:** A state bans AR-15-style semi-automatic rifles, the most popular rifle platform in America. **Why:** The AR-15 is arguably the "most preferred" rifle for lawful purposes (including home defense), which would bring it within Heller's protection. But the majority also endorsed the "dangerous and unusual weapons" exception and limited protection to bearable arms in "common use." Whether a semi-automatic rifle with features associated with military design constitutes a protected arm in "common use for lawful purposes" or falls outside the right's scope has divided lower courts. (This question was later addressed through the Bruen framework.)

---

## Critique

Heller is one of the most consequential and contested originalist opinions in modern constitutional law. Both the majority and the dissent claim fidelity to original meaning, yet reach opposite conclusions -- raising foundational questions about originalism as a methodology.

Scalia's opinion is a masterclass in textual parsing, but critics note that his reading of "bear arms" as encompassing individual civilian carry requires dismissing the overwhelmingly military usage of the phrase in founding-era documents. Stevens catalogued dozens of examples where "bear arms" clearly meant military service, and Scalia's response -- that some examples used the phrase in a non-military context -- has struck many linguists as unpersuasive given the statistical weight of the evidence.

The "presumptively lawful" regulations list is arguably the opinion's weakest point. Scalia offered no historical justification for why felon-in-possession laws or sensitive-place restrictions survive analysis under his originalist framework. These carve-outs read more like pragmatic concessions than principled applications of the methodology the opinion champions.

The refusal to specify a standard of review left lower courts adrift for over a decade, leading most circuits to adopt a two-step framework (historical scope + means-end scrutiny) that the Court eventually rejected in New York State Rifle & Pistol Association v. Bruen (2022) in favor of a text-history-and-tradition test.

Breyer's interest-balancing critique has force: if the enumeration of the right truly forecloses judicial balancing, it is hard to explain how the majority can simultaneously declare entire categories of regulation "presumptively lawful" without engaging in precisely the kind of balancing it condemns.

## Key Points

- First Supreme Court case to hold definitively that the Second Amendment protects an individual right unconnected to militia service
- The right's core protection is self-defense in the home -- "the right of law-abiding, responsible citizens to use arms in defense of hearth and home"
- Protection extends to all "bearable arms" in "common use for lawful purposes," not limited to founding-era weapons
- "Presumptively lawful" regulations survive: felon-in-possession laws, sensitive-place restrictions, commercial sale conditions
- The Court rejected both rational-basis review and Breyer's interest-balancing approach but declined to specify strict or intermediate scrutiny
- Applies only to the federal government (D.C. is a federal enclave); incorporation against the states came two years later in McDonald v. City of Chicago (2010)
- The standard-of-review gap was later filled by Bruen (2022), which adopted a text-history-and-tradition framework
