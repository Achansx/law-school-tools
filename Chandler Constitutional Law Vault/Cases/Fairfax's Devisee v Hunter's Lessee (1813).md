# Fairfax's Devisee v Hunter's Lessee (1813)

## Memory Jogger

> Virginia could not complete its escheat of a British subject's Virginia lands after the Jay Treaty of 1794 confirmed the alien owner's title, giving the Supreme Court its first occasion to hold that a federal treaty preempts a state's inchoate claim to real property.

---

## Facts

Lord Fairfax, a British subject, held a vast proprietary grant in the Northern Neck of Virginia by royal patent. He devised the Northern Neck lands to his nephew Denny Martin Fairfax (later Denny Fairfax), also a British subject. During and after the Revolution, Virginia enacted statutes purporting to confiscate lands held by British subjects and granted parcels of the Northern Neck to its own citizens, including David Hunter, whose grant ripened into Hunter's Lessee's interest in the tract at issue.

Virginia, however, never completed an inquest of office, the common-law procedure required to perfect the Commonwealth's title to alien-held lands. In 1783 the Treaty of Paris ended the war and included a clause forbidding future confiscations; in 1794 the Jay Treaty expressly confirmed the titles of British subjects already holding lands in the United States, allowing them to continue to hold, grant, sell, or devise the lands as if they were natives. Hunter's Lessee brought an action of ejectment in Virginia state court against Fairfax's devisee, claiming title through the Virginia grant.

The case frames the Supreme Court's first decision to invalidate a state claim of title under the Supremacy Clause and lays the groundwork for the contest over federal jurisdiction that would produce Martin v Hunter's Lessee three years later.

---

## Procedural History

The Virginia state courts entered judgment for Hunter's Lessee, sustaining Virginia's claim and treating the Commonwealth as having divested Fairfax's title. Fairfax's devisee obtained a writ of error to the Supreme Court of the United States under Section 25 of the Judiciary Act.

The Supreme Court reversed. Justice Story wrote for the Court; Chief Justice Marshall did not participate because he had purchased portions of the Northern Neck lands from Denny Fairfax. Justice Johnson dissented. On remand, the Virginia Court of Appeals refused to comply, producing the jurisdictional confrontation resolved in Martin v Hunter's Lessee (1816).

## Holding

An alien may take and hold land by purchase or devise, subject to divestiture by the sovereign through an inquest of office. Until the state completes that inquest, the alien's title remains intact. Virginia's statutes did not clearly and unequivocally dispense with the inquest-of-office requirement, and even if they had, the Jay Treaty of 1794 supervened and confirmed Denny Fairfax's title against the state's inchoate claim. Under the Supremacy Clause the treaty, as the supreme law of the land, rendered Virginia's subsequent grant to Hunter void as against Fairfax's devisee.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion (Story)

Justice Story began with the settled common-law rule that an alien may purchase or be devised land and that the resulting title is defeasible only through an affirmative act of the sovereign, typically an inquest of office. Virginia's Revolutionary statutes penalizing alien holdings did not displace this requirement, because ousting the common-law rule required clear and explicit legislative language. Reading the statutes narrowly, Story found that the Commonwealth had not completed the required procedural steps to vest title in itself or in Hunter. Story then turned to the Treaty of Paris (1783) and the Jay Treaty (1794). He doubted that the 1783 treaty's prohibition on future confiscations, by itself, cured an underlying legal disability unrelated to revolutionary war conduct. The Jay Treaty, however, directly preserved the titles of British subjects then holding lands and gave them the same rights of ownership, alienation, and devise as natives. As the supreme law of the land the treaty overrode whatever inchoate claim Virginia retained. The Commonwealth's derivative grant to Hunter accordingly failed.

## Key Quotations

> "It is incontrovertibly settled upon the fullest authority, that the title acquired by an alien by purchase, is not divested until office found. The principle is founded upon the ground, that as the freehold is in the alien, and he is tenant to the lord of whom the lands are holden, it cannot be divested out of him but by some notorious act, by which it may appear that the freehold is in another." ## Key Points

- First Supreme Court decision to invalidate a state claim of title under a federal treaty, establishing the pattern for Article VI treaty preemption.
- Justice Story writes for the Court because Marshall is recused (he held an interest in the Northern Neck lands).
- Virginia's refusal to comply on remand produces the jurisdictional confrontation resolved in Martin v Hunter's Lessee (1816).
- Establishes the "clear and explicit" canon against reading state statutes to displace settled common-law procedures.
- Frequently paired with Martin as the doctrinal lead-up to Cohens v Virginia (1821).
