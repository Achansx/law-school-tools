# Dames and Moore v Regan (1981)

## Memory Jogger

> Iran Hostage Crisis aftermath: President Carter's IEEPA asset freeze and President Reagan's Executive Order No. 12294 nullified pending attachments on Iranian assets and suspended American-national claims, sending them to the Iran-United States Claims Tribunal. A unanimous Court (Rehnquist, J.) upheld both moves: IEEPA was specific congressional authorization for the nullification, and a long pattern of congressional acquiescence in claims-settlement executive agreements made the suspension a permissible gloss on Article II. The Court read Jackson's Youngstown tripartite framework as a continuum rather than three boxes.

---

## Facts

On November 4, 1979, Iranian revolutionaries seized the United States Embassy in Tehran and took 52 Americans hostage. On November 14, 1979, President Carter, invoking the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA, 50 U.S.C. §§ 1701-1706), froze "all property and interests in property of the Government of Iran" held in the United States or by U.S. persons. Executive Order No. 12170 declared a national emergency and authorized the Treasury's Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC) to issue licenses permitting or denying transactions with Iranian assets.

Petitioner Dames & Moore, an American engineering firm, had contracted with the Atomic Energy Organization of Iran for site studies on a proposed nuclear power plant. When Iran stopped paying, Dames & Moore sued in the United States District Court for the Central District of California and, in December 1979, obtained orders of attachment against Iranian bank deposits and property. In October 1980, the district court entered partial summary judgment in Dames & Moore's favor for more than $3.4 million.

On January 19, 1981, immediately before the hostages' release, the United States and Iran executed the Algiers Accords. The agreement committed the United States to (a) terminate all legal proceedings in U.S. courts involving claims of U.S. nationals against Iran, (b) nullify all attachments and judicial orders entered against Iranian property, and (c) transfer those claims to a newly created Iran-United States Claims Tribunal sitting at The Hague. Iran transferred blocked assets to escrow accounts at the Bank of England for arbitration-judgment payments. President Carter issued a package of Executive Orders the same day implementing the Accords; President Reagan, on February 24, 1981, issued Executive Order No. 12294, which "suspended" all claims in U.S. courts that were cognizable before the Claims Tribunal and nullified any attachment or judgment inconsistent with the Accords.

Relevant statutory framework: IEEPA grants the President broad authority, during a declared national emergency, to "investigate, regulate . . . prevent or prohibit . . . any acquisition, holding, withholding, use, transfer, . . . exportation, or importation" of foreign property subject to U.S. jurisdiction (50 U.S.C. § 1702(a)(1)(B)). The Hostage Act (22 U.S.C. § 1732) authorizes the President to "use such means, not amounting to acts of war . . . as he may think necessary and proper" to secure the release of American citizens unjustly imprisoned abroad. The Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act of 1976 (28 U.S.C. §§ 1602 et seq.) displaced executive-branch case-by-case immunity decisions in favor of statutory standards.

---

## Procedural History

Dames & Moore filed the action that reached the Supreme Court in the District Court for the Central District of California against the Secretary of the Treasury and other federal officers, seeking a declaratory judgment that the Carter and Reagan Executive Orders were unconstitutional insofar as they nullified the attachments and suspended the claim. The district court entered partial summary judgment for Dames & Moore on the merits of the underlying debt but denied preliminary injunctive relief against the Executive Orders and dismissed the constitutional challenge. The Supreme Court granted certiorari before judgment in the Ninth Circuit because of the "extreme sensitivity and emergency" of the issues (453 U.S. at 660) and set the case for expedited briefing and argument in June 1981.

## Holding

The President's nullification of the Iranian-asset attachments was specifically authorized by IEEPA. The President's suspension of claims in U.S. courts pursuant to Executive Order No. 12294 was not specifically authorized by either IEEPA or the Hostage Act, but a long, unbroken, and systematic history of congressional acquiescence in executive claims-settlement agreements, combined with legislation (IEEPA, the Hostage Act, and the International Claims Settlement Act of 1949) indicating broad executive discretion in foreign-affairs crises, raises a presumption of congressional consent sufficient to place the action within Jackson category one or, at worst, a congressionally tolerated exercise in the "zone of twilight." The petitioner's Fifth Amendment takings claim against the suspension of claims is not ripe, and no jurisdictional bar prevents a later Tucker Act suit in the Court of Claims if the Claims Tribunal proves an inadequate forum.
