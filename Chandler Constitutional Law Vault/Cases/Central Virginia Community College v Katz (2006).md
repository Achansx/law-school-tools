# Central Virginia Community College v Katz (2006)

## Memory Jogger

> States cannot invoke sovereign immunity to block bankruptcy proceedings because they impliedly surrendered that immunity when the Constitution was ratified — the Bankruptcy Clause's structural logic, not a congressional abrogation statute, is the source of the waiver.

---

## Facts

Wallace's Bookstores, Inc., a Kentucky company, filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy and its estate was administered by debtor-in-possession Bernard Katz, who served as liquidating supervisor. Prior to the bankruptcy filing, Wallace's had made payments to the Central Virginia Community College and other Virginia state educational institutions for textbooks and related supplies. These payments occurred within the 90-day preference period before the bankruptcy filing.

Under the Bankruptcy Code, trustees and debtors-in-possession may recover preferential transfers — payments made to creditors within 90 days before bankruptcy that give those creditors more than they would receive in a liquidation. Katz filed adversary proceedings against the Virginia educational institutions to recover these preferential transfers. The Virginia institutions asserted Eleventh Amendment sovereign immunity as a defense, arguing that Congress could not subject them to suit in federal bankruptcy court without their consent.

---

## Procedural History

The Bankruptcy Court declined to dismiss, holding that the Virginia institutions could not invoke sovereign immunity in bankruptcy proceedings. The District Court and Sixth Circuit affirmed on the merits. The Virginia institutions sought certiorari, and the Supreme Court granted review to address whether state sovereign immunity bars preference-avoidance actions in bankruptcy.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority:** Stevens (joined by O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer)
- **Dissent:** Thomas (joined by Rehnquist, Scalia, Kennedy)

---

## Holding

States may not assert sovereign immunity to bar preference-avoidance and other proceedings necessary to effectuate the in rem jurisdiction of bankruptcy courts. By ratifying the Constitution, the states acquiesced in the subordination of their sovereign immunity in bankruptcy proceedings, regardless of whether Congress has separately abrogated that immunity by statute.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Justice Stevens's majority opinion rested on a structural constitutional argument distinct from the Section 5 enforcement power framework used in Seminole Tribe, Florida Prepaid, and Garrett. Rather than asking whether Congress had validly abrogated state sovereign immunity, the majority asked whether states retained sovereign immunity in bankruptcy proceedings at all.

The majority concluded they did not. The Bankruptcy Clause (Article I, § 8, cl. 4) grants Congress the power to establish "uniform Laws on the subject of Bankruptcies throughout the United States." At the Founding, uniform bankruptcy law was understood to require the ability to bind all creditors — including states — and to administer the debtor's estate as a whole through in rem jurisdiction over property. The historical practice at the Founding and in the early republic reflected that states were subject to bankruptcy proceedings. When the states ratified the Constitution and granted Congress the bankruptcy power, they impliedly consented to be subject to bankruptcy court proceedings necessary to administer debtors' estates. This structural consent is different from congressional abrogation: states did not surrender sovereignty by statute, they surrendered it by ratification.
