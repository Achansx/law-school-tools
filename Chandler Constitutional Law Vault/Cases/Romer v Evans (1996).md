# Romer v Evans (1996)

## Memory Jogger

> Kennedy's majority struck down Colorado's Amendment 2 -- which barred any governmental unit from protecting gay and lesbian persons from discrimination -- under rational basis review, holding that a law born of animosity toward a disfavored group fails equal protection because it lacks any legitimate justification other than a bare desire to harm.

---

## Facts

Colorado voters enacted Amendment 2 to the state constitution by referendum in 1992. The amendment prohibited all legislative, executive, and judicial action at any level of state or local government from protecting gay, lesbian, or bisexual persons from discrimination. Its immediate effect was to nullify local ordinances in Denver, Aspen, and Boulder that had extended civil-rights protections to gay and lesbian residents, and to bar the enactment of any similar ordinances statewide. Respondents -- individuals and several Colorado municipalities -- challenged the amendment as a violation of the Equal Protection Clause. Colorado defended the amendment on several grounds: it preserved personal, religious, and familial privacy; it prevented special rights for gay people; and it conserved resources to fight discrimination against other groups.

---

## Procedural History

The Colorado Supreme Court enjoined enforcement of Amendment 2 and, applying strict scrutiny, held it unconstitutional on the ground that it infringed the fundamental right to participate in the political process. The United States Supreme Court granted certiorari and affirmed, but on different grounds -- applying rational basis review rather than strict scrutiny or any heightened standard for sexual-orientation classifications.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority:** Kennedy (joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer) -- Amendment 2 fails rational basis; animus cannot be a legitimate state interest
- **Dissent (Scalia, joined by Rehnquist and Thomas):** Argued the majority invented a novel constitutional prohibition; Colorado was entitled to resolve the culture war question through democratic means; homosexuality is neither a suspect class nor a protected right

---

## Holding

Amendment 2 to the Colorado Constitution violates the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. A law that withdraws from one identifiable group the right to seek specific legal protections from the government -- while leaving all others free to do so -- imposes a special disability that bears no rational relationship to any legitimate governmental interest. Laws born of animosity toward a disfavored group cannot survive even rational basis review.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Justice Kennedy's majority opinion is notable for what it did not do as much as for what it did. The Court declined to decide whether sexual orientation is a suspect or quasi-suspect classification, and it did not apply heightened scrutiny. Instead, it applied rational basis review and found Amendment 2 wanting on its own terms.

Kennedy's central move was to characterize Amendment 2 as a sweeping disability imposed specifically on gay, lesbian, and bisexual persons, disqualifying them from the ordinary operation of state and local law. The amendment did not merely decline to protect a class; it restructured the entire political process to place one group at a categorical disadvantage. Drawing on equal protection precedent forbidding class legislation, Kennedy held that a law can have no legitimate purpose if its sole identifiable rationale is animosity toward the affected group. Colorado's stated justifications -- privacy, resource conservation, avoiding special rights -- could not explain why the law swept so broadly and operated so completely against a single group.

The distinction between withdrawing political access and merely declining to confer a benefit is the hinge of Kennedy's opinion. A state can decline to pass an anti-discrimination ordinance covering gay and lesbian residents. A state can repeal such an ordinance. What a state cannot do, under Romer, is structurally foreclose the possibility of such protection by constitutional amendment. Amendment 2 did not say Colorado lacked an anti-discrimination ordinance covering sexual orientation. It said that no Colorado governmental unit, at any level, in any branch, could ever adopt one without another constitutional amendment. That meta-move, imposing a categorical disability on one group's ability to seek ordinary political protection, was what the Court held unconstitutional. Other groups in Colorado could still lobby for anti-discrimination coverage and receive it through the ordinary legislative process. Only gay, lesbian, and bisexual persons were foreclosed.

The "class legislation" strand of equal protection doctrine that Kennedy invoked has roots in the Lochner era but was generally thought long dormant. Nineteenth-century cases had held that the Equal Protection Clause forbids legislation directed at one class for its own sake, without a legitimate public purpose. That strand had been overshadowed by the suspect-class framework and by the modern tiers of scrutiny. Kennedy revived it without saying so. A law cannot have a legitimate purpose if its sole identifiable rationale is a bare desire to harm the affected group. That principle, repeated in multiple forms in the opinion, is a descendant of the class-legislation rule and a precursor to what scholars now call "animus doctrine."

Whether Romer applied true rational basis or a form of "rational basis with bite" has been debated for three decades. The opinion does not use the words "with bite," and its formal framework is ordinary rational basis. In substance, however, the Court demanded more than Colorado offered. The state's stated reasons (protecting privacy and association, conserving resources, avoiding special rights) would have survived ordinary rational basis review if applied to a narrower measure. Here they could not explain why the amendment swept so broadly, disqualified one group so completely, and made the political process itself the site of exclusion. Some scholars read this as a quiet upgrade of scrutiny without a formal label. Others read it as a faithful application of rational basis on an unusually weak factual record. The truth may be that the Court wanted a doctrinal tool that could be cabined to egregious cases without committing to strict scrutiny for sexual orientation.

Kennedy's refusal to decide the suspect-class question was deliberate. Treating sexual orientation as a suspect or quasi-suspect class would have required the Court to apply the usual factors: history of discrimination (clearly yes), political powerlessness (contested), immutability (contested), and relevance of the trait to legitimate government aims (contested). Declaring sexual orientation a suspect class would have had immediate doctrinal consequences across many state laws, including the then-recent federal Defense of Marriage Act. Kennedy avoided that terrain by deciding Romer on a narrower ground. The animus doctrine did the work without requiring suspect-class reasoning. That choice preserved flexibility and avoided an immediate collision with the hundreds of sexual-orientation classifications then on the books in state and federal law.

The connective tissue between Romer and the Kennedy LGBTQ trilogy (Lawrence v Texas (2003), United States v Windsor (2013), Obergefell v Hodges (2015)) is the animus strand. In each case, Kennedy located some version of the argument that a classification whose chief effect was to demean or exclude gay and lesbian persons could not survive review because such a purpose is not a legitimate government purpose. Windsor built explicitly on Romer. Obergefell turned the animus claim inside out: if a state cannot exclude gay people from anti-discrimination protection, it cannot exclude them from the fundamental right to marriage. The doctrinal through-line runs directly from Romer.

### Dissent (Scalia)

Justice Scalia's dissent, joined by Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justice Thomas, is one of his most pointed. Its opening line describes the majority as having taken sides in the culture war, and the rest of the opinion develops that theme. Colorado's voters, Scalia argues, had adopted Amendment 2 by direct democratic processes in response to local ordinances that they viewed as granting gay persons special legal protections. The state was entitled to resolve that contested social question through the ballot box. The Court's intervention to strike the amendment down, in Scalia's view, substituted the Justices' preferences for the voters'.

Scalia's second move was to recharacterize Amendment 2 as a provision that simply put gay persons on the same footing as everyone else. Before Amendment 2, gay residents of Denver, Aspen, and Boulder enjoyed anti-discrimination protections that nongay residents did not. Amendment 2, in Scalia's telling, did no more than prevent that form of preferential treatment, leaving gay persons with the same legal rights as everyone else in Colorado. The framing is sharply different from the majority's. The majority saw a law withdrawing political access from one group. Scalia saw a law leveling the playing field by eliminating local special-rights rules.

Scalia's third theme was that the majority's reasoning was unintelligible. Rational basis review does not permit courts to strike classifications based on their intuition that a law's supporters were motivated by animus. If rational basis means anything, Scalia argued, it means accepting any plausible legitimate purpose a legislature could have had. Kennedy's willingness to find Colorado's stated reasons insufficient, on Scalia's reading, was a covert application of heightened scrutiny dressed up in rational-basis clothing. The "bare desire to harm" concept, he wrote, is not a workable constitutional doctrine. Every criminal law, for example, reflects a "desire to harm" its target by penalizing particular conduct. What makes gay-targeted legislation different, if the legislature has stated any plausible public purpose?

The originalist challenge to animus doctrine is the deepest layer. Scalia argued that the Fourteenth Amendment, as ratified in 1868, had nothing to say about classifications based on sexual conduct or identity, and that reading into it a prohibition on laws motivated by hostility to particular lifestyles is a modern invention. He would have left the question to democratic processes.

The continuity of Scalia's opposition across LGBTQ cases is striking. In Lawrence v Texas (2003), he dissented from Kennedy's extension of substantive due process to same-sex intimacy; in United States v Windsor (2013), he dissented from the animus-based invalidation of the Defense of Marriage Act; in Obergefell v Hodges (2015), he dissented from the constitutional recognition of same-sex marriage. In each, he developed the same themes he laid down in Romer: democratic legitimacy, the absence of originalist warrant, and the incoherence of a doctrinal tool that lets judges strike laws they dislike as motivated by animus.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

*State Amendment Excluding Immigrants from Anti-Retaliation Protections.* A state adopts a constitutional amendment providing that no state or local government may adopt or enforce any ordinance protecting immigrants (regardless of status) from workplace retaliation, housing discrimination, or refusal of public accommodations. Supporters cite fiscal concerns about a predicted flood of complaints but the measure followed campaign advertising describing immigrants in dehumanizing terms. Under Romer, the amendment would likely fail. It withdraws one identifiable group from ordinary political protection, and the stated reasons are insufficient to explain the sweeping disability. The Kennedy framework treats this kind of targeted political-process exclusion as an animus-driven law that cannot survive even rational basis.

*Locality-Specific Ballot Measure Disqualifying Sex Offenders from Public Library Access.* A city enacts a charter amendment barring any city or county body from extending anti-discrimination protections to persons with sex-offense convictions, specifically in public library access, park use, and city-owned housing. The amendment does not address child-safety-specific rules; it foreclosees protection across a wide range of public goods. Under Romer, this is at least presumptively invalid because the sweep is broader than any public-safety rationale requires and the political-access exclusion is structural.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

*State Refusal to Enact Affirmative Action for Employment of Left-Handed Workers.* The state legislature declines to pass a proposed statute providing hiring preferences or anti-discrimination protections for left-handed workers. No ordinance withdraws anything; the legislature simply did not act. Romer does not apply. A state is free to decline to confer a benefit on a group; Romer's concern is structural withdrawal of political access, not legislative inaction.

*Repeal of a Local Ordinance Extending Protections Beyond State Law.* A city's anti-discrimination ordinance covering marital status is repealed by an ordinary city-council vote after a regulatory review concluded that the protection had produced an unmanageable volume of litigation without meaningful compliance benefits. The repeal is ordinary legislation; it does not foreclose future re-enactment; the state's reason is tied to a legitimate regulatory concern. Under Romer, this is not an animus case. Ordinary political processes produced the result and the stated reason is not a bare desire to harm.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

*Ballot Measure Excluding a Group from a Specific Benefit with Arguable Policy Basis.* A state adopts a ballot measure providing that no state or local education authority may fund domestic-partner benefits for same-sex partners of public employees. Supporters cite a policy interest in "strengthening traditional marriage" and in fiscal consistency with federal retirement benefit rules. Critics argue the measure was passed against the backdrop of organized campaigns describing the targeted group in pejorative terms. Under Romer, is this analogous to Amendment 2 or to ordinary legislative policy choice? The rule targets one group for exclusion, but only in one benefit category rather than across the board. The stated reasons are more specific than Colorado's were. A court applying Kennedy's framework could go either way, and the case tests the boundary between permissible policy choice and impermissible animus-driven exclusion.

## Critique

*Gay-rights critique that Romer was too narrow.* From the perspective of gay-rights advocates and movement lawyers, Romer was a correct outcome reached by the wrong route. The Court could have declared sexual orientation a suspect or quasi-suspect classification and subjected all orientation-based laws to heightened scrutiny. That route would have produced immediate doctrinal leverage against a wide range of state and federal sexual-orientation rules. Instead, Kennedy chose an animus-based rationale tethered to the specific sweep of Amendment 2. Subsequent litigation had to relitigate the scrutiny question case by case, and lower courts produced inconsistent treatments of sexual-orientation classifications well into the 2010s. The narrowness of Romer prolonged the period during which movement lawyers had to argue first-principles questions about how courts should view gay rights claims.

*Democratic-legitimacy critique.* Justice Scalia's concerns have force. Colorado voters passed Amendment 2 by a majority of nearly 53% in a contested referendum. Colorado voters were entitled, on a democratic-legitimacy view, to disagree with the policy judgments made by Denver, Aspen, and Boulder city councils. Overturning a constitutional amendment adopted by direct democratic process is an extraordinary act of judicial review, and the rationale on which it rested (animus) lacks a clear limiting principle. Every classification could be said to reflect a "bare desire to harm" if framed pejoratively. The critique is not that Amendment 2 was good policy but that an unelected Court should not substitute its moral judgment for a majority of the state's voters on a contested social question.

*Doctrinal critique of rational basis inconsistency.* Romer exemplifies a persistent instability in rational basis review. The official formulation is that any conceivable legitimate purpose justifies a classification. In practice, the Court applies that formulation strictly in some cases (Williamson v Lee Optical) and loosely in others (Romer, Cleburne, Moreno). The variable is not an articulated legal distinction but judicial sympathy with the affected group. That produces two harms. The predictability of rational basis review is damaged. And the Court's apparent tier-strictness is revealed as a kind of moral calibration masquerading as doctrine. Either the Court should be open about when it applies heightened scrutiny or it should apply rational basis consistently. The Romer majority does neither.

## Key Points

- Kennedy's majority struck Colorado's Amendment 2 under rational basis review, explicitly declining to decide whether sexual orientation is a suspect or quasi-suspect classification.
- Revived the dormant "class legislation" strand of equal protection and articulated the modern animus doctrine: a bare desire to harm a politically unpopular group cannot be a legitimate state interest.
- Hinge distinction between legislative inaction and structural foreclosure. A state can decline to enact anti-discrimination protections and can repeal them; it cannot foreclose the possibility of such protections by a constitutional amendment that singles out one group for political disability.
- Doctrinal through-line to the Kennedy LGBTQ trilogy. Lawrence v Texas (2003), United States v Windsor (2013), and Obergefell v Hodges (2015) each rest on Romer's animus-and-dignity frame, with Windsor explicitly building on Romer.
- Ongoing scholarly debate over whether Romer applied "ordinary" rational basis or "rational basis with bite." The opinion uses rational-basis language but demands more than Colorado's asserted justifications (privacy, resource conservation, avoiding special rights) offered.
- Scalia's dissent (joined by Rehnquist and Thomas) previewed decades of originalist objections across the LGBTQ arc: democratic legitimacy of direct-ballot results, absence of originalist warrant in the Fourteenth Amendment for sexual-orientation protections, and the indeterminacy of "animus" as a workable judicial standard.
- Colorado Supreme Court had applied strict scrutiny on a political-process fundamental-rights theory. SCOTUS affirmed the result but on a different rationale, preserving flexibility to decide future sexual-orientation cases without a formal suspect-class holding.

## Connections

- [[Topics/Equal Protection]]: Romer is the foundational animus case and the pivot point in the modern equal protection treatment of sexual orientation classifications.
- [[Cases/Lawrence v Texas (2003)]]: Kennedy's next major LGBTQ opinion, overruling Bowers v Hardwick and striking sodomy laws. Lawrence builds on Romer's moral logic (the state's interest in enforcing majority disapproval of homosexual conduct is not legitimate) while shifting the doctrinal hook to substantive due process.
- [[Cases/Obergefell v Hodges (2015)]]: The culmination of the Kennedy arc. Obergefell holds that same-sex couples have a constitutional right to marry, drawing on the animus-and-dignity strand begun in Romer and continued through Windsor.
- [[Cases/Bowers v Hardwick (1986)]]: The case the Kennedy LGBTQ arc eventually overruled. Scalia's Romer dissent leans heavily on Bowers, arguing that if states can criminalize homosexual conduct (as Bowers held), they can surely foreclose anti-discrimination protections for persons who engage in it.
- [[Cases/303 Creative LLC v Elenis (2023)]]: Recent chapter on the intersection of anti-discrimination laws and First Amendment claims. The terrain of Romer and the terrain of 303 Creative overlap: both involve the constitutional status of state rules protecting LGBTQ persons from disparate treatment.
- [[Cases/City of Cleburne v Cleburne Living Center (1985)|City of Cleburne v Cleburne Living Center (1985)]]: the doctrinal parent of Romer's animus principle. Cleburne held that bare negative attitudes toward a politically unpopular group (there, persons with intellectual disabilities) are not a legitimate governmental interest even at rational basis. Romer extends Cleburne's logic to sexual orientation and supplies the modern formulation of "rational basis with bite" that both opinions share.
- [[Cases/United States Department of Agriculture v Moreno (1973)|USDA v Moreno (1973)]]: the original animus precedent. Kennedy's Romer majority quotes Moreno's "bare desire to harm a politically unpopular group" formulation directly (the Key Quotation in Romer is verbatim from Moreno). Moreno-Cleburne-Romer is the canonical rational-basis-with-bite trio; Romer is the doctrinal hinge that ports Moreno's Fifth Amendment equal-protection-component reasoning into Fourteenth Amendment doctrine and applies it to sexual-orientation classifications.
