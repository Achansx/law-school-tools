# City of Rome v United States (1980)

## Memory Jogger

> The Court upholds the Voting Rights Act's discriminatory-effect preclearance standard and its 1975 extension, holding that Congress may prohibit electoral changes with discriminatory impact even absent discriminatory intent, and that municipalities in covered states cannot independently bail out of the Act's requirements.

---

## Facts

The city of Rome, Georgia, was subject to preclearance requirements under Section 5 of the Voting Rights Act of 1965 because Georgia was a covered jurisdiction (having used a literacy test and had low voter registration). Between 1964 and 1975, Rome made 60 changes to its electoral system and annexation boundaries, and sought preclearance from the Attorney General for all of them.

The Attorney General refused to preclear several changes: conversions from plurality-win to majority-win elections, numbered voting posts for candidates, staggered terms, and certain annexations. The Attorney General found that these changes, while not enacted with discriminatory purpose, had a discriminatory effect: they would dilute Black voting strength by frustrating "single-shot voting" in a city with racial bloc voting and a white majority.

Rome then sought a declaratory judgment that the Voting Rights Act's preclearance requirement was unconstitutional, arguing that (1) Section 5 could not prohibit electoral changes that had only a discriminatory effect (not purpose), and (2) the Act's 1975 extension was unconstitutional, and (3) Rome should be permitted to bail out of coverage independently of Georgia.

---

## Procedural History

A three-judge district court upheld the Voting Rights Act in all respects and denied Rome's request for preclearance. Rome appealed directly to the Supreme Court, which affirmed on all three issues.

## Holding

First, Section 5 of the Voting Rights Act validly prohibits electoral changes with a discriminatory effect, even without discriminatory purpose: the statute's conjunctive "purpose and effect" language requires both to be absent for preclearance, and Congress had authority under Section 2 of the Fifteenth Amendment to enact such prophylactic legislation beyond the Amendment's own Section 1 requirement of no purposeful discrimination. Second, the 1975 extension of the Act was constitutional because Rome had not shown that the Act's coverage had outlived its usefulness for the covered jurisdiction. Third, municipalities in a covered state cannot independently bail out of the Act's preclearance requirements; bailout must be sought by the state as a whole.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Justice Marshall's majority reasoned in three steps. On the discriminatory effect standard, the majority held that Section 5's "purpose and effect" language is conjunctive: preclearance requires showing both purposes and effects are non-discriminatory. Congress plainly intended to prohibit changes with only discriminatory effect. The constitutional authority for this was Section 2 of the Fifteenth Amendment (parallel to Katzenbach v Morgan's Section 5 framework): Congress may prohibit practices that don't themselves violate Section 1 of the Fifteenth Amendment if the prohibition is an appropriate method of advancing the Amendment's anti-discrimination purposes. Congress could rationally conclude that electoral changes in jurisdictions with a demonstrated history of intentional discrimination create a risk of purposeful discrimination even when not directly proven, justifying a discriminatory-effect standard.

On the 1975 extension, the majority deferred to Congress's finding that covered jurisdictions still posed a risk of disenfranchisement, applying the familiar principle that Congress need not strike at all evils at once and may calibrate its legislation to the most acute problems.

On bailout, the majority read the statutory text to allow only covered states (not their subdivisions) to seek independent bailout, because the coverage formula operated on the state as a whole.

## Key Points

- Section 5 of the Voting Rights Act can prohibit electoral changes with discriminatory effect even absent discriminatory intent, and Congress has Fifteenth Amendment Section 2 authority for this.
- The prophylactic legislation principle: Congress may prohibit conduct that is not itself unconstitutional if the prohibition is appropriate to prevent actual constitutional violations in jurisdictions with a history of discrimination.
- Municipalities in a covered state cannot independently seek bailout; the covered unit is the state as a whole.
- This case signals the high-water mark of congressional deference in the Voting Rights Act era; later limited by Shelby County v Holder (2013).
- The "federalism yields to Civil War Amendments" principle is central to the enforcement-power canon.

### Comparison: Enforcement-power deference, before and after *Boerne*

| Case | Year | Amendment / Section | Test applied | Record demanded | Outcome |
|------|------|---------------------|--------------|-----------------|---------|
| *Katzenbach v Morgan* | 1966 | XIV §5 | McCulloch-style rationality plus a one-way "ratchet" permitting expansion of the substantive floor | Deference to the legislative judgment that the prohibition served the enforcement object | Sustained (English-literacy-test bar valid) |
| *City of Rome* | 1980 | XV §2 | "Appropriate" prophylactic legislation; effects-based bar permissible against jurisdictions with a record of intentional discrimination | Pattern-of-discrimination record from prior preclearance practice; no case-by-case intent showing required | Sustained (Section 5 preclearance with effects test valid) |
| *City of Boerne v Flores* | 1997 | XIV §5 | Congruence and proportionality; remedial only, not substantive | Targeted record of unconstitutional conduct matching the statutory sweep | Invalidated (RFRA exceeded enforcement power as applied to states) |
| *Shelby County v Holder* | 2013 | XV §2 | "Equal sovereignty"; coverage formula must be tied to current conditions | Current-conditions evidence, not historical record alone | Invalidated (VRA §4(b) coverage formula no longer congruent and proportional) |

The table reads in two halves. The Morgan-City of Rome era treats Civil War Amendment enforcement as a positive grant measured by McCulloch deference; the legislative record can be thin so long as the prohibition is "appropriate." Boerne and Shelby reverse the polarity, demanding a fact-specific match between the statute and the constitutional violations Congress is preventing. *City of Rome* survives as a benchmark precedent precisely because *Shelby County* did not formally overrule it; the deferential review *City of Rome* applied to the §5 preclearance machinery was instead retired indirectly through the §4(b) coverage-formula attack.
