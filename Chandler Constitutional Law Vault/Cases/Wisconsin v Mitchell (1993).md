# Wisconsin v Mitchell (1993)

## Memory Jogger

> Todd Mitchell directed a racially motivated attack on a white teenager after watching Mississippi Burning; the Court unanimously upheld Wisconsin's hate crime enhancement because criminal motive has always been a traditional sentencing factor and the chilling effect on pure speech is too speculative to sustain an overbreadth challenge.

---

## Facts

Todd Mitchell, a Black teenager, was among a group that had just watched the film Mississippi Burning and discussed a scene depicting a white man beating a young Black boy while he prayed. Mitchell then turned to his companions and said: "Do you all feel hyped up to move on some white people?" When 14-year-old Gregory Reddick, a white youth, walked by, Mitchell directed the group to attack him, saying: "There goes a white boy; go get him." The group chased Reddick and beat him severely, leaving him unconscious for four days.

Mitchell was convicted of aggravated battery. Under Wisconsin's penalty-enhancement statute, §939.645, a defendant's maximum sentence is increased when the offense is motivated by the victim's race, religion, color, disability, sexual orientation, national origin, or ancestry. Because the jury found beyond a reasonable doubt that Mitchell selected his victim based on race, his maximum sentence was enhanced from two years to seven years. The trial court sentenced him to four years. The Wisconsin Supreme Court struck down the enhancement statute as violative of the First Amendment, relying primarily on R.A.V. v. St. Paul (1992).

---

## Procedural History

Mitchell was convicted in Kenosha County Circuit Court and sentenced to four years in prison under the enhanced provision. The Wisconsin Court of Appeals affirmed. The Wisconsin Supreme Court reversed, holding that the penalty-enhancement statute violated the First Amendment under R.A.V. because it punished offenders' bigoted thought or belief. The State of Wisconsin petitioned for certiorari. The Supreme Court granted cert and reversed unanimously.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority (Unanimous):** Rehnquist (writing for the Court, joined by all eight other Justices)
- **No separate concurrences or dissents**

---

## Holding

Wisconsin's hate crime penalty-enhancement statute, which increases the maximum sentence when a defendant selects a victim based on race, religion, or other protected characteristic, does not violate the First Amendment or the Equal Protection Clause. The statute punishes criminal conduct -- the act of selecting a victim on a prohibited basis -- rather than punishing an offender's beliefs or abstract advocacy. Criminal motive is a traditional factor in sentencing; courts routinely take it into account in imposing punishment. The chilling effect of the statute on free speech is too speculative and attenuated to sustain a facial overbreadth challenge, since there is no realistic danger that the statute will significantly compromise free expression.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Chief Justice Rehnquist distinguished R.A.V. v. St. Paul on the ground that Wisconsin's enhancement statute does not single out speech or symbolic conduct: it targets conduct -- the selection of a crime victim based on protected characteristics. While motive may include beliefs or associations, courts have always considered motive in sentencing, and this alone does not render a statute a First Amendment violation. Rehnquist drew an analogy to federal civil rights statutes, conspiracy laws, and antidiscrimination laws, all of which make proof of motive essential without being invalidated on First Amendment grounds.

Rehnquist next rejected the "chilling effect" argument. Wisconsin argued that the enhancement might chill defendants from expressing racist views because such speech could later be used as evidence of motive. Rehnquist responded that this concern was "too speculative" to support an overbreadth ruling: the prospect that a defendant's prior statements might be used at sentencing does not constitute a substantial impairment of protected speech. Every state and federal court applies similar evidentiary rules to show intent and motive. Finally, Rehnquist noted that Wisconsin had a legitimate interest in punishing bias-motivated crimes more severely because such crimes inflict greater individual and societal harm -- they terrorize entire communities, not just the immediate victim.

## Key Points

- Mitchell stands for the proposition that hate crime **penalty enhancements** do not violate the First Amendment because they target criminal **conduct** (victim selection) rather than the defendant's abstract beliefs or speech.
- The Court distinguished **R.A.V. v. St. Paul** (1992): R.A.V. involved a statute that singled out certain kinds of fighting words for punishment; Mitchell involves an enhancement that increases punishment for criminal conduct motivated by prohibited discrimination.
- **Motive** is a traditional sentencing factor that courts have always considered; the First Amendment does not bar states from treating bias-motivated crimes as more serious offenses.
- The **overbreadth/chilling effect** argument failed because the risk that defendants will self-censor their racist views to avoid generating motive evidence is "too speculative and attenuated" to invalidate the statute on its face.
- Mitchell has significant implications for **evidence**: defendants' prior speech expressing bias (including protected speech) may be introduced at trial or sentencing as evidence of discriminatory motive.
