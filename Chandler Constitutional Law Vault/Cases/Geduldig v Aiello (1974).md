# Geduldig v Aiello (1974)

## Memory Jogger

> Excluding pregnancy-related disabilities from a state disability insurance program is not sex discrimination under the Equal Protection Clause because the line drawn is between pregnant persons and nonpregnant persons, and nonpregnant persons include members of both sexes.

---

## Facts

California operated a disability insurance program funded by employee contributions that paid benefits to private-sector workers who became unable to work because of non-work-related disabilities. The program excluded from coverage disabilities resulting from "normal pregnancy" (though it covered some pregnancy-related complications). Four women challenged the exclusion, arguing that because only women become pregnant, the exclusion constituted sex discrimination in violation of the Equal Protection Clause.

The state defended the exclusion on fiscal grounds: including pregnancy would substantially increase the program's costs, requiring either higher employee contributions, reduced benefits, or a narrowing of other covered conditions. The state argued the classification was fiscal, not gender-based.

---

## Procedural History

A three-judge federal district court in the Northern District of California held the exclusion unconstitutional under the Equal Protection Clause and enjoined its enforcement. The State appealed directly to the Supreme Court, which noted probable jurisdiction.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority:** Stewart (joined by Burger, White, Blackmun, Powell, and Rehnquist)
- **Dissent:** Brennan (joined by Douglas and Marshall)

---

## Holding

California's exclusion of normal pregnancy from its disability insurance program does not constitute sex discrimination under the Equal Protection Clause. The state's classification distinguishes between "pregnant women" and "nonpregnant persons," and because the second group includes both men and women, the classification is not gender-based on its face. Under rational basis review, the state's interest in keeping the program's costs within self-sustaining contributions justifies excluding a costly condition. Absent a showing that the exclusion is a pretext for gender discrimination, the classification survives.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Justice Stewart's majority opinion applies rational basis review rather than heightened scrutiny because it characterizes the classification as one between pregnant and nonpregnant persons rather than between men and women. The opinion concedes that only women can become pregnant, but treats that biological fact as distinguishing the program from a gender classification: nonpregnant persons include members of both sexes, so the line is not drawn along gender lines. Under rational basis review, California's interest in a self-sustaining insurance fund and the high cost of pregnancy coverage provide adequate justification. The state may choose to address one problem at a time and may leave gaps in coverage. The classification is not a pretext for gender discrimination because the record shows the state designed the program as a cost-constrained disability scheme rather than to disadvantage women. Legislatures have wide latitude to design social insurance programs with fiscal limits even if the resulting coverage is underinclusive.

### Dissent (Brennan)

Justice Brennan, joined by Justices Douglas and Marshall, argues that the majority's "pregnant versus nonpregnant" framing masks what is in substance a sex-based classification. Three moves drive the dissent. First, the dissent insists pregnancy is "a gender-linked disability peculiar to women," and excluding it from an otherwise comprehensive disability program creates "a double standard": men receive compensation for disabilities unique to or predominant in males (the dissent lists prostatectomies, circumcision, hemophilia, and gout as examples covered by the program), while women are denied compensation for the one disability unique to their sex. The program's economic effects on women are therefore categorical, not incidental. Second, Brennan would apply the heightened scrutiny signaled by Reed v Reed (1971) and Frontiero v Richardson (1973). He reaffirms his Frontiero position that sex classifications, like race and alienage classifications, are inherently suspect and should receive strict scrutiny. Under that standard, fiscal self-sufficiency cannot justify categorical exclusion because the State has less restrictive alternatives (raising contributions marginally, adjusting benefit amounts, or adopting Title VII's approach of treating pregnancy like any other temporary disability). Third, the dissent charges the majority with reviving the pre-Reed era of Muller v Oregon (1908), Goesaert v Cleary (1948), and Hoyt v Florida (1961), in which legislatures were free to treat women differently on the basis of "traditional" role assumptions. By accepting fiscal reasoning that absorbs a uniquely female disability, the Court allows generalized assumptions about women's reproductive role to shape the scope of a social insurance program and thereby perpetuates the very stereotypes the Court had begun to disassemble. The dissent would affirm the district court's invalidation of the exclusion.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

1. A state tort-claims statute caps damages for pregnancy-related medical malpractice at a lower figure than other medical malpractice. The state defends the cap as a fiscal measure to keep its indemnity fund self-sustaining. Under Geduldig's framing the classification distinguishes pregnancy-related injuries from other injuries rather than men from women. On rational basis review the fiscal justification suffices. The classification survives on the same reasoning that sustained the disability exclusion in Geduldig.

2. A state-administered workers compensation program excludes temporary disabilities associated with elective cosmetic procedures. The state argues the exclusion is necessary to keep the program actuarially sound. Any disparate effect on one sex (because cosmetic procedures disproportionately affect women) is incidental, and under Geduldig the classification is not sex-based unless the plaintiff shows the state used the classification as a pretext for gender discrimination. Same outcome as Geduldig.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

1. A state university health plan covers every disability except those requiring "female reproductive services" (a defined term that includes pregnancy, pregnancy termination, miscarriage, and related postpartum care). The exclusion is openly gender-keyed because it isolates conditions that only women experience and frames them together as a single excluded category. Unlike Geduldig, this classification is unmistakably sex-based on its face: the category tracks gender with no pretense of pregnancy-versus-nonpregnancy framing. Under Craig v Boren or United States v Virginia the classification fails heightened scrutiny absent an exceedingly persuasive justification. Opposite outcome.

2. An employer-provided disability plan governed by Title VII (post-Pregnancy Discrimination Act 1978) excludes pregnancy. Under the PDA, pregnancy must be treated "the same" as other temporary disabilities for employment purposes. The statutory framework overrides Geduldig's constitutional analysis. Opposite outcome.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

A state-funded health plan excludes coverage for prostate-cancer screening on the ground that screening has high false-positive rates and is not uniformly recommended by medical organizations. A male beneficiary sues, arguing the exclusion singles out a sex-specific condition and therefore operates as sex discrimination against men, parallel to the pregnancy exclusion the dissent attacked in Geduldig. The state replies that the exclusion is medically driven (evidence-based coverage limits) and incidentally correlates with sex. Whether the Geduldig framing (nonprostate versus prostate patients, including members of both sexes) controls, and whether the evidence-based justification survives heightened review if the Court treats the classification as sex-based, is genuinely contested.

---

## Critique

Geduldig is the textbook case for the limits of formal equality analysis. The majority's "pregnant versus nonpregnant" framing is analytically coherent in the narrow sense that the excluded group (pregnant persons) is defined by a condition rather than by sex alone, but the framing ignores that the condition is uniquely female and that the exclusion's economic impact falls entirely on women. The opinion therefore stands as the leading example of how a facially gender-neutral classification can reproduce the substantive inequality that heightened scrutiny was designed to address.

Progressive and substantive-equality readings, led by Catharine MacKinnon's dominance critique, treat Geduldig as confirmation that equal protection doctrine asks the wrong question when it focuses on formal categories. Under the dominance account, sex inequality is a social system in which women's reproductive role is central to subordination, and a legal test that refuses to call pregnancy-based classifications sex-based is one that structurally underprotects women. Reva Siegel's anti-subordination reading similarly argues that Geduldig's framing insulates from scrutiny the precise classifications that do the most work reinforcing gender hierarchy. These critiques gained political traction immediately. In 1978, Congress passed the Pregnancy Discrimination Act, amending Title VII to define sex discrimination to include discrimination on the basis of pregnancy, childbirth, and related medical conditions. The PDA statutorily overrides Geduldig for employment-related benefits but does not touch the constitutional holding.

Originalist and formalist responses accept the majority's framing on its own terms: the equal protection clause forbids classifications based on sex, and a classification that turns on a physical condition is not sex-based even if only one sex experiences the condition. On this view the majority draws the right line between real sex classifications (Reed, Frontiero) and programs that merely correlate with sex through neutral criteria. Stewart's opinion is careful to preserve the possibility that a pregnancy exclusion used as a "pretext" for gender discrimination would be scrutinized differently, which formalists treat as a meaningful constraint. Critics respond that the pretext exception is nearly impossible to satisfy given how legislatures describe their aims.

Geduldig's post-1978 status is contested. For statutory employment claims, the PDA makes the case a historical curiosity. For constitutional claims, Geduldig was reaffirmed in Bray v Alexandria Women's Health Clinic (1993), which applied the pregnant-versus-nonpregnant framing to a 1985(3) conspiracy claim. More recently, Dobbs v Jackson Women's Health Organization (2022) cited Geduldig for the proposition that regulation of abortion is not sex-based on its face, reinvigorating the framing in a domain where it had been widely thought moribund. Whether Geduldig survives under a future Court prepared to reconsider sex-classification doctrine is an open question.

Chandler-exam framing: Geduldig is the disfavored but still-binding frame against which later sex-classification cases are measured. Students should separate (a) what Geduldig constitutionally holds (rational basis for pregnancy classifications absent pretext), (b) what the PDA statutorily overrides (Title VII pregnancy treatment), and (c) what Dobbs reinvigorated (the pregnant-versus-nonpregnant framing in the abortion context).

## Key Points

- The "pregnant/nonpregnant" classification is the canonical example of formal equality reasoning that ignores disparate impact on women.
- Congress responded with the Pregnancy Discrimination Act of 1978, overriding Geduldig for Title VII but not for constitutional claims.
- Geduldig remains good law for equal protection purposes, though Bray v Alexandria Women's Health Clinic reaffirmed its holding narrowly.
- Often paired with Bradwell v Illinois and Hoyt v Florida as pre-modern pregnancy/sex-role cases.
- Chandler exam trap: distinguish equal protection doctrine (Geduldig survives) from Title VII statutory doctrine (overridden by PDA).
