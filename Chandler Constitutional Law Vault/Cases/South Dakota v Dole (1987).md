# South Dakota v Dole (1987)

## Memory Jogger

> Congress can indirectly push states toward policies it cannot directly require by attaching strings to federal funds, so long as the conditions clear a four-part test and the financial pressure does not amount to compulsion.

---

## Facts

In 1984 Congress enacted 23 U.S.C. § 158, which directed the Secretary of Transportation to withhold a percentage of federal highway funds from any state that allowed the purchase or public possession of alcohol by persons under twenty-one. The withholding rate was 5% in the first year and 10% in later years. South Dakota permitted nineteen-year-olds to purchase beer containing up to 3.2% alcohol. If South Dakota refused to raise its drinking age, it stood to lose a portion of its federal highway allocation, equal to about 4 to 5 percent of the funds the State would otherwise receive under 23 U.S.C. chapter 1.

South Dakota sued Secretary of Transportation Elizabeth Dole in federal court seeking a declaratory judgment that § 158 violated the spending power and the Twenty-first Amendment. The State argued that Congress was using the spending power to regulate an area, minimum drinking ages, reserved to the states under the Twenty-first Amendment's second section.

---

## Procedural History

The U.S. District Court for the District of South Dakota rejected the State's challenge and upheld § 158. The Eighth Circuit affirmed, reading the spending power broadly and finding no Twenty-first Amendment bar because Congress was regulating indirectly through a condition on funds rather than directly prohibiting state policy.

The Supreme Court granted certiorari and affirmed 7-2. Chief Justice Rehnquist wrote for the majority; Justices Brennan and O'Connor filed separate dissents.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority:** Rehnquist, C.J. (joined by White, Marshall, Blackmun, Powell, Stevens, Scalia) — 7-2
- **Concurrence:** None
- **Dissent:** Brennan; O'Connor

---

## Holding

Congress may use its Article I § 8 spending power to attach conditions on the receipt of federal funds that encourage states to adopt policies Congress could not directly enact, subject to four requirements: the spending must be for the general welfare; the condition must be unambiguous; the condition must be germane to the federal interest in the particular program; and no other constitutional provision may independently bar the condition. A 5% highway-fund withholding is "relatively mild encouragement," not unconstitutional coercion.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Chief Justice Rehnquist recognized that "objectives not thought to be within Article I's enumerated legislative fields" may still be attained through the conditional grant of federal funds. He articulated what has since been called the four-part Dole test: (1) the spending must pursue the general welfare, with substantial deference to Congress; (2) conditions must be stated unambiguously so states can knowingly accept; (3) conditions must be related to the federal interest in the program; and (4) other constitutional provisions may provide an independent bar. The Twenty-first Amendment did not supply such a bar, Rehnquist reasoned, because Congress was not directly regulating drinking ages but conditioning highway funds on a related state policy, and highway safety is plainly connected to drinking-age law. The Court also floated but did not apply a coercion principle drawn from Steward Machine Co. v Davis, concluding that a 5% fund loss fell comfortably on the "encouragement" side of the line.

### Concurrence

There was no concurrence. What is notable is the coalition: Rehnquist wrote for a 7-justice bloc that spanned the ideological spectrum, including Brennan-era liberals (White, Marshall, Blackmun) and originalist-leaning conservatives (Scalia). The breadth of the majority signalled a settled pre-NFIB consensus that conditional spending is a lightly-policed corner of Article I, and it explains why Dole's four-part test dominated doctrine uncontested for a quarter century until NFIB v Sebelius (2012) finally put coercive weight on the "no compulsion" language.

### Dissent (Brennan)

Justice Brennan filed a terse one-paragraph dissent. He agreed with O'Connor that "regulation of the minimum age of purchasers of liquor falls squarely within the ambit of those powers reserved to the States by the Twenty-first Amendment" and drew the straightforward doctrinal inference: because Section 2 of the Twenty-first Amendment reserves this regulatory area to the states, the Amendment itself is an independent constitutional bar under the fourth Dole prong. Congress therefore could not do indirectly through a funding condition what the Twenty-first Amendment forbids it from doing directly. Brennan's move was primarily a sovereignty-preservation reading of the Twenty-first Amendment, and it signalled the fragility of the "independent bar" test: if Section 2 does not count as a bar, it is hard to see what state-reserved subject matter would.

### Dissent (O'Connor)

Justice O'Connor wrote the substantive dissent, focusing on the third Dole prong (relatedness/germaneness). She accepted the majority's four-part framework but argued that a drinking-age condition is not "reasonably related to the purpose of the expenditure" of highway construction funds. Her key doctrinal move was a sharper definition of what a "condition" is: a spending condition is valid only where it "specifies in some way how the money should be spent, so that Congress' intent in making the grant will be effectuated." A requirement that goes beyond specifying how the money is spent is "not a condition, but a regulation, which is valid only if it falls within one of Congress' delegated regulatory powers." Applied to Section 158, the statute did not specify how highway funds must be spent (for example, on sober-driving enforcement or median barriers) — it regulated a wholly separate area of state police power. She also attacked the drunk-driving rationale as both over- and under-inclusive, noting the condition "stops teenagers from drinking even when they are not about to drive on interstate highways." O'Connor's germaneness framework presaged the tightened relatedness analysis later adopted by NFIB's Medicaid-expansion holding and by lower-court coercion and relatedness challenges in the ACA and immigration-enforcement grant contexts.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

1. **Hypo:** Congress conditions 3% of federal Title I education funds on state adoption of a reading-proficiency benchmark aligned with a federal literacy program. **Why:** The condition is unambiguous, germane to the federal interest in improving literacy funded by Title I, pursues the general welfare, and a 3% withholding is well within the Dole "mild encouragement" zone. No independent constitutional bar is present.
2. **Hypo:** Congress conditions 2% of federal public-health grants on state adoption of a uniform infectious-disease reporting protocol. **Why:** The reporting protocol directly specifies how the federally-funded public-health activity is carried out, so it satisfies O'Connor's stricter "specifies how the money should be spent" test as well as Rehnquist's germaneness prong. The withholding is small enough that the coercion objection does not bite.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

3. **Hypo:** Congress conditions federal highway funds on a state's agreement to impose corporal punishment on drunk drivers. **Why:** Even if related to highway safety and a small percentage, the condition induces states to enact a policy that would itself violate the Eighth Amendment. Under the fourth Dole prong (no independent constitutional bar), the condition fails: "the power [to spend] may not be used to induce the States to engage in activities that would themselves be unconstitutional."
4. **Hypo:** Congress conditions federal agricultural-subsidy funds on state adoption of a minimum wage for restaurant workers. **Why:** The condition fails the germaneness (relatedness) prong under Rehnquist's test and fails even more clearly under O'Connor's stricter test: restaurant minimum wages do not specify how agricultural-subsidy money is to be spent and bear no meaningful relationship to the federal interest in farm subsidies.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

5. **Hypo:** Congress conditions the entirety of Medicaid funding (roughly 10% of the average state's budget) on state adoption of a new single-payer primary care coverage expansion that Congress could not enact directly under Article I. **Why:** Under pre-NFIB Dole this probably survives — the condition is germane to health-care spending, stated clearly, and in service of the general welfare. But after NFIB v Sebelius (2012), withholding all Medicaid funds for refusing a new and severable expansion triggers the coercion principle that Steward Machine and Dole flagged but left unenforced. The hypo falls on whichever side of the Dole/NFIB coercion line the court draws — the exact percentage at which "mild encouragement" becomes "a gun to the head" remains judicially unresolved.

---

## Critique

**Progressive / structural critique.** Commentators in the federalism tradition (Lynn Baker, Mitchell Berman) have argued that Dole's four-part test is a paper tiger. Three of the four prongs are essentially non-justiciable — "general welfare" gets total deference, "unambiguous" is a drafting fix, and "independent bar" is a null set if even the Twenty-first Amendment cannot qualify. That leaves germaneness as the only operative limit, and Rehnquist applied it so loosely (any plausible nexus suffices) that Congress can in practice purchase state regulatory compliance on any subject by pricing the withholding below the coercion threshold. The result is a structural end-run around the enumerated-powers limits on Article I that the Court took seriously in New York v United States (1992) and Printz v United States (1997): Congress cannot commandeer state legislatures directly, but it can effectively buy them off at a price.

**Originalist / textualist critique.** Justice Thomas has argued (most fully in his Cutter v Wilkinson (2005) and NFIB concurrences) that the spending power does not reach "objectives not thought to be within Article I's enumerated legislative fields" in the first place, so the entire Dole framework is miscalibrated. On this reading, only spending that directly effectuates an enumerated power (raising armies, establishing post offices, regulating interstate commerce) is permitted. Dissenting originalists and Madison scholars also point to the Madisonian position, discarded in United States v Butler (1936), that the General Welfare Clause limits the taxing power to objects enumerated elsewhere in Article I. The critique is less that Dole applies its own test poorly and more that Dole inherited a post-New Deal spending-power framework that reads the General Welfare Clause far more capaciously than any plausible 1787 understanding would support.

**Internal doctrinal tension.** Even accepting Dole's framework, the opinion's treatment of coercion is in tension with its own language. Rehnquist invokes Steward Machine's "pressure turns into compulsion" formulation, disclaims any judicial role in enforcing it, and then validates a condition whose "success in achieving the congressional objective" was offered by South Dakota as evidence of coercion. That judicial retreat from the coercion prong is what NFIB v Sebelius later had to undo, and scholars have noted that Dole's dictum-coercion/real-germaneness split produced 25 years of doctrinal drift.

## Key Points

- Canonical statement of the four-part conditional-spending test.
- Vote: 7-2. Rehnquist majority; Brennan and O'Connor separate dissents.
- Coercion doctrine is teed up but not seriously applied until NFIB v Sebelius (2012) finds a Medicaid expansion coercive.
- O'Connor's dissent on germaneness becomes influential in later conditional-spending scholarship.
