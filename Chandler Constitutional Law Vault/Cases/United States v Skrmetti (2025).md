# United States v Skrmetti (2025)

## Memory Jogger

> Tennessee banned puberty blockers and cross-sex hormones for gender-affirming care for minors; the Court applied rational basis review -- not heightened scrutiny -- because it held the law classifies by age and medical use, not by sex or transgender status, and the state's interest in medical caution for minors satisfies rational basis given scientific uncertainty.

---

## Facts

Tennessee enacted Senate Bill 1 (SB1), which prohibits healthcare providers from administering or prescribing puberty blockers or cross-sex hormones to minors when the treatment is intended to (1) enable a minor to identify with a gender different from the minor's sex, or (2) treat gender dysphoria, gender identity disorder, or gender incongruence. The same medications remain available to minors for other medical purposes -- for example, puberty blockers used to treat precocious puberty, and hormones used to treat endocrine disorders.

The plaintiffs included three adolescents diagnosed with gender dysphoria and their parents, who sued to challenge SB1. They argued that the law discriminated on the basis of sex and transgender status, requiring heightened scrutiny, and that it failed such scrutiny. The United States intervened as a plaintiff. The Biden Administration argued that the law was a form of sex discrimination because it regulated treatments depending on whether the patient's intended use aligned with or diverged from the patient's birth sex.

---

## Procedural History

The District Court for the Middle District of Tennessee preliminarily enjoined SB1. The Sixth Circuit reversed, finding the law subject to only rational basis review and upholding it. The Supreme Court affirmed the Sixth Circuit, applying rational basis review and sustaining SB1.

## Holding

Tennessee's law prohibiting puberty blockers and cross-sex hormones for transgender minors is subject to rational basis review -- not heightened scrutiny -- under the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. The law does not classify on the basis of sex because it categorizes equally based on age (minors vs. adults) and medical use (the condition being treated), not on the patient's sex. Nor does it classify on the basis of transgender status, a category the Court declined to recognize as suspect or quasi-suspect. Under rational basis, the law is upheld because there is a reasonably conceivable basis -- concern about uncertain long-term effects of these treatments on minors -- for the legislative classification.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Chief Justice Roberts' central analytical move was to characterize what SB1 actually classifies. The plaintiffs argued SB1 is facially sex-based because whether a minor may receive testosterone, for example, depends on whether the minor is biologically male or female -- i.e., on sex. Roberts rejected this framing. The law, he said, classifies on two axes: (1) age -- these treatments are restricted for minors, not adults; and (2) medical use -- the restrictions apply only when the treatment's purpose is to address gender dysphoria or gender incongruence, not when prescribed for other conditions. Those are not sex classifications, even if sex is one feature of the underlying biological reality, because the law applies equally to all persons who would seek treatment for these diagnoses.

Having concluded that no heightened scrutiny applied, Roberts addressed rational basis. States have broad discretion to regulate in areas of medical and scientific uncertainty, and SB1 was adopted against a background of genuine scientific uncertainty about the long-term effects of puberty blockers and hormones on adolescent development. The majority cited recent regulatory reassessments in European countries that had previously been more permissive, underscoring that expert consensus was unsettled. That is precisely the kind of environment in which courts defer to legislative judgment rather than imposing their own assessment of the medical evidence.

Roberts explicitly declined to decide whether transgender persons constitute a suspect or quasi-suspect class, finding that question unnecessary because SB1 did not classify on the basis of transgender status at all.

## Key Points

- Skrmetti holds that **rational basis review** -- not heightened scrutiny -- applies to laws restricting gender-affirming care for minors, because the law classifies by **age** and **medical use**, not by sex or transgender status.
- The majority explicitly **declined to decide** whether transgender individuals constitute a suspect or quasi-suspect class, leaving that question open for future litigation.
- The **classification-framing technique**: by characterizing SB1 as a law about what condition a treatment may address (rather than who may receive it), the majority avoided triggering heightened scrutiny -- a method that may be widely replicated.
- Under **rational basis**, states have "wide discretion" to legislate in areas of medical and scientific uncertainty; the Court pointed to European regulatory reversals on puberty blockers as evidence that the science is unsettled.
- Sotomayor's dissent invoked **Bostock v. Clayton County** (2020), arguing that discriminating based on transgender status necessarily involves discriminating based on sex, and that the Bostock logic should extend to the Equal Protection Clause.
