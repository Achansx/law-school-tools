# Milliken v Bradley (1974)

## Memory Jogger

> An interdistrict busing remedy for school segregation is unavailable absent a showing that the racially discriminatory acts of one district caused significant segregation in another, or that district lines were drawn on a racial basis.

---

## Facts

Detroit's public schools were heavily segregated. A federal district court found that both the Detroit Board of Education and the State of Michigan had committed de jure acts of segregation contributing to the racial concentration of black students in city schools. By the early 1970s, Detroit's public school enrollment was predominantly African-American, while the surrounding suburban districts were overwhelmingly white. Because a Detroit-only remedy would not achieve meaningful desegregation given the demographic reality, the district court ordered a metropolitan remedy that would bus students across fifty-four separate school district lines, consolidating Detroit with suburban districts into a single desegregation plan.

The suburban districts had not been shown to have committed de jure segregation, nor had the boundary lines between Detroit and the suburbs been drawn on a racial basis. The remedy nonetheless required the suburbs to participate on the theory that no intradistrict Detroit-only remedy could cure the constitutional violation.

---

## Procedural History

The district court found constitutional violations by Detroit and Michigan state defendants and ordered a metropolitan interdistrict busing remedy. The Sixth Circuit affirmed. The Supreme Court granted certiorari, limiting its review to the scope of the remedy rather than the underlying finding of liability.

## Holding

A federal court may not impose an interdistrict remedy for school segregation absent a showing that the racially discriminatory acts of one or more school districts caused segregation in an adjacent district, or that the district lines themselves were drawn for racial reasons. Because the record established de jure segregation only within Detroit and not in the suburban districts, and because no showing was made that the Detroit-suburb district boundaries were racially gerrymandered, the multi-district metropolitan remedy exceeded the scope of the constitutional violation and must be vacated.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Chief Justice Burger's majority opinion rests on the principle that the nature and scope of an equitable remedy must match the scope of the constitutional violation found. The district court found de jure segregation by Detroit and state officials affecting Detroit schools; it did not find that the fifty-three suburban districts had engaged in de jure segregation or that their boundary lines were drawn for racial reasons. Without such findings, the interdistrict remedy swept beyond the violation. The majority emphasizes the deep tradition of local control of public education, describing school district autonomy as central to public education in the United States. Expanding a remedy across district lines absent interdistrict fault would transfer governance from local boards to a federal court and disregard the principle that equitable relief must be tailored to the wrong. The opinion distinguishes single-district cases (Swann v Charlotte-Mecklenburg) where district-wide busing was permissible because the violation was district-wide.

## Key Points

- Establishes that remedies must be tailored to the violation -- no interdistrict remedy without interdistrict violation.
- The "local control" rationale becomes a recurring structural theme in subsequent desegregation and education cases.
- Marshall's dissent is historically significant as the "death knell" prediction that northern metropolitan segregation would persist.
- Often paired with Swann v Charlotte-Mecklenburg (permissible district-wide busing) and San Antonio v Rodriguez (no fundamental right to education) as the core desegregation-remedy trilogy of the 1970s.
- Chandler emphasis: Milliken illustrates the constitutional-remedy distinction and frames the limits of federal judicial power in public school cases.
