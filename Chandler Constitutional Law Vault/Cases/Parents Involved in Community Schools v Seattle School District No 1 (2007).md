# Parents Involved in Community Schools v Seattle School District No 1 (2007)

## Memory Jogger

> Voluntary K-12 student assignment plans using individual racial classifications as a tiebreaker failed strict scrutiny -- racial balancing is not a compelling interest and the plans were not narrowly tailored -- but Justice Kennedy's controlling concurrence preserved some race-conscious options.

---

## Facts

Two consolidated cases challenged race-conscious student assignment plans in public K-12 schools.

Seattle School District No. 1 operated ten regular public high schools. In 1998, the district adopted a student assignment plan allowing ninth graders to rank preferred schools; when a school became oversubscribed, the district applied a series of tiebreakers. The first favored students with siblings already enrolled. The second, a racial tiebreaker, depended on whether a student's race would bring the school's enrollment closer to the district-wide average of approximately 41 percent white and 59 percent nonwhite students. Geographic proximity was the next tiebreaker. The plan typically affected roughly 300 students per year; between 80 and 90 percent of students received their first-choice assignment.

Jefferson County Public Schools (Louisville, Kentucky) had operated under a court desegregation decree since 1975 and was declared a unitary district in 2000, dissolving the decree. In 2001, Jefferson County voluntarily adopted a new assignment plan requiring all nonmagnet schools to maintain Black enrollment between 15 and 50 percent, and applying racial guidelines to elementary school assignments and transfer requests.

Parents in both districts sued, contending that assigning students to different schools based on race violated the Fourteenth Amendment's equal protection guarantee. Lower courts in both cases upheld the plans; the Supreme Court consolidated them on certiorari.

---

## Procedural History

Seattle: Parents Involved in Community Schools, a nonprofit corporation, sued Seattle School District No. 1. The Ninth Circuit upheld the plan en banc. The Supreme Court reversed.

Jefferson County: Crystal Meredith sued Jefferson County Public Schools on behalf of her son. The Sixth Circuit upheld the plan. The Supreme Court reversed.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Plurality (Parts I, II, III-A, IV):** Chief Justice Roberts (joined by Scalia, Thomas, Alito) -- held that the plans served no compelling interest, that the diversity interest from Grutter does not extend to K-12, and that racial balancing is unconstitutional
- **Majority (on strict scrutiny application and unconstitutionality):** Roberts, Scalia, Thomas, Alito, and Kennedy (5-4 overall judgment reversing both lower courts)
- **Concurrence (controlling on narrow tailoring/compelling interest in Part III-B):** Justice Kennedy (joined the judgment but not the plurality's reasoning that diversity is never a compelling interest in K-12; held the specific plans were not narrowly tailored)
- **Concurrence (separate):** Justice Thomas (emphasizing the anticlassification principle; critiquing the dissent's historical framing)
- **Dissent:** Justice Stevens (emphasizing doctrinal inconsistency with prior race-conscious remedies)
- **Dissent:** Justice Breyer (joined by Stevens, Ginsburg, Souter; defending the plans' compelling interest and narrow tailoring on the grounds that voluntary integration serves educational goals and avoids resegregation)

---

## Holding

Government distribution of burdens or benefits based on individual racial classifications is subject to strict scrutiny, and the student assignment plans at issue fail both prongs. As to compelling interest: racial balancing -- assigning students based on a school's deviation from the district-wide racial average -- is not a compelling interest; racial proportionality is "patently unconstitutional" as an end in itself. As to narrow tailoring: the plans were not narrowly tailored because they relied on blunt racial classifications (white/nonwhite or Black/other) rather than individualized consideration of diversity factors, and neither district had given serious good-faith consideration to race-neutral alternatives before adopting race-conscious measures.

---

## Analysis

### Plurality (Roberts, Scalia, Thomas, Alito -- Parts III-A, IV)

Chief Justice Roberts' plurality opinion holds that the interest asserted by the districts -- achieving racial balance in student enrollment -- is not a compelling interest at all; it is the very thing the Equal Protection Clause prohibits governments from pursuing. Grutter's diversity interest, endorsed in the context of holistic higher education admissions, does not translate to K-12 assignment plans that use race as a binary or near-binary tiebreaker. Roberts distinguishes Grutter: there, race was one of many individualized factors in a holistic review; here, race is determinative once a student falls on the wrong side of the racial cutoff. Roberts closes with the memorable declaration: "The way to stop discrimination on the basis of race is to stop discriminating on the basis of race."

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

1. **Hypo:** A suburban school district assigns students to schools based on a binary racial criterion (white vs. minority), calibrated to keep each school within 10 percentage points of the district's overall racial makeup. **Why:** This is materially identical to the Seattle plan; racial balancing for its own sake is patently unconstitutional under the plurality and Kennedy concurrence alike.
<!-- ENRICH: Note that Kennedy's concurrence does not change the result for plans that rely on binary individual racial classifications. -->

2. **Hypo:** A school district, after finding that race-neutral magnet programs reduced racial imbalance by only 2 percent, implements a race-based lottery that directly assigns students by race. **Why:** Narrow tailoring requires serious, good-faith consideration of race-neutral alternatives; a 2 percent improvement does not satisfy the "good-faith consideration" standard, especially where a race-based lottery is the least tailored possible means.
<!-- ENRICH: Analyze the distinction between evidence of good-faith consideration (required) and a showing that race-neutral alternatives are completely ineffective (not required). -->

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

3. **Hypo:** A school district draws new attendance zone boundaries with awareness that certain zones contain racially identifiable neighborhoods, for the purpose of achieving racially mixed schools, without assigning individual students by race. **Why:** Kennedy's concurrence explicitly preserved this approach; the constitutional problem in Parents Involved was individual racial classification, not race-awareness in policy design.
<!-- ENRICH: Develop Kennedy's "targeted" vs. "individualized" distinction and explain how race-conscious policies that avoid individual classification pass constitutional muster under his framework. -->

4. **Hypo:** A district under an active court desegregation decree uses race-conscious assignment to satisfy its court-ordered obligations. **Why:** Green v. County School Board's affirmative-duty rationale and court-supervised remedial decrees occupy different constitutional ground than voluntary action by a district that has achieved unitary status; Parents Involved specifically distinguished the remedial context.
<!-- ENRICH: Discuss how the unitary-status determination dissolves the remedial context and leaves districts as voluntary actors subject to the ordinary strict scrutiny framework. -->

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

5. **Hypo:** A school district deploys a facially race-neutral "socioeconomic tiebreaker" that, by design, correlates closely with race because the district's segregated housing patterns are primarily along class lines; the district openly acknowledges the racial purpose but uses only income data in the formal assignment algorithm. **Why:** Kennedy's concurrence suggests race-conscious but race-neutral means are permissible; but if the facially neutral criterion is a transparent proxy for race, the distinction from individualized racial classification may be illusory -- and lower courts are genuinely divided on how much racial intent can accompany a facially neutral mechanism.

## Key Points

- No majority opinion on several key propositions; Kennedy's concurrence is the controlling opinion for what school districts may permissibly do going forward.
- Kennedy's concurrence preserves race-conscious but non-individually-classifying measures: strategic school siting, race-aware zone drawing, faculty recruitment, enrollment tracking.
- Roberts' "stop discriminating on the basis of race" line vs. Breyer's closing "it is not often in the law that so few have so quickly changed so much" -- a sharp exchange that captures the doctrinal stakes.
- The decision effectively limits Grutter to higher education's holistic admissions context; it does not extend to K-12 binary or near-binary racial tiebreakers.
- Connection to SFFA v. Harvard (2023): Roberts' anticlassification logic in Parents Involved reappears in the SFFA majority, which he also authored.
