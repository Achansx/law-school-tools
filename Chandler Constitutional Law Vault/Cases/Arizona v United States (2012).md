# Arizona v United States (2012)

## Memory Jogger

> Three provisions of Arizona's SB 1070 immigration-enforcement statute are preempted by federal law, but the "show me your papers" provision authorizing status checks during lawful stops survives a facial preemption challenge because it can be enforced consistently with federal immigration law.

---

## Facts

In 2010 Arizona enacted Senate Bill 1070 to address concerns about the federal government's enforcement of immigration law within the state. Four provisions were challenged. Section 3 made it a state misdemeanor for an alien to fail to carry federal alien-registration documents. Section 5(C) criminalized an unauthorized alien's seeking or performing work in Arizona. Section 6 authorized warrantless arrests of persons state officers had probable cause to believe had committed a removable public offense. Section 2(B) required state officers conducting a lawful stop, detention, or arrest to make a reasonable attempt to determine the person's immigration status if reasonable suspicion existed, and to check the status of every arrested person before release.

The federal government sued Arizona, arguing that the Supremacy Clause preempted all four provisions because Congress had occupied the field of alien registration, already criminalized unauthorized employment-related conduct in a balanced federal scheme, assigned arrest decisions to federal officials, and intended unitary federal control over immigration enforcement.

---

## Procedural History

The district court preliminarily enjoined all four provisions, and the Ninth Circuit affirmed. The Supreme Court granted certiorari, consolidating the preemption questions for review.

---

---

## Holding

Sections 3, 5(C), and 6 of SB 1070 are preempted. Section 3 is field-preempted because Congress occupied the entire field of alien registration through the comprehensive federal Registration Act scheme. Section 5(C) is obstacle-preempted because Congress in IRCA made a deliberate choice to sanction employers rather than impose criminal liability on unauthorized workers, and Arizona's contrary criminal penalty conflicts with that balance. Section 6 is obstacle-preempted because it authorizes state officers to make unilateral arrest decisions on removability without federal supervision, disrupting the unified federal enforcement system. Section 2(B), the status-check provision, survives facial challenge because nothing in the federal scheme categorically forbids state officers from checking immigration status during lawful stops; future as-applied challenges remain possible if its enforcement produces unreasonable detention or systematic constitutional violations.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Justice Kennedy's majority opinion structures the analysis around three preemption doctrines. Field preemption forbids state regulation where Congress has so thoroughly occupied a field that no room remains for state action; Congress did so through the Registration Act, making Section 3's state-law duplicate impermissible. Obstacle preemption forbids state laws that stand as an obstacle to the accomplishment of federal objectives; Section 5(C) flunks because Congress in IRCA deliberately chose not to criminalize the unauthorized worker and Arizona's criminal penalty frustrates that choice, and Section 6 flunks because assigning state officers unilateral authority to arrest on removability interferes with the discretionary, centralized federal enforcement scheme. Section 2(B) survives because the state can attempt to determine immigration status during an otherwise lawful stop without itself usurping federal authority. Kennedy reserves as-applied challenges that could arise if the provision is used to justify prolonged detention or to institute racial profiling, leaving its ultimate constitutionality open on a more developed record.

## Key Points

- A textbook multi-provision preemption analysis using all three preemption doctrines (field, obstacle, conflict) in a single opinion.
- Section 2(B)'s survival on facial challenge illustrates the limits of pre-enforcement preemption and invites subsequent as-applied challenges.
- IRCA's balanced employer-sanction scheme is the key statutory predicate for §5(C)'s obstacle preemption.
- Field preemption requires a comprehensive and occupied scheme, not merely federal regulation in the general area.
- Chandler emphasis: Arizona pairs with Chamber of Commerce v Whiting (2011) and INS v Chadha as the modern immigration-preemption trilogy and illustrates the federal executive's centralized enforcement role.
