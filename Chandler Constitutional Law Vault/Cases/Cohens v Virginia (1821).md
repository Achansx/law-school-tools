# Cohens v Virginia (1821)

## Memory Jogger

> Chief Justice Marshall, over Virginia's objection, held that the Supreme Court has appellate jurisdiction over state criminal judgments that present federal questions, then ruled on the merits that a federal lottery statute for the District of Columbia did not authorize ticket sales in states that forbade them.

---

## Facts

In 1802 Congress authorized the Corporation of the City of Washington to raise revenue by conducting lotteries for municipal improvements. The City's authorized agents sold lottery tickets in Norfolk, Virginia, through the Cohen brothers, Philip J. and Mendes I. Cohen. Virginia law, however, prohibited the sale of out-of-state lottery tickets within its borders. A Norfolk justice of the peace convicted the Cohens and fined them $100 under the Virginia statute.

The Cohens invoked the federal lottery statute as a defense: because Congress had authorized the Washington lottery, they argued, its tickets should be saleable in Virginia. They further contended that any Virginia statute penalizing such sales was an unconstitutional interference with a federal power exercised under the Commerce Clause and the Necessary and Proper Clause. After the state court affirmed their conviction, the Cohens sought review in the Supreme Court of the United States under Section 25 of the Judiciary Act of 1789.

Virginia moved to dismiss the writ for want of jurisdiction, arguing that the Eleventh Amendment barred suits against a state by its own citizens and that Article III did not permit appellate review of state criminal judgments. The political context was charged: Virginia's leadership, including Spencer Roane and Thomas Ritchie, had waged a sustained public campaign against Martin v Hunter's Lessee (1816) and viewed Cohens as the decisive confrontation with the Marshall Court.

---

## Procedural History

The Norfolk Borough Court convicted the Cohens and fined them. Under Virginia law the conviction was final at that level for a petty offense, and no further state appeal was available. The Cohens obtained a writ of error from the United States Supreme Court under Section 25 of the Judiciary Act. Virginia appeared only to challenge jurisdiction, not to defend the merits.

The Supreme Court unanimously held that it had appellate jurisdiction over the state judgment, then affirmed the conviction on the merits by construing the federal lottery statute narrowly.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority:** Marshall, C.J. (unanimous) — 7-0

---

## Holding

The Supreme Court has appellate jurisdiction over state court judgments that turn on federal questions, including state criminal judgments. The Eleventh Amendment does not bar such appellate review because a writ of error is not an original suit commenced against the state. On the merits, the federal statute authorizing the City of Washington to conduct lotteries does not by implication authorize the sale of those tickets in states where such sales are prohibited by state law; the power granted was local in nature and did not express a clear intent to displace state penal laws.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Chief Justice Marshall treated the jurisdictional question as having been substantially resolved by Martin v Hunter's Lessee five years earlier but took the occasion to restate and fortify the reasoning. Appellate review of state judgments presenting federal questions is essential to uniformity in the interpretation of federal law and is the ordinary mechanism by which the supremacy of federal law is vindicated where states are hostile or indifferent. A writ of error is a continuation of the proceeding below, not an original suit, so the Eleventh Amendment does not bar the Court from examining a judgment the state itself has rendered. On the merits, Marshall deployed a canon of narrow construction when federal statutes would otherwise displace state penal laws. Congress's grant of lottery authority was a local power tied to municipal improvement within the District of Columbia, not a command to override state prohibitions. Without a clear and unequivocal expression of intent by Congress, a federal statute authorizing a local lottery does not license the extraterritorial sale of its tickets in states that forbid such sales. The Cohens' conviction therefore stood.

### Concurrence

No separate concurrence was filed. The unanimity is doctrinally significant because Cohens was decided under intense Jeffersonian political pressure. Spencer Roane's public essays in the Richmond Enquirer (writing as "Algernon Sidney" and "Hampden") had attacked Martin v Hunter's Lessee (1816) and McCulloch v Maryland (1819) as illegitimate nationalist overreach, and Thomas Jefferson himself had written privately that Cohens was the decisive case in which Virginia should resist federal judicial supremacy. The Marshall Court still carried Jeffersonian appointees (Justices William Johnson and Thomas Todd among them), and a divided decision on the jurisdictional question would have given the states'-rights critique a partial win to quote against Martin. Marshall's ability to hold every Justice to a single opinion — including Justice Johnson, Jefferson's first appointee, who had pushed back against nationalist reasoning in earlier cases — is the concurrence-shaped absence that carries meaning in this opinion. Unanimity here was itself a rhetorical move against the compact-theory critique.

### Dissent

No dissent was filed, and the silence is doctrinally weighty precisely because of what was in the public record. Roane's Richmond Enquirer essays had argued that the Constitution was a compact among sovereign states, that the Supreme Court was itself a party's tribunal when federal questions touched state interests, and that appellate review of state judgments was therefore illegitimate. Marshall addresses these arguments at length in the jurisdictional portion of the opinion without ever naming Roane. The rhetorical choice is deliberate: refuting a published attack by responding to its substance rather than its source would have looked defensive and elevated the attack; responding silently but comprehensively treated Roane's position as one that could be overcome on the merits by any fair reader. The absence of a written dissent meant there was no in-Court counter-voice for the Roane position, which starved the compact-theory critique of the one authority (a Supreme Court Justice's published dissent) that might have carried it into later cases. The effect was to bank the jurisdictional holding for good.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

1. **State drug-possession conviction with federal-law defense.** A defendant convicted in state court of marijuana possession argues that federal medical-marijuana research exemptions protected his conduct; the state court rejects the federal defense and affirms. He petitions the United States Supreme Court under 28 U.S.C. Section 1257 (the modern successor to Section 25). Cohens cleanly establishes that the federal question presented in a state criminal judgment is reviewable on writ of certiorari (historically writ of error), and that the state's sovereign-immunity defense does not bar the review because the case reaches the Court as a continuation of the state-court proceeding, not as an original suit. The state wins or loses on the federal-defense merits, but it does not get a jurisdictional dismissal.

2. **District of Columbia zoning license spilling across state lines.** Congress authorizes a municipal entity in the District of Columbia to issue beverage-sales licenses whose terms (hours of operation, minimum age) are more permissive than neighboring Maryland and Virginia statutes. A licensee operates a branch in Alexandria, Virginia, claiming the D.C. authority travels with the license. Virginia arrests him under its beverage laws. Under Cohens' narrow-construction canon, absent a clear and unequivocal congressional expression of intent to override state penal laws, the D.C. license does not carry extraterritorial immunity, and the Virginia prosecution stands. The canon is the same one Cohens deployed to uphold Virginia's lottery-sale conviction.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

3. **Original damages action against a state.** A private plaintiff files an original action in federal district court against the Commonwealth of Virginia seeking damages for an alleged federal constitutional tort. The Eleventh Amendment squarely bars the suit because, unlike Cohens, this is an original suit commenced against a state, not an appellate review of a judgment the state itself has rendered. Hans v Louisiana (1890) extends the bar to suits by citizens of the same state, and modern sovereign-immunity doctrine (Alden v Maine (1999), Seminole Tribe v Florida (1996)) reinforces it. Cohens' carve-out for writs of error does not rescue an original complaint.

4. **Federal statute explicitly preempting state lottery-sale bans.** Congress amends the D.C. lottery statute to authorize the sale of D.C. lottery tickets "in every State of the Union notwithstanding any State law prohibiting such sale." A state prosecutes a ticket seller under its own anti-lottery statute. The Cohens narrow-construction canon now comes out the opposite way: with clear and unequivocal congressional expression of intent to override state penal laws, the Supremacy Clause displaces the state prohibition and the federal authorization controls. Cohens is not actually about what Congress cannot do; it is about what Congress will not be taken to have done silently. Explicit preemption changes the outcome.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

5. **Federal conditional-spending lottery-enforcement scheme.** Congress conditions a grant of federal transportation funds on state enactment and enforcement of lottery-marketing rules that match federal standards. Some states adopt the rules; a litigant in one of those states is convicted under the state scheme and argues that the federal-conditional-spending structure effectively commandeers the state's enforcement apparatus in violation of the anti-commandeering doctrine announced in Printz v United States (1997) and New York v United States (1992). Cohens does not itself answer this, because Cohens addresses what Congress has or has not authorized via a D.C. corporate power, not what Congress may require states to do. The hypo is a fence-sitter because it exposes the limit of Cohens' jurisdictional and narrow-construction holdings: they resolve the older question of whether federal and state penal schemes can coexist by reading each narrowly, but they leave the twentieth-century anti-commandeering doctrine intact as the rule for coercive conditional-spending arrangements. The answer turns on whether the condition is coercive under the South Dakota v Dole (1987) / NFIB v Sebelius (2012) framework, not on anything Cohens holds.

## Critique

**Progressive-nationalist critique.** The dominant twentieth-century reading of Cohens (developed by William W. Van Alstyne, Martin Flaherty, and others) treats the opinion as the consolidating move in Marshall's nationalist project that began with Marbury (1803), McCulloch (1819), and Martin (1816). Where Martin established appellate review over state civil judgments resting on federal questions, Cohens extended that review to state criminal judgments and did so in the face of a nearly-peak states'-rights political backlash. The extension mattered because state criminal processes were then (as now) a primary mechanism by which state sovereignty was expressed, and without appellate review of those processes the federal judiciary would have been unable to enforce the supremacy of federal law in the entire criminal-law domain. From this perspective Cohens is not a technical expansion of jurisdiction but the indispensable predicate for modern federal constitutional criminal procedure: every incorporation-era decision from Gitlow (1925) forward depends on the availability of the writ of error (now certiorari) path that Cohens locked in.

**Originalist / compact-theory critique.** Roane's Richmond Enquirer essays ("Algernon Sidney" and "Hampden") argued that the Constitution is a compact among sovereign states; that the Eleventh Amendment, adopted in 1798 in response to Chisholm v Georgia (1793), reflects a sweeping sovereign-immunity principle going beyond its literal text; and that Section 25 of the Judiciary Act (to the extent it purports to authorize appellate review of state judgments) is unconstitutional. Modern originalist scholarship (Akhil Amar, Kurt Lash, William Fletcher) is split. Amar's "two-tier" reading of Article III vindicates Marshall's holding that the Court's original and appellate jurisdiction serve different structural functions, and treats the Eleventh Amendment as a narrow bar on original diversity suits rather than a wholesale state-sovereignty shield. Critics on the compact-theory side (Lash on the Reconstruction reworking of sovereignty, and earlier work in the states'-rights tradition) argue that the Eleventh Amendment's drafting history supports a broader reading — one that would cover any federal-court proceeding in which the state is the real party and is not participating voluntarily. Marshall's answer — that a writ of error is a continuation of the state's own proceeding and so not a "commencement" of a suit against the state — is textually plausible but carries weight only if one accepts the functional framing that the state's participation below removes the sovereign-immunity concern. The originalist critique is that this framing owes more to nationalist judicial instinct than to the literal text of the Eleventh Amendment.

## Key Points

- Reaffirms and extends Martin v Hunter's Lessee to state criminal judgments.
- Vote: unanimous. Marshall writes despite intense Virginia political pressure.
- Establishes narrow-construction canon for federal statutes that would displace state penal laws.
- Eleventh Amendment does not bar appellate review because a writ of error is not an original suit.
- Frequently invoked alongside Martin when justifying federal judicial supremacy over state courts.
