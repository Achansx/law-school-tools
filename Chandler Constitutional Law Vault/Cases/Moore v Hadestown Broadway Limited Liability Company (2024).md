# Moore v Hadestown Broadway Limited Liability Company (2024)

## Memory Jogger

**Broadway casting as compelled speech.** Camille Moore, a Black actress, was removed from Hadestown's Workers Chorus when producers realized an all-Black chorus was expressing an unintended "white savior story." She sued for race discrimination. The court dismissed most claims: casting decisions in a live theatrical production are inherently expressive acts protected by the First Amendment -- requiring the producer to reinstate Moore would compel the company to express a message it rejected. Retaliation claims survived because firing someone for complaining to HR is not a First Amendment-protected creative decision.

---

## Facts

Hadestown is a Broadway musical depicting Orpheus and Eurydice set in a post-apocalyptic industrial backdrop. It features a "Workers Chorus" -- an ensemble that appears throughout the show. On the evening of October 7, 2021, Plaintiff Camille Moore, a Black woman, was performing in the Workers Chorus alongside other Black performers. On November 23, 2021, Dale Neumann, a Hadestown executive, sent an email to the entire cast apologizing because an all-Black Workers Chorus had conveyed a "white savior story" in recent performances. Neumann stated that Hadestown's executives did not intend to convey this message and that he and director Rachel Chavkin needed to consider "all future combinations of actors on stage" to avoid the unintended narrative. Chavkin subsequently indicated she hired a white woman to replace Moore to avoid an entirely Black Workers Chorus.

Moore alleged race discrimination under federal and New York City law, as well as retaliation claims based on her complaint to human resources. Defendant moved to dismiss under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6).

---

## Procedural History

Moore filed suit in the Southern District of New York (No. 1:23-cv-04837). Defendant moved to dismiss all claims on First Amendment grounds, arguing that its casting decisions are expressive acts protected from government compulsion. District Judge Liman granted the motion to dismiss as to most discrimination claims and denied it as to the retaliation claims.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Court:** District Judge Lewis J. Liman (S.D.N.Y.) -- unanimous opinion (no dissent; single-judge district court)

## Holding

A live theatrical production company's casting decisions are inherently expressive acts protected by the First Amendment when those decisions are motivated by the producer's intent to control the artistic message of the production. Enforcing anti-discrimination claims that would penalize or compel reversal of such casting decisions constitutes unconstitutional compelled speech under the First Amendment. Hurley v. Irish-American Gay, Lesbian and Bisexual Group of Boston (1995) and Rumsfeld v. Forum for Academic and Institutional Rights (2006) control. The First Amendment defense does not apply to retaliation claims where the alleged retaliatory firing has no connection to the company's creative vision.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion (Liman, J.)

Judge Liman's analysis begins with a structural premise: live theater is protected First Amendment expression (citing Schad v. Borough of Mount Ephraim (1981) and Southeastern Promotions v. Conrad (1975)). From that premise, the court holds that casting decisions -- employment decisions about who performs which roles -- are "inherently expressive" because they directly determine the message the production conveys. The Neumann emails prove this point from the defendants' own words: Hadestown's executives understood that "certain arrangements of actors on stage" had caused performances to convey the unintended "white savior story," and they made casting changes specifically to control that expressive content.

Under Hurley, the First Amendment bars the government from compelling a parade organizer to include a message it does not wish to express. The court extends this principle: just as the parade organizer in Hurley could exclude a group whose message clashed with its expressive choices, Hadestown could exclude cast compositions that conveyed an unintended artistic narrative. Enforcing Moore's discrimination claims would require Hadestown to "accommodate" a cast composition that expressed a message it had affirmatively rejected -- unconstitutional compelled expression under Rumsfeld.

The court rejects Moore's argument that the First Amendment defense is time-limited to initial casting. Producers of live theater may need to make ongoing casting adjustments as the performance of the work evolves. The First Amendment's protection is co-extensive with the creative decision-making period; it did not expire once the original cast was assembled.

**The retaliation claims survive.** Moore also alleged she was fired in retaliation for complaining to human resources about discrimination. The court finds no connection between that alleged retaliatory firing and Hadestown's artistic decisions. Firing an employee for lodging an HR complaint is not a First Amendment-protected creative act -- it is garden-variety employment retaliation. The First Amendment defense requires the adverse employment action to be tied to the company's creative vision; retaliation for HR complaints lacks that nexus. The retaliation claims proceed.
