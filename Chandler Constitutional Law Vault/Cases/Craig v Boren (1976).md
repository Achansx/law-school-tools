# Craig v Boren (1976)

## Memory Jogger

> The landmark case in which the Supreme Court formally established intermediate scrutiny for sex-based classifications under the Equal Protection Clause, holding that gender distinctions must serve important governmental objectives and be substantially related to their achievement.

---

## Facts

Oklahoma law prohibited the sale of 3.2% beer to males under 21 and to females under 18 -- a three-year sex-based disparity in the drinking age. Oklahoma defended the differential on traffic safety grounds: statistical evidence showed that 0.18% of females aged 18-20 were arrested for drunk driving while 2% of males in the same age group were arrested -- a ratio of about 10-to-1. Craig, a male between 18 and 21, could not legally purchase 3.2% beer while females his age could. He challenged the statute together with Carolyn Whitener, a licensed vendor of beer who would have faced criminal penalties for selling to Craig. The case presented two preliminary issues alongside the merits: (1) whether Craig had standing (he reached 21 before the case was decided, raising mootness); (2) whether Whitener, as a vendor, had third-party standing to assert the constitutional rights of her male customers who were denied equal access to the market.

---

## Procedural History

Craig filed suit in federal district court challenging the Oklahoma statute under the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. The district court upheld the statute, accepting Oklahoma's statistical evidence as a rational basis for the sex-based classification. The Supreme Court noted probable jurisdiction and reversed, 7-2.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority:** Brennan (joined by White, Marshall, Powell, Stevens, and Stewart)
- **Concurrence (Powell):** Wrote separately to note the case did not require adoption of strict scrutiny for sex
- **Concurrence (Stevens):** Argued for a single sliding-scale standard rather than multiple tiers
- **Concurrence (Stewart):** Agreed the statute was irrational
- **Concurrence (Blackmun):** Joined judgment; agreed on intermediate scrutiny
- **Dissent (Burger):** Would uphold the statute under rational basis; criticized the majority for departing from traditional deference to legislative judgments
- **Dissent (Rehnquist):** Argued rational basis was the correct standard; accepted Oklahoma's statistical evidence; objected to the majority's approach to third-party standing

---

## Holding

To withstand constitutional challenge under the Equal Protection Clause, classifications by gender must serve important governmental objectives and must be substantially related to the achievement of those objectives. Oklahoma's sex-differentiated drinking-age law failed this test: while traffic safety is an important objective, the statistical correlation between sex and drunk driving was too weak and imprecise to establish that a three-year sex-based disparity in drinking age was substantially related to traffic safety. The law was struck down. Additionally, Whitener had third-party standing to raise her customers' equal protection claims because enforcement of the restriction against her indirectly violated the constitutional rights of male purchasers.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Justice Brennan's majority opinion formally announced what courts had been building toward since Reed v Reed (1971) and Frontiero v Richardson (1973): a distinct intermediate tier of constitutional scrutiny for sex-based classifications. The test -- important objectives / substantially related -- sits between rational basis (conceivable legitimate purpose / rationally related) and strict scrutiny (compelling interest / narrowly tailored). The majority explicitly declined to adopt the Frontiero plurality's strict-scrutiny approach; instead it settled on a middle standard that would prove durable.

Applying the test, the majority examined Oklahoma's statistical evidence closely and found it unpersuasive. The drunk-driving arrest data showed that 2% of males aged 18-20 were arrested for drunk driving versus 0.18% of females -- a meaningful disparity, but one that said nothing about 98% of males and 99.82% of females in that age group. A sex-based classification that accurately describes only a tiny fraction of its targets cannot be said to be substantially related to any objective. The majority rejected the argument that statistical overinclusiveness could justify sex stereotyping: "proving broad sociological propositions by statistics is a dubious business, and one that inevitably is in tension with the normative philosophy that underlies the Equal Protection Clause."

On standing, the majority held that Whitener could raise her customers' claims under a long-recognized exception permitting vendors and analogous parties to resist restrictions on their operations by asserting third-party rights. This resolved the concern that Craig himself might lack standing after turning 21.

The choice of "important / substantially related" over "compelling / narrowly tailored" was both pragmatic and doctrinal. Justice Brennan had pushed for strict scrutiny of sex classifications in Frontiero v Richardson (1973), but he had only been able to muster a plurality. Justice Powell, who had declined to join the Frontiero plurality, became the critical fifth vote in any sex-classification case for the rest of the decade. A strict-scrutiny rule would have lost Powell. A rational-basis-with-bite rule, which the Court had arguably applied in Reed v Reed (1971), would have left the doctrine incoherent. The intermediate formulation sat precisely where a majority could coalesce: strong enough to strike overinclusive sex stereotypes, weak enough that Justices reluctant to treat sex like race would sign on. That diplomatic positioning is a large part of why Craig's rule proved durable.

On the statistical question, the majority did not deny that the drunk-driving numbers showed a real sex-based disparity. It denied that the disparity justified the state's chosen means. The 2% versus 0.18% arrest-rate comparison left more than 98% of young men and 99.82% of young women entirely innocent of the conduct the state cited to ban them from beer. Even more tellingly, the statute banned sale rather than consumption, meaning its effect was cosmetic with respect to actual drunk-driving behavior, because young men could still drink beer they purchased from female intermediaries or obtained at home. The fit between sex and state purpose was loose at both ends.

The Twenty-first Amendment angle is often overlooked. Oklahoma argued that the Amendment, which repealed Prohibition and gave states broad power to regulate alcohol, relaxed the ordinary equal protection scrutiny applicable to alcohol-related sex classifications. The Court rejected that argument. The Twenty-first Amendment shields state alcohol regulation from certain commerce-clause challenges, but it does not confer on states a power to violate other constitutional guarantees, including equal protection. This is a rare passage in which the Court had to reconcile two separate constitutional provisions in a sex-equality case, and it emphatically held that the Fourteenth Amendment governs.

The standing issue and the merits are more intertwined than they appear. If Whitener had been denied third-party standing, Oklahoma's sex-classified alcohol rule would likely have survived because Craig himself had turned 21 during the appeal and had no personal stake left. By permitting Whitener, as vendor, to press her male customers' equal-protection claims, the Court preserved a forum for sex-discrimination challenges to consumer-access statutes that might otherwise escape review through simple delay. The standing rule and the substantive rule together amount to a judicial commitment to police sex classifications in the market for ordinary goods.

### Concurrence (Powell)

Justice Powell wrote separately to underscore that the Court was deciding no more than it needed to. He agreed the Oklahoma statute failed equal protection, but he wrote to register his view that the case did not require the Court to choose whether sex ought to be reviewed under strict scrutiny. That posture fit Powell's general approach: he was skeptical of categorical moves that would bind future cases on factual records the Court had not yet seen. His willingness to sign on to intermediate scrutiny for sex, while declining to foreclose the suspect-class question, supplied exactly the kind of narrow consensus Brennan needed to hold a majority together.

Powell's concurrence mattered for a reason that only became clear later. Because the doctrinal peg of Craig was intermediate scrutiny and not strict scrutiny, and because Powell's vote was needed to assemble the peg, the Court retained flexibility to describe the test in different ways in later cases. By the time of United States v Virginia (1996), Justice Ginsburg's majority opinion reformulated the standard to require an "exceedingly persuasive justification," which some readers saw as a tightening of Craig toward de facto strict scrutiny. That trajectory would not have been available if Craig had fixed a rigid formula in 1976. Powell's deliberate understatement preserved room for the doctrine to evolve.

### Dissent (Rehnquist)

Justice Rehnquist's dissent is a full-throated defense of the rational basis floor for equal protection review of classifications outside the suspect-class canon. In his view, the Equal Protection Clause protected classifications the Court had flagged as suspect (race, alienage, national origin) under strict scrutiny, and everything else under a deferential rational basis test. Inserting a middle tier for sex, he argued, was a species of judicial policymaking. It gave judges a standard so "diaphanous and elastic" that they could strike down any classification they disliked on an intuition labeled as "important governmental objectives."

On the evidence, Rehnquist accepted Oklahoma's data as sufficient under rational basis. If 2% of males aged 18-20 were arrested for drunk driving compared to 0.18% of females, that was enough for a legislature pursuing traffic safety to draw the line where it had. Rational basis does not require empirical tightness; it requires only that the legislature not be irrational, and Rehnquist thought the Oklahoma legislature plainly was not.

His objection to the standing analysis was separate and sharper. Rehnquist viewed the majority's grant of third-party standing to Whitener as a doctrinal stretch designed to preserve jurisdiction once Craig's personal claim mooted out. He saw the standing move and the merits move as complementary: both, in his telling, reflected the majority's determination to reach a question that traditional doctrine should have left outside the courthouse.

The Craig dissent is an early statement of the position Rehnquist would continue to hold about sex classifications. By the time of United States v Virginia (1996), Rehnquist concurred in judgment but declined to endorse Ginsburg's "exceedingly persuasive justification" formulation precisely because it echoed Craig's intermediate-tier drift toward strict scrutiny. The continuity of his position across twenty years illustrates how Craig's formal establishment of intermediate scrutiny never fully displaced a dissenting thread in the Court about whether that tier should exist at all.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

*Sex-Based Insurance Rate Classification.* A state insurance regulator permits life insurers to charge men 30% higher premiums than women based on actuarial tables showing men die earlier on average. The state defends the differential on the important objective of accurate risk pricing. Under Craig, this would likely fail. The statistical correlation is real, but most men in any given cohort do not die earlier than the average woman, and using sex as a proxy when more granular underwriting data (smoking, exercise, occupational hazard) is available would fail the "substantially related" requirement. The fit is loose and the state has better tools.

*State-Licensed Professional Certification Exemption.* A state professional licensing board provides that female applicants for a real-estate license are exempt from a three-hour in-person testing requirement because of a statistical finding that female applicants pass written exams at a higher rate. The state cites administrative efficiency. Under Craig, this fails. The correlation does not justify treating all male applicants as the subset that needed the longer test, and sex is not substantially related to testing integrity when individualized alternatives are available.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

*Pregnancy-Related Medical Leave.* A state statute grants paid medical leave to employees who have given birth, with a separate leave program available to fathers and non-birthing parents for bonding purposes. Women-only medical leave survives Craig because pregnancy and childbirth are biological conditions that affect only birthing parents, and the state's objective of permitting recovery from childbirth is substantially related to a classification that tracks the actual condition. The line between biological sex and social role is the line the Court has repeatedly treated as marking legitimate sex-specific classifications.

*Sex-Specific Military Conscription for Combat Roles.* A federal statute registers only males for the draft under a combat-exclusion framework. Even after Craig, this remained constitutional under Rostker v Goldberg (1981), because Congress's military-readiness objective was important and the sex-based registration rule was substantially related to a genuine combat-readiness context in which women were formally excluded. Whether that conclusion survives if the combat exclusion is formally lifted is a different question, but within Craig's framework as applied on its own terms, the selective-service classification was upheld.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

*Sex-Differentiated Age of Majority for Financial Autonomy.* A state statute sets the age of financial majority at 18 for men and 16 for women, citing statistical data that women of 16 show maturity metrics comparable to men of 18 (the statistical correlation is about 15-to-1 in the favorable direction). The state's important objective is ensuring parties to contracts are mature enough to understand them. Under Craig, this is close. The correlation is stronger than Oklahoma's, but the classification still treats every 16-year-old woman as presumptively mature and every 16-year-old man as presumptively not, without individualized inquiry. Post-Craig courts would likely strike it down, but a variant with individualized testing and a sex-based presumption rather than a sex-based bar might survive. The hypo tests exactly the boundary between statistical trends and overbreadth that Craig left blurry.

## Critique

*Progressive feminist critique.* From the perspective of feminist legal theorists like Catharine MacKinnon and Reva Siegel, intermediate scrutiny is a second-class tier for a second-class equality claim. The test invites courts to accept a government's self-declared "important" objective without asking hard questions about whether the objective rests on sex stereotypes or on subordination of women. The doctrinal result is predictable: laws protecting women from overt discrimination survive easily, while laws that entrench gender roles, pregnancy exclusions from insurance, or differential treatment of mothers and fathers are more difficult to challenge. Intermediate scrutiny has also proved vulnerable to claims that sex-based preferences for women constitute "reverse discrimination" and must be justified at the same tier, a parity that race doctrine (where strict scrutiny applies uniformly only after Adarand) does not require in the same form.

*Textualist / originalist critique.* From a textualist or originalist vantage, Craig invents a tier of review with no textual basis. The Equal Protection Clause does not distinguish between "compelling," "important," or "legitimate" objectives. It requires equal protection of the laws. The tiered framework was a twentieth-century judicial construct that Justice Rehnquist rightly described as "diaphanous and elastic." On this view, the Court should apply rational basis to all classifications outside those with clear historical precedent for heightened review (principally race, because of the specific context of the Fourteenth Amendment's ratification). The complaint is not necessarily that Oklahoma's law was good policy, but that the Court's intervention to strike it on constitutional grounds was outside the judicial role.

*Pragmatic critique of doctrinal elasticity.* Intermediate scrutiny has produced an uneven body of case law. In some decisions, the Court has demanded almost strict-scrutiny-grade justifications (United States v Virginia's "exceedingly persuasive justification"). In others, sex-based rules have survived on thin reasoning (Michael M v Superior Court's sex-differentiated statutory rape law). The inconsistency is a function of the elasticity that made the tier politically achievable in 1976. "Important" is in the eye of the beholder, and "substantially related" is in the judgment of the reviewing court. For practitioners, the result is that Craig provides no reliable rule of decision; for theorists, it is evidence that the tier system is a doctrinal fig leaf over ad hoc judicial balancing.

## Key Points

- Formal establishment of intermediate scrutiny for sex-based classifications: important governmental objectives, substantially related to their achievement. The first case to name the middle tier that Reed and Frontiero had gestured toward.
- Doctrinal positioning was strategic. Brennan had only mustered a plurality for strict scrutiny in Frontiero v Richardson (1973); Powell's vote was the ceiling. Intermediate scrutiny was the tier that could hold a seven-Justice majority together.
- Powell's narrow concurrence preserved flexibility for the doctrine to evolve. United States v Virginia (1996) later reformulated the test as requiring an "exceedingly persuasive justification" — a tightening Craig's elasticity allowed.
- Statistical correlations do not satisfy "substantially related" when they describe only a small fraction of the regulated class. Oklahoma's 2% vs 0.18% drunk-driving arrest disparity left more than 98% of young men and 99.82% of young women innocent of the conduct the state cited.
- Third-party standing: vendors (Whitener) can assert the equal-protection claims of their customers. The standing move preserved jurisdiction once Craig himself turned 21 and his personal stake mooted.
- Twenty-first Amendment does not immunize state alcohol regulation from Fourteenth Amendment scrutiny. The Amendment's commerce-clause shield does not confer on states the power to violate equal protection.
- Rehnquist's dissent is the early statement of the position he would hold throughout his tenure: the intermediate tier is "diaphanous and elastic," outside the suspect-class canon rational basis should apply, and intermediate scrutiny is judicial policymaking.
- Treatment: Neutral. Craig is the pivot case in the sex-classification arc; Hogan, J.E.B., and VMI all build on its test.
