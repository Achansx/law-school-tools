# Hamdi v Rumsfeld (2004)

## Memory Jogger

> A U.S. citizen held by the military as an enemy combatant is entitled to notice of the factual basis for his detention and a fair opportunity to rebut the government's evidence before a neutral decisionmaker, even in wartime.

---

## Facts

Yaser Esam Hamdi, an American citizen born in Louisiana and raised in Saudi Arabia, was captured in late 2001 in Afghanistan by the Northern Alliance and turned over to United States forces. The government alleged he had affiliated with a Taliban military unit, received weapons training, and was carrying a Kalashnikov rifle when captured. Hamdi was transferred to Guantanamo Bay, and after his American citizenship was confirmed in 2002 he was moved to a naval brig in Norfolk, Virginia, and later to one in Charleston, South Carolina.

The government held Hamdi incommunicado and without criminal charge as an "enemy combatant," relying on the Authorization for Use of Military Force (AUMF) that Congress passed on September 18, 2001, and on the President's Article II authority as Commander in Chief. The government filed a single affidavit, from Department of Defense adviser Michael Mobbs, purporting to establish Hamdi's enemy-combatant status based on summaries of intelligence reports. Hamdi's father filed a habeas petition as next friend, arguing that Hamdi's detention violated due process and the constitutional prohibition on detention of citizens absent a criminal charge or an Act of Congress.

---

## Procedural History

The District Court for the Eastern District of Virginia appointed a federal public defender to represent Hamdi and ordered unmonitored access to counsel. The Fourth Circuit reversed, holding that the government's Mobbs declaration supplied a sufficient factual basis for the detention and that further judicial inquiry would intrude on executive war powers.

The Supreme Court granted certiorari to consider the scope of executive detention authority and the process constitutionally due. A fractured Court reversed and remanded.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Plurality:** O'Connor (joined by Rehnquist, C.J., Kennedy, Breyer) — 4-justice plurality
- **Concurrence (in judgment):** Souter (joined by Ginsburg) — providing fifth and sixth votes for reversal, though disagreeing on AUMF authorization
- **Dissent:** Scalia (joined by Stevens); Thomas

---

## Holding

The AUMF supplies congressional authorization for the detention of individuals who were part of or supporting Taliban forces engaged in armed conflict with the United States, for the duration of the relevant hostilities, which satisfies the statutory requirement of 18 U.S.C. § 4001(a). But the Due Process Clause requires that a citizen detained as an enemy combatant on U.S. soil receive notice of the factual basis for his classification and a fair opportunity to rebut the government's factual assertions before a neutral decisionmaker. The "some evidence" standard adopted by the Fourth Circuit is constitutionally inadequate when the detainee has had no prior adversarial proceeding.

---

## Analysis

### Plurality Opinion (O'Connor)

Justice O'Connor walked between two positions. On the statutory question she accepted, assuming without deciding that Section 4001(a)'s non-detention rule applied, that the AUMF provided the required congressional authorization for detention of enemy combatants, because detention of captured fighters is "a fundamental incident of waging war." She read the AUMF narrowly, limited to the ongoing conflict in Afghanistan and to the duration of active hostilities there, and warned that this understanding could "unravel" if the conflict's practical circumstances diverged from traditional law-of-war premises. On the constitutional question she applied the Mathews v Eldridge balancing framework: the private interest in personal liberty is profound; the risk of erroneous deprivation through executive affidavits alone is substantial; and while the government's interest in efficient wartime detention is weighty, it does not extinguish the citizen's right to challenge the factual basis for his classification. She specifically endorsed procedural adaptations, including the use of hearsay and a rebuttable presumption in favor of the government's evidence once a credible case is made. Citing Youngstown, O'Connor memorably observed that "a state of war is not a blank check for the President when it comes to the rights of the Nation's citizens."

### Concurrence in the Judgment (Souter)

Justice Souter, joined by Justice Ginsburg, would have held that the AUMF is not a "clear statement" of congressional authority to detain a U.S. citizen and therefore cannot satisfy 18 U.S.C. § 4001(a)'s requirement that citizens be detained only "pursuant to an Act of Congress." Souter read § 4001(a) against the legislative history of Congress's 1971 repeal of the Emergency Detention Act, enacted in response to the Korematsu internment, and concluded that Congress in 1971 meant to force a heightened clear-statement rule on any future attempt to detain citizens. The AUMF's generic authorization to use "necessary and appropriate force" does not clear that bar. Souter also pressed a second, narrower point: the Government had not shown that its treatment of Hamdi conformed to the customary laws of war (including the Geneva Convention) that would normally supply the substantive detention authority the Government invoked. Because five votes for reversal were needed and he and Ginsburg agreed with the plurality on the procedural-due-process holding, Souter concurred in the judgment, while making clear he would have reversed on statutory grounds alone and not reached the constitutional question.

### Dissent (Scalia, joined by Stevens)

Justice Scalia's Suspension Clause dissent is the opinion's most provocative. He argued that the Constitution offers the Executive exactly two options for a U.S. citizen accused of waging war against the United States: (1) prosecution in an Article III court for treason (with the Article III § 3 two-witness requirement) or another federal crime, or (2) detention under a congressionally-enacted suspension of the writ of habeas corpus pursuant to Article I § 9 cl. 2. "Absent suspension," he wrote, "the Executive's assertion of military exigency has not been thought sufficient to permit detention without charge." Because Congress had not suspended the writ, and because no one argued the AUMF was itself a suspension, Hamdi's detention was unlawful, and the correct remedy was release (not the plurality's procedural remand to a neutral decisionmaker). Scalia treated the plurality's Mathews v Eldridge balancing as a category error: the Due Process Clause does not authorize ad hoc judicial cost-benefit trade-offs against the ancient common-law and constitutional requirement of criminal process or suspension. His opinion is studded with Founding-era and Civil War history (Hamilton, Blackstone, Lincoln's July 4, 1861 message to Congress) mobilized to show that executive detention of citizens without charge has always required formal suspension.

### Dissent (Thomas)

Justice Thomas wrote alone and in direct opposition to both the plurality and Scalia. On his view, the Federal Government's war powers are committed to the political branches and judicial second-guessing of an Executive determination that a particular person is an enemy combatant exceeds the Judiciary's institutional competence. Thomas rejected the plurality's use of Mathews balancing in the national-security context, arguing that "the Federal Government's war powers can [not] be balanced away by this Court." His functional test was that when the Executive acts pursuant to statutory and constitutional authority, "due process requires nothing more than a good-faith executive determination" that detention is necessary for public safety, "even if he is mistaken." Thomas was the only Justice who would have affirmed the Fourth Circuit outright; eight of nine Justices rejected the "some evidence" standard as constitutionally inadequate.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

1. **Hypo:** A U.S. citizen is captured in eastern Afghanistan in 2005, carrying an AK-47 alongside a Taliban unit, and held in a naval brig in Charleston without criminal charge. The Government's entire factual return is a single two-page declaration by a DoD adviser summarizing intelligence reports. **Why:** Facts are on all fours with Hamdi. The AUMF authorizes the detention under O'Connor's reading; 18 U.S.C. § 4001(a) is satisfied. But the "some evidence" affidavit-only process fails the plurality's Mathews balancing: the citizen is entitled to notice of the factual basis and a chance to rebut before a neutral decisionmaker. Remand, as in Hamdi.
2. **Hypo:** A U.S. citizen is detained at a CBP facility after returning from Syria, where the Government alleges (in an intelligence summary) that he trained with ISIS. Congress has by this point passed a 2015 AUMF-style authorization covering ISIS. **Why:** A 2015 ISIS-AUMF plausibly triggers Hamdi's statutory analysis (though O'Connor's "unravel" caveat could cut the other way if the conflict looks stateless). Even if detention is authorized, the bare-affidavit procedure fails: notice, counsel, and opportunity to rebut before a neutral decisionmaker are required. Hypo likely comes out the same way — remand for adequate process.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

3. **Hypo:** A non-citizen Yemeni national is captured in Pakistan and detained at Guantanamo as an enemy combatant, held on the same "some evidence" affidavit. **Why:** Hamdi's procedural-due-process holding rests on the Fifth Amendment rights of U.S. citizens and does not directly govern Guantanamo non-citizens. The non-citizen's remedy arrives four years later in Boumediene v Bush (2008) via habeas and the Suspension Clause, not via Hamdi. Pre-Boumediene, the bare-affidavit procedure would not have been struck down under Hamdi itself.
4. **Hypo:** A U.S. citizen is detained during a declared war under a formal congressional declaration (not an AUMF) where the Government establishes compliance with Geneva Convention standards, treats the detainee as a traditional POW, and tries him before a duly constituted military commission applying law-of-war rules. **Why:** Once the Government demonstrates law-of-war compliance, Souter's statutory objection dissolves; the commission satisfies the plurality's "neutral decisionmaker" requirement; and the formal declaration clearly authorizes detention under § 4001(a). The constitutional infirmity in Hamdi arose from the ad hoc "some evidence" procedure, not from POW detention as such. Different outcome, no remand required.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

5. **Hypo:** A U.S. citizen is detained by U.S. forces in 2030 in a counter-terrorism operation against a non-state actor in a country with which the United States has no prior AUMF coverage, and the only congressional authority the Government invokes is a general appropriations rider funding "counter-terrorism operations worldwide." **Why:** O'Connor's "unravel" caveat about non-traditional conflicts is squarely triggered — detention in this setting bears little resemblance to the Afghan-theater law-of-war detention she approved. Souter's clear-statement approach would clearly prohibit this detention; Scalia's Suspension Clause approach would require criminal charge or release; Thomas's view would uphold the detention. The plurality's framework itself is ambiguous: is an appropriations rider a clear-enough AUMF? Does the absence of traditional hostilities-end exhaust the detention authority? A future Court would genuinely struggle.

## Critique

**Progressive / civil-liberties critique.** Scholars such as David Cole and Stephen Vladeck have argued that O'Connor's plurality gives the due-process floor a deceptively hospitable formulation while setting it dangerously low. Once the Government can clear its initial burden with a single Mobbs-style declaration, the Due Process Clause permits presumption-shifting, hearsay, and quasi-military adjudication. The Combatant Status Review Tribunals that the Department of Defense fielded in Hamdi's wake arguably occupied that floor rather than elevating above it, and the result was to normalize an administrative-detention regime for citizens without the discipline that an Article III criminal proceeding would impose. On this view Scalia's dissent is the more protective constitutional reading because it refuses any trade-off between liberty and military convenience short of formal suspension.

**Originalist / Suspension-Clause critique.** The force of Scalia's dissent lies in its textual and historical fidelity. Article I § 9 cl. 2 makes suspension of habeas corpus the sole constitutional mechanism for detaining citizens without criminal charge, and Article III § 3 cl. 1 limits treason prosecutions in ways that would not apply to the AUMF. Critics of the plurality (and of Thomas's dissent) argue that reading the AUMF as ambient authority to detain citizens reduces the Suspension Clause to a dead letter, because the Executive could always obtain "necessary and appropriate force" language in an AUMF without Congress ever having to consciously suspend the writ. Stephen Griffin, Amanda Tyler, and others have developed the point that the Suspension Clause is the original, carefully-drafted compromise balancing liberty against wartime necessity, and the plurality's Mathews balancing reintroduces the very ad-hoc judgment the Clause was meant to foreclose.

**Methodological critique.** Scholars have also pressed a methodological objection to the plurality's import of Mathews v Eldridge from the administrative-benefits context into the criminal-adjacent context of wartime detention. Mathews was designed to evaluate procedural cost-benefit trade-offs in Social Security disability termination; the private interest there is property, the error costs are modest, and the adversarial process is abundant. Hamdi inverts every one of those assumptions: the interest is bodily liberty, the error costs are catastrophic and irreversible, and the Government's asserted need for secrecy forecloses ordinary adversarial process. Treating the two as structurally analogous has been called "category-traveling" — a formal test optimized for one class of problems applied to another it was never meant for.

**Thomas's institutional-competence critique and its limits.** Thomas's approach — courts lack the expertise to second-guess enemy-combatant designations — has an institutional pedigree (political-question doctrine, war-powers deference), but eight Justices rejected it as applied. The force of the rejection is that if Thomas's view were correct, citizenship itself ceases to mean anything enforceable against a wartime Executive, and the Court would have nothing left to say about Korematsu. The critique of Thomas is not that deference is wrong but that his version of it reduces Article III review to a rubber stamp for the very designation at issue.

## Key Points

- First Supreme Court decision squarely addressing the constitutionality of citizen detention under the post-9/11 AUMF.
- Vote is fractured: plurality of four, concurrence in judgment of two, three dissenters; eight justices reject the Fourth Circuit's "some evidence" approach.
- Applies Mathews v Eldridge balancing to wartime detention, a methodological move that remains contested.
- Paired with Rasul v Bush (2004) and later Boumediene v Bush (2008) as the foundational post-9/11 detention trilogy.
- O'Connor's AUMF reading is narrow; later litigation (Al-Marri, Hedges) tests its limits outside the Afghan theater.
