# Learning Resources Inc v Trump (2026)

## Memory Jogger

> IEEPA Does Not Authorize Tariffs. The Trump administration imposed sweeping tariffs on imports under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA), which authorizes the President to "regulate" imports in a national emergency. The Court of International Trade held that "regulate" does not mean "tax" -- imposing tariffs is a form of taxation that requires clear congressional authorization. The major questions doctrine reinforces this: a decision of this economic and political magnitude requires Congress to speak clearly. The court also held it has exclusive jurisdiction over tariff challenges.

---

## Facts

- President Trump invoked IEEPA, 50 U.S.C. §1701 et seq., to declare a national emergency and impose tariffs on a wide range of imported goods
- IEEPA grants the President power to "regulate" imports in response to a declared national emergency threatening national security, foreign policy, or economy
- Plaintiff Learning Resources, Inc., a manufacturer of educational toys and materials, challenged the tariffs, arguing IEEPA does not authorize the imposition of tariffs and that doing so violates the major questions doctrine
- The tariffs significantly increased costs for Learning Resources, which imported products from abroad

---

## Procedural History

- Challenge filed in the United States Court of International Trade (CIT), which has exclusive jurisdiction over actions arising out of tariff laws
- CIT: held IEEPA does not authorize tariffs; granted relief to plaintiffs

## Holding

IEEPA does not authorize the President to impose tariffs. The statutory power to "regulate . . . importation" does not encompass the power to impose tariffs because "regulate" -- as ordinarily understood and as confirmed by Black's Law Dictionary -- means to "fix, establish, or control; to adjust by rule, method, or established mode; to direct by rule or restriction; to subject to governing principles or laws." The term "regulate" is not ordinarily thought to include taxation.

The major questions doctrine applies: imposing tariffs on the scale accomplished here is a decision of vast economic and political significance that requires clear statutory authorization. Courts will not permit the executive branch to claim such sweeping authority on the basis of ambiguous or general language in a statute when the claimed authority has no clear precedent in agency practice and when Congress is fully capable of addressing the subject directly.

The Court of International Trade has exclusive jurisdiction over challenges to tariff laws and related executive actions.

## Key Points

- **Statutory holding**: IEEPA's power to "regulate importation" does not encompass power to impose tariffs; "regulate" does not equal "tax"
- **Major questions doctrine**: tariffs of this economic and political magnitude require clear congressional authorization; broad or general statutory language is insufficient
- **No MQD exception for emergency statutes**: the emergency character of IEEPA does not relax the major questions requirement; the doctrine applies with equal force to emergency powers
- **No MQD exception for foreign affairs**: foreign affairs context does not eliminate the need for clear congressional authorization
- **CIT exclusive jurisdiction**: challenges to tariff laws must be brought in the Court of International Trade, not in district courts
- **Significance**: the first major judicial check on the executive's use of IEEPA as a tariff authority; potential to constrain the breadth of emergency economic powers that have expanded significantly in recent years
