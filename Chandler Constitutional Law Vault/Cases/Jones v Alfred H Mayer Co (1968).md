# Jones v Alfred H Mayer Co (1968)

## Memory Jogger

> The Court holds that 42 U.S.C. section 1982 bars all racial discrimination -- private as well as public -- in the sale or rental of property, and that Congress has power under Section 2 of the Thirteenth Amendment to reach such private conduct as a badge and incident of slavery.

---

## Facts

Joseph Lee Jones sought to purchase a home in a federally subsidized development in St. Louis County, Missouri. The Alfred H. Mayer Company refused to sell to him solely because he was Black. No state law authorized or required the refusal; the discrimination was purely private commercial conduct.

Jones brought suit under 42 U.S.C. section 1982, a Reconstruction-era statute providing that all citizens shall have the same right as white citizens to inherit, purchase, lease, sell, hold, and convey real and personal property. The company argued that section 1982 reached only state-sanctioned discrimination (because the Fourteenth Amendment's state-action limit would apply), not private conduct, and that Congress had no constitutional authority to reach private housing discrimination.

The core constitutional question was whether Congress, acting under Section 2 of the Thirteenth Amendment, could prohibit private racial discrimination in the sale of real property.

---

## Procedural History

The district court dismissed Jones's complaint, and the Eighth Circuit affirmed, holding that section 1982 applied only to state-sanctioned discrimination and did not reach purely private conduct. The Supreme Court reversed, holding both that the statute reached private discrimination and that it was a valid exercise of Thirteenth Amendment enforcement power.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority:** Stewart (joined by Warren, Douglas, Brennan, Fortas, Marshall)
- **Concurrence:** Douglas
- **Dissent:** Harlan (joined by White)

---

## Holding

42 U.S.C. section 1982 bars all racial discrimination, private as well as public, in the sale or rental of property. The statute is a valid exercise of Congress's power under Section 2 of the Thirteenth Amendment. Congress has the power under that Amendment rationally to determine what are the badges and incidents of slavery, and the authority to translate that determination into effective legislation reaching private actors.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Justice Stewart's majority made two moves. First, on statutory interpretation, the Court held that section 1982 was enacted under the Thirteenth Amendment's authority in the Civil Rights Act of 1866 and plainly covers private as well as public discrimination. The text draws no state-action limit; it speaks of the rights of citizens without qualification.

Second, on constitutional authority, the majority invoked Section 2 of the Thirteenth Amendment, which authorizes Congress to pass "appropriate legislation" to enforce the Amendment. Unlike the Fourteenth Amendment, the Thirteenth Amendment prohibits slavery itself -- an institution maintained by private actors as well as state law. Therefore, Section 2 reaches private conduct, not only state action. The key question was whether Congress could rationally classify private housing discrimination as a badge or incident of slavery.

The Court held it could. When racial discrimination herds people into ghettos and makes their ability to buy property depend on the color of their skin, that discrimination is a relic of the slave system. The right to purchase and hold property was among the fundamental civil freedoms that slavery denied, and which the Thirteenth Amendment was designed to secure. Harlan's dissent in the Civil Rights Cases had anticipated this reasoning; Stewart effectively adopted it.

## Key Points

- Holds that Section 2 of the Thirteenth Amendment authorizes Congress to legislate against private (not just state-sanctioned) racial discrimination that constitutes a badge or incident of slavery.
- Section 1982 reaches purely private housing discrimination without any state-action predicate.
- Effectively undermines the Thirteenth Amendment holding in the Civil Rights Cases, vindicating Harlan's dissent.
- The "badges and incidents" standard gives Congress broad discretion to identify and prohibit vestiges of slavery.
- Later cases (City of Memphis v Greene) show the limits of the doctrine where the discriminatory impact is indirect or de minimis.
