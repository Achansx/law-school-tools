# Gong Lum v Rice (1927)

## Memory Jogger

> A Chinese-American citizen could be constitutionally classified as "colored" and excluded from white public schools under the separate-but-equal doctrine -- overruled by Brown v. Board of Education (1954).

---

## Facts

Gong Lum was a Chinese merchant residing in the Rosedale Consolidated High School District in Mississippi, a native-born U.S. citizen. His daughter, Martha Lum, also a U.S. citizen (born January 21, 1915), was nine years old and of educable age; neither father nor daughter had any connection to the Chinese consular service or any foreign government. Martha appeared at the Rosedale Consolidated High School at its opening but was notified at noon recess that she would not be allowed to return. The Board of Trustees, acting on instructions from the State Superintendent of Education, had issued an order excluding her from the school solely on the ground that she was of Chinese descent and not a member of the white or Caucasian race.

The petition alleged there was no school for colored children in the Rosedale district that Martha could conveniently attend. The Mississippi Supreme Court found -- and the U.S. Supreme Court accepted -- that a colored school was available to her, though not in the same immediate district; the federal constitutional question was thus framed as whether the State could compel a Chinese-American citizen to attend a school for "colored" children rather than the white school in her district.

---

## Procedural History

Martha Lum, through her next friend (Chew How, a native-born U.S. citizen), petitioned for a writ of mandamus directing school authorities to admit her to the white school. The Mississippi Supreme Court denied relief, holding that Section 207 of the Mississippi Constitution of 1890 -- "Separate schools shall be maintained for children of the white and colored races" -- classified all non-white races as "colored" and that Martha Lum's classification as yellow/Mongolian placed her in the colored category. The U.S. Supreme Court affirmed on certiorari.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority:** Chief Justice Taft (for a unanimous Court; all Justices joined)
- **Concurrence:** None
- **Dissent:** None

---

## Holding

A state does not violate the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment by classifying a child of Chinese ancestry as "colored" and requiring her to attend schools provided for "the brown, yellow and black races" rather than the white school, so long as equal educational facilities are available. The establishment of separate schools for white and colored children is a valid exercise of legislative power, and the legal principle permitting separation of white and Black pupils in public schools applies equally to the separation of white pupils and pupils of the yellow races.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Chief Justice Taft's opinion treats the case as essentially decided by existing precedent. Plessy v. Ferguson (1896) upheld separate-but-equal in railway cars; Cumming v. Richmond County Board of Education (1899) and other decisions had already blessed separate schools for white and Black children. Taft reasons that the question is no different when the excluded student is Chinese rather than Black: the logic of separate-but-equal applies across all non-white racial classifications, and the Constitution leaves education to the states as a matter of state policy. The only question in Taft's framing is whether a Chinese-American citizen is denied equal protection by being classed among the colored races and furnished facilities equal to those offered to all others -- and the answer, on the authority of prior cases assuming the constitutionality of separate schools, is no. The opinion is conspicuously thin on reasoning, treating the outcome as self-evident from precedent rather than independently justifying racial classification in education.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

1. **Hypo (pre-Brown):** A state in 1930 classifies Japanese-American students as "colored" and assigns them to the "colored" public school, which has equivalent facilities but is physically farther from the student's home. **Why:** Under Gong Lum's logic, the separate-but-equal doctrine applies equally to all non-white racial classifications; geographic inconvenience, absent a denial of equal facilities, is not a constitutional issue under the pre-Brown framework.
<!-- ENRICH: Note that this is a historical hypothetical only; post-Brown it would be unconstitutional. -->

2. **Hypo (pre-Brown):** A state requires that Native American children attend a separate school system with facilities equivalent to those of white schools. **Why:** Gong Lum's majority treated all non-white race classifications as interchangeable for purposes of the separate-but-equal doctrine; a Native American student would face the same result as Martha Lum under the pre-1954 framework.
<!-- ENRICH: Note the distinct federal trust relationship with Native American tribes, which complicates the analogy but does not change the basic separate-but-equal analysis as understood in 1927. -->

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

3. **Hypo (post-Brown, any year):** A state requires that Chinese-American children attend a separate public school, even with equal physical facilities. **Why:** Brown v. Board of Education (1954) overruled the separate-but-equal doctrine as applied to public education, holding that separate educational facilities are inherently unequal. Gong Lum is a dead letter after Brown.
<!-- ENRICH: Analyze Brown's explicit rejection of Plessy in the education context and how that necessarily overrules Gong Lum as well. -->

4. **Hypo:** A state enacts a statute classifying multiracial students by their "predominant" race for school assignment purposes, placing a student with one Chinese and one white parent in the "colored" school. **Why:** Even under Gong Lum's own premises, imposing a racial classification on a student without clear membership in either category would strain the doctrine; post-Brown, any racial classification in school assignment is unconstitutional.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

5. **Hypo:** A state in 1927 (before Brown) classifies a child of mixed Chinese and white parentage as "colored" and excludes her from the white school; the child argues she is predominantly Caucasian and contests the classification. **Why:** Gong Lum takes the classification question as settled by Mississippi law, but the equal protection challenge to the classification itself -- as opposed to the general constitutionality of separate schools -- is less clearly answered; a challenge to the accuracy of the state's racial categorization (as opposed to its existence) is genuinely distinct from the question Gong Lum resolved.

## Key Points

- Extends Plessy's separate-but-equal doctrine beyond the Black-white binary to encompass all non-white racial classifications in public education.
- Overruled by Brown v. Board of Education (1954), which held that separate educational facilities are inherently unequal.
- Historically significant as evidence that the separate-but-equal doctrine was applied to Asian-Americans, not just Black Americans.
- Chief Justice Taft's thin, precedent-dependent reasoning reveals the fragility of the doctrinal foundation Brown later dismantled.
- Note that Brown cited Gong Lum without explicitly overruling it by name; it was implicitly overruled as part of Brown's categorical rejection of separate-but-equal in public education.
