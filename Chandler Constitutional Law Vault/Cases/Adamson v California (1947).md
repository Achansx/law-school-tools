# Adamson v California (1947)

## Memory Jogger

> The last major pre-Warren holdout against incorporation, Adamson refused to apply the Fifth Amendment's self-incrimination privilege to the states and sparked the Black-Frankfurter total-incorporation debate that would dominate the 1960s.

---

## Facts

Admiral Dewey Adamson was tried in a California state court for the first-degree murder of a 64-year-old woman, killed during a burglary at her Los Angeles home. Physical evidence included stocking tops found in Adamson's apartment that did not match a stocking fragment found under the victim's body. California law permitted the prosecution and the trial judge to comment on a criminal defendant's failure to testify and allowed the jury to draw an adverse inference from that silence. Adamson had prior felony convictions that would have been disclosed on cross-examination if he took the stand, and he accordingly did not testify in his own defense.

Both the prosecutor and the judge commented extensively on Adamson's silence. The jury convicted Adamson and imposed the death penalty. On appeal Adamson argued that California's comment rule violated the Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination as made applicable to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process or Privileges or Immunities Clauses, and that the comments denied him fundamental fairness.

---

## Procedural History

The California Supreme Court affirmed the conviction and death sentence, upholding the comment rule as consistent with state law and with the federal Constitution as interpreted in Twining v New Jersey (1908). Adamson sought review in the United States Supreme Court.

The Supreme Court affirmed 5-4. Justice Reed wrote for the majority, joined by Chief Justice Vinson and Justices Jackson and Burton. Justice Frankfurter concurred separately to develop the fundamental-fairness framework. Justice Black dissented, joined by Justice Douglas, in an opinion that treated the Fourteenth Amendment as incorporating the entire Bill of Rights. Justice Murphy, joined by Justice Rutledge, dissented separately.

---

## Holding

The Fifth Amendment's privilege against self-incrimination is not incorporated through the Fourteenth Amendment as a restraint on state criminal procedure, and the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause does not draw all the rights of the federal Bill of Rights under its protection. A state rule permitting limited comment on a criminal defendant's failure to explain or deny evidence, without any presumption of guilt, does not violate due process so long as the trial remains fundamentally fair.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Justice Reed grounded the decision in stare decisis and the long line of cases holding that the Privileges or Immunities Clause does not transfer the federal Bill of Rights to the states. Under Twining and the Slaughter-House Cases, protection against self-incrimination is a privilege of state citizenship, not a privilege of national citizenship. He then rejected the alternative theory of total incorporation through the Due Process Clause, citing Palko v Connecticut for the principle that only those rights implicit in the concept of ordered liberty bind the states. Protection against compelled testimony, Reed concluded, is valuable but not essential to an ordered criminal system. California's narrow comment rule permitted inferences from proven facts without creating any presumption of guilt and preserved the defendant's right to remain silent in substance if not in every incident. The due process inquiry asks whether the trial was fundamentally fair; on that standard, California's rule was constitutional.

### Concurrence (Frankfurter)

Frankfurter concurred to defend and systematize the fundamental-fairness framework against Black's total-incorporation attack. His opinion made three moves. First, he treated the question as settled by precedent: Palko v Connecticut (1937) had already announced, and Twining v New Jersey (1908) had held earlier, that the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause does not mechanically carry forward the federal Bill of Rights, but only those rights "implicit in the concept of ordered liberty." For Frankfurter, thirty-nine years of doctrine had built on that reading, and stare decisis was not a consideration to be brushed aside by a constitutional theory "just discovered."

Second, he articulated the methodology affirmatively. Due process, he wrote, asks whether a state practice offends "those canons of decency and fairness which express the notions of justice of English-speaking peoples even toward those charged with the most heinous offenses." The standards are not "authoritatively formulated anywhere as though they were prescriptions in a pharmacopoeia," but neither are judges "wholly at large"; the judgment "must move within the limits of accepted notions of justice and is not to be based upon the idiosyncrasies of a merely personal judgment." The method thus required case-by-case evaluation of the overall fairness of a proceeding rather than mechanical application of enumerated Bill of Rights protections.

Third, he rejected total incorporation on a specifically historical ground: the Fourteenth Amendment's framers, he argued, had not articulated any understanding that Section One would "fasten upon the States procedural arrangements which, in the language of Mr. Justice Cardozo, only those who are 'narrow or provincial' would deem essential to 'a fair and enlightened system of justice.'" Grand-jury indictment, twelve-person juries in petty civil cases, and (implicitly) the self-incrimination rule were not constitutive of fair process; they were one of many available ways a free society could organize its procedure. To read them wholesale into Section One would, in Frankfurter's phrase borrowed from Tocqueville, be "to confound the familiar with the necessary."

The concurrence mattered because it gave the majority a methodologically developed alternative to Black's all-or-nothing frame. Reed's opinion did the work of restating the Twining/Palko rule; Frankfurter's concurrence transformed that rule into a principled judicial posture — restrained, historically grounded, attentive to comparative procedure — that would define due-process adjudication through the mid-1960s. That posture's weakness, which Black's dissent immediately exposed, was that "canons of decency" and "civilized standards" left substantial discretion in the Justices' own hands, and successive applications of the framework produced the ad-hoc "fundamental fairness" doctrine that Justice Black accused of being indistinguishable from the natural-law formulas Twining itself had rejected.

### Dissent (Black)

Black's dissent, joined by Douglas, is the most historically ambitious dissenting opinion in the Bill of Rights-incorporation line. Its core claim has two parts. First, as a matter of methodology, the "canons of decency" and "civilized standards" test gives Justices unbridled discretion to substitute their "own concepts of decency and fundamental justice for the language of the Bill of Rights." Black read this as a return of the very natural-law jurisprudence that Slaughter-House had purported to reject and that had produced Lochner-era economic substantive due process. The specific Bill of Rights guarantees, he argued, were designed precisely to constrain judicial discretion by fixing textual protections; "civilized standards" unfixed them.

Second, and more controversially, Black attached a thirty-one-page historical appendix arguing that the Thirty-Ninth Congress (which drafted the Fourteenth Amendment) had understood Section One — in some combination of the Privileges or Immunities, Due Process, and Equal Protection Clauses — to incorporate the Bill of Rights wholesale against the states. The appendix drew on congressional debates featuring Representative John Bingham's drafting commentary and Senator Jacob Howard's floor statement introducing the amendment in the Senate with explicit reference to the "personal rights" of the first eight amendments. Black treated this as showing original public meaning: readers in 1868 would have understood Section One to carry the Bill of Rights with it, and the Court's rejection of that reading since Hurtado v California (1884) and Twining had been a century-long departure from the Constitution's actual text and history.

Black's remedy followed directly from the diagnosis. He would have preferred "the selective process of the Palko decision applying some of the Bill of Rights to the States" to the Twining rule applying none, but his first choice was "what I believe was the original purpose of the Fourteenth Amendment — to extend to all the people of the nation the complete protection of the Bill of Rights." The dissent thus laid out the total-incorporation theory as a matter of original understanding, not merely as a normative preference for textual specificity.

The dissent's doctrinal significance is that it did not carry the day in 1947 but drove the jurisprudence of the next two decades. The Warren Court never adopted total incorporation as Black had defended it, but the selective-incorporation revolution — Mapp v Ohio (1961), Gideon v Wainwright (1963), Malloy v Hogan (1964, which directly overruled Adamson on the self-incrimination issue), Griffin v California (1965, which overruled Adamson on adverse-inference comments), Duncan v Louisiana (1968) — incorporated enough of the Bill of Rights that by 1969 only a handful of provisions remained unincorporated. Black's ambition shaped the terrain even as his specific historical claim remained controversial.

### Dissent (Murphy)

Murphy's dissent, joined by Rutledge, is a short but doctrinally distinctive opinion. Murphy agreed with Black that "the specific guarantees of the Bill of Rights should be carried over intact into the first section of the Fourteenth Amendment" — total incorporation. But he declined to treat the Bill of Rights as an outer limit on due process. "Occasions may arise where a proceeding falls so far short of conforming to fundamental standards of procedure as to warrant constitutional condemnation in terms of a lack of due process despite the absence of a specific provision in the Bill of Rights." In other words, Murphy would have combined total incorporation with a residual fundamental-fairness reach — what commentators later called "incorporation plus."

The Murphy position is rarely taught as its own category, but doctrinally it prefigured two moves the Court eventually made. First, the modern doctrine's "fundamental rights" substantive-due-process line (Griswold v Connecticut (1965), Roe v Wade (1973) in its original framing, Lawrence v Texas (2003), Obergefell v Hodges (2015)) treats due process as protecting unenumerated rights beyond the Bill of Rights, which is what Murphy foresaw. Second, the Court's approach to incorporation itself became selective-plus-capacious: the Bill of Rights guarantees are carried in, but they do not exhaust what due process protects. Black never agreed with this; he consistently read the Bill of Rights as setting the ceiling as well as the floor. Murphy's concurrence with Black-on-incorporation-only carved out a distinct total-incorporation-plus theory that shaped the later substantive-due-process doctrine even as Black's own narrower position did not.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

1. **1955 state prosecutorial-comment rule.** Nevada permits the prosecutor to remark on a defendant's decision not to testify, though without any formal presumption of guilt, and the state supreme court affirms a conviction where the comment was made. The defendant petitions for certiorari in 1955. Under Adamson's fundamental-fairness framework, the rule looks almost identical to California's in 1947, and the doctrine has not yet begun to move; the petition is likely denied or the conviction affirmed. The Justices in the majority at that moment would continue to treat the Twining rule as controlling: self-incrimination is not fundamental, and comment on silence is not constitutionally condemned so long as the trial was overall fair.

2. **Adverse-inference jury instruction with no shift of burden.** A state judge instructs the jury that it "may, but need not, draw an inference against the defendant from his failure to explain evidence within his peculiar knowledge." The instruction expressly preserves the prosecution's burden of proof beyond a reasonable doubt. Under Adamson, this clears the fundamental-fairness bar: there is no presumption of guilt, the burden has not shifted, and the defendant retained the formal right to remain silent. The California rule Adamson upheld was structurally the same, and the result holds for as long as Adamson is the law.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

3. **Same fact pattern in 1970.** A defendant convicted of murder in California state court in 1969 appeals on the ground that the prosecutor commented on his failure to testify. By 1970 the doctrinal landscape has changed completely. Malloy v Hogan (1964) overruled Adamson's Privileges-or-Immunities and Due Process holdings and incorporated the Fifth Amendment's self-incrimination clause against the states. Griffin v California (1965) then held that comment on silence violates the newly-incorporated privilege. The conviction is reversed. The hypothetical illustrates how narrow Adamson became within eighteen years of its issuance: the specific comment rule it upheld was the first thing Griffin struck down.

4. **State rule that shifts burden of persuasion.** A state statute provides that where a defendant "fails to explain evidence within his exclusive knowledge, the jury shall presume such evidence unfavorable to him, and the defendant must then produce evidence rebutting the presumption." This is not Adamson's rule; it shifts the burden of persuasion, creates a presumption of guilt, and operates as compelled testimony by consequence. Even under Adamson's fundamental-fairness framework this likely fails: Reed's majority carefully distinguished permissible inferences from presumptions-and-burden-shifts, and the latter was not before the Court. Post-Griffin, the rule fails under the Fifth Amendment self-incrimination doctrine directly. Either way, the Adamson result does not travel to a burden-shifting rule.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

5. **1959 state bail scheme tested under fundamental-fairness.** A state denies pretrial bail entirely in capital cases without any provision for individualized determination. The defendant, charged with a non-capital offense for which bail is set at a statutorily-fixed amount far beyond his means, challenges the scheme under the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause and the Eighth Amendment's Excessive Bail Clause. In 1959 the Eighth Amendment has not yet been incorporated (Robinson v California, 1962, will incorporate the Cruel and Unusual Punishments Clause; the Excessive Bail Clause's incorporation status remains unsettled even today). The fundamental-fairness question is genuinely uncertain because the Court in the late 1950s is actively moving rights from "not fundamental" to "fundamental" on a case-by-case basis: Moore v Dempsey (1923) had already indicated that fundamentally unfair state criminal procedures violate due process, and the reach of that line into bail was contestable. A plausible outcome in 1959 is that the Court applies the fundamental-fairness test and finds the bail scheme adequate so long as individualized hearings are formally available, but a litigant with a strong record of arbitrariness might prevail. The hypothetical captures the instability of the Adamson framework in its final decade: the doctrinal machinery was designed to resist mechanical incorporation but lacked an internal principle for when a specific right should nonetheless be carried forward.

## Critique

**Progressive critique.** The standard progressive-legalist indictment of Adamson was made in the contemporaneous and then-rising civil-liberties scholarship (Learned Hand's Holmes Lectures (1958) took a different direction, but Henry Hart, Herbert Wechsler, and Charles Black all registered concerns with the fundamental-fairness framework). The complaint has two parts. First, the framework was indeterminate. "Canons of decency" and "fundamental justice" supplied standards without content, leaving each case to be decided by the Justices' own sense of what decency required. As a result, state criminal procedure varied wildly across jurisdictions, and the Court's interventions were ad hoc and often erratic: a right was "fundamental" when the Court said so and not when it did not, with no principle linking one result to another. Second, the framework produced substantive inequality. A defendant tried in a state that happened to have protective procedural rules got substantive protection that a defendant in a less protective state did not. The Bill of Rights had been written partly to guarantee a uniform floor; the fundamental-fairness test left that floor to each state to set. Black's methodological dissent made this argument in real time, and the Warren Court's selective-incorporation revolution is the practical answer to it: the Court moved, right by right, to the uniform-floor position Black advocated.

**Originalist / historical critique.** Black's historical appendix provoked one of the most substantial scholarly debates in twentieth-century constitutional history. Charles Fairman's "Does the Fourteenth Amendment Incorporate the Bill of Rights?" (2 Stan. L. Rev. 5 (1949)) dismantled Black's reading in detail, arguing that the congressional record did not support the total-incorporation thesis and that post-ratification state practice (state criminal procedure inconsistent with federal Bill of Rights protections continuing with no apparent legal resistance) belied any understanding that the Fourteenth Amendment had fastened the Bill of Rights onto the states. William Crosskey's "Charles Fairman, 'Legislative History,' and the Constitutional Limitations on State Authority" (22 U. Chi. L. Rev. 1 (1954)) pushed back sharply in Black's defense. Later scholarship (Michael Kent Curtis, No State Shall Abridge (1986); Akhil Amar, The Bill of Rights (1998); Kurt Lash, The Fourteenth Amendment and the Privileges and Immunities of American Citizenship (2014)) has tended to rehabilitate parts of the incorporation thesis while conceding that the specific clause through which incorporation runs — Privileges or Immunities, Due Process, or both — is less textually clean than Black's reading implied. The modern near-consensus is that the Reconstruction Congress did intend substantially more constitutional protection against state power than Fairman allowed, but that neither the wholesale mechanical total-incorporation reading nor the strict Slaughter-House contraction of Privileges or Immunities fully captures the historical record. Justice Thomas's opinions in Saenz v Roe (1999) and McDonald v Chicago (2010) have attempted to rebuild the Privileges-or-Immunities path Black sketched, though no majority has joined him.

**Institutional critique.** Frankfurter's response to Black — that total incorporation would "fasten upon the States procedural arrangements" that provincial observers might deem essential to a fair system — has its own modern critics. The concern is not with any particular result Frankfurter reached but with the framework's inability to discipline the Justices: if "canons of decency" control and those canons are not textually fixed, then the concurrence effectively delegates the content of state criminal procedure to whatever the sitting Justices find unacceptable. In practice Frankfurter's own jurisprudence was often more permissive of state prerogatives than Black's, but there is no intrinsic limit in "fundamental fairness" preventing a different composition of Justices from imposing substantially stricter readings via the same discretion. The critique is less ideologically partisan than it looks: originalists and textualists have made it against Frankfurter; progressive formalists have made it against Powell and Kennedy in later fundamental-rights cases. The shared worry is that fundamental-fairness doctrine leaves judges "wholly at large" in exactly the way Frankfurter claimed it did not.

## Key Points

- High-water mark of selective incorporation resisting the Black total-incorporation theory.
- Vote: 5-4. Reed majority, Frankfurter concurrence, Black dissent joined by Douglas, Murphy dissent joined by Rutledge.
- Directly overruled in relevant part by Malloy v Hogan (1964), which incorporated the self-incrimination privilege, and by Griffin v California (1965), which outlawed adverse-inference comments.
- The Black-Frankfurter exchange on total versus selective incorporation frames the debate that drives the 1960s incorporation revolution.
