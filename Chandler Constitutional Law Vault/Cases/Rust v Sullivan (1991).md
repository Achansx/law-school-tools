# Rust v Sullivan (1991)

## Memory Jogger

> The government may selectively fund a program to encourage certain activities without funding alternative approaches; HHS regulations prohibiting Title X grantees from providing abortion counseling do not violate the First Amendment because the government is simply defining the limits of its own program.

---

## Facts

Title X of the Public Health Service Act funds family planning services. The Secretary of Health and Human Services issued "gag rule" regulations prohibiting Title X project staff from: (1) providing abortion counseling or referrals, even in response to patient requests; (2) advocating, promoting, or encouraging abortion as a method of family planning; and (3) engaging in any activities that encourage or promote abortion. The regulations also required Title X grantees to maintain physical and financial separation between Title X projects and any abortion-related activities at the same facility. Several Title X grantees and physicians challenged the regulations as violating both the First Amendment (suppressing speech in the doctor-patient relationship) and the Fifth Amendment's due process guarantee by placing an unconstitutional condition on the receipt of government funds.

---

## Procedural History

The district courts reached conflicting results. The Second Circuit upheld the regulations as a permissible interpretation of Title X and as consistent with the Constitution. Other circuits had invalidated similar provisions. The Supreme Court granted certiorari, upheld the regulations as a reasonable statutory interpretation, and then proceeded to the constitutional questions, rejecting both First and Fifth Amendment challenges.

## Holding

The HHS Title X regulations do not violate the First Amendment. The government does not infringe a constitutional right when it chooses to fund one activity (family planning leading to childbirth) to the exclusion of another (abortion counseling). When the government appropriates public funds to establish a program, it is entitled to define the limits of that program; conditioning funding on compliance with that scope does not impose an unconstitutional condition. The government has no constitutional duty to subsidize abortion counseling merely because abortion itself is constitutionally protected.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion (Rehnquist, C.J.)

Rehnquist's majority rests on the distinction between government prohibition of speech and government failure to subsidize speech. The First Amendment does not require the government to subsidize all viewpoints equally. A government decision to fund childbirth services to the exclusion of abortion services is viewpoint-neutral in the sense that it selects among programs rather than among speakers in an otherwise open forum; the government is not suppressing abortion speech but simply choosing not to fund it. The condition does not prohibit anyone from speaking — Title X grantees and their staff are free to discuss abortion in any non-Title X context; they simply cannot use Title X project funds to do so. The "unconstitutional conditions" doctrine is not triggered because grantees retain all of their rights in non-subsidized activities. The Court draws an analogy to *Maher v. Roe* (1977) and *Harris v. McRae* (1980), which upheld Congress's refusal to fund medically necessary abortions through Medicaid: the government may implement its value judgment that childbirth is preferred over abortion through the allocation of public funds.

## Key Points

- *Rust* establishes the government-speech exception to the viewpoint discrimination rule: when the government funds a program to convey its own message, it may exclude competing viewpoints.
- *Legal Services Corp. v. Velazquez* (2001) limits *Rust*: the government-speech rationale fails when the government is subsidizing private speakers engaged in independent expression (legal advocacy), not delivering a government-scripted message.
- *Rust* is frequently paired with *Velazquez* as the two poles of the government-speech/spending-conditions spectrum in a First Amendment survey course.
- Congress legislatively reversed the "gag rule" after the Clinton administration, but the holding as a constitutional matter remains valid.
