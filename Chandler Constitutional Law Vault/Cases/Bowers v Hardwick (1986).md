# Bowers v Hardwick (1986)

## Memory Jogger

> The Court held 5-4 that the Constitution does not protect a fundamental right to engage in homosexual sodomy, applying the historical-tradition test to reject the claim and sustaining Georgia's sodomy statute on rational basis grounds; overruled seventeen years later by Lawrence v Texas (2003).

---

## Facts

Michael Hardwick was found engaged in consensual sexual activity with another adult man in the bedroom of his own home by a police officer who had entered to serve an unrelated arrest warrant for a minor traffic violation. The officer arrested Hardwick under Georgia's sodomy statute, which criminalized oral and anal sex and applied to both same-sex and opposite-sex conduct. The District Attorney initially declined to present the case to a grand jury but left the charge open. Hardwick, joined by a married heterosexual couple (whose standing was later dismissed by the Eleventh Circuit), brought a declaratory judgment action challenging the statute as unconstitutional as applied to consensual adult homosexual sodomy in the home. Georgia argued the statute was justified by the presumed moral beliefs of the majority of its citizens.

---

## Procedural History

The Northern District of Georgia dismissed the complaint, holding that Stanley v Georgia (1969) -- which had protected the private possession of obscene materials in the home -- did not extend a general right to engage in otherwise illegal conduct at home. The Eleventh Circuit reversed, finding a fundamental right to engage in private consensual sexual conduct, and applied strict scrutiny, which the statute could not survive. The Supreme Court granted certiorari and reversed the Eleventh Circuit by a 5-4 vote, upholding the Georgia statute. The Court expressly overruled Bowers in Lawrence v Texas (2003).

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority (5):** White (author), joined by Burger (C.J.), Powell, Rehnquist, O'Connor
- **Concurrence:** Burger (C.J.) (emphasizing natural law and Judeo-Christian tradition)
- **Concurrence:** Powell (expressing uncertainty; thought the case might be decided on Eighth Amendment grounds but ultimately concurred on the merits)
- **Dissent:** Blackmun (joined by Brennan, Marshall, Stevens) (privacy as a right to be let alone)
- **Dissent:** Stevens (joined by Brennan, Marshall) (prohibition arbitrary if not applied equally to heterosexual conduct)

---

## Holding

The federal Constitution does not confer a fundamental right upon homosexuals to engage in sodomy. Because no fundamental right is at stake, Georgia's criminal sodomy statute survives rational basis review, supported by the state's interest in public morality as embodied in the presumed belief of the electorate that homosexual sodomy is immoral. The majority's prior privacy cases (Griswold, Eisenstadt, Roe, Stanley) do not support extending constitutional protection to this conduct.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion (White)

Justice White framed the question narrowly and tendentiously: whether the Constitution "confers a fundamental right upon homosexuals to engage in sodomy." He then applied the two-part test from Moore v City of East Cleveland and Palko v Connecticut, asking whether the claimed right is either "deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition" or "implicit in the concept of ordered liberty." Surveying the historical record, the majority found that sodomy had been a criminal offense at common law, was forbidden in all 13 original states at the time of the Bill of Rights' ratification, remained illegal in all 50 states until 1961, and was still criminal in 24 states and DC at the time of the decision. Given this unbroken history of condemnation, White concluded that the claimed right was "at best, facetious." The majority then sustained the statute on rational basis grounds, holding that majoritarian moral disapproval is a sufficient rationale for a law even if courts may disagree with that judgment. White also rejected the argument that Stanley v Georgia extended protection to all illegal conduct occurring in the home: Stanley rested on the First Amendment, and conduct otherwise subject to criminal prohibition does not become constitutionally protected merely because it occurs in a private residence.

## Key Points

- Employed the historical-tradition test and framed the claimed right at the most specific level of generality (homosexual sodomy), making it easy to find no tradition of protection
- Rational basis review sustained the statute on public morality grounds -- important precedent for morality as a sufficient rational basis
- Explicitly overruled by [[Cases/Lawrence v Texas (2003)|Lawrence v Texas]] (2003), which reframed the right as consensual adult intimate conduct rather than homosexual sodomy specifically
- Justice Powell later stated publicly that his vote was a mistake
- The level-of-generality problem -- how broadly or narrowly to characterize the claimed right -- is the central methodological issue, later formalized by Scalia in Michael H v Gerald D footnote 6
- Treatment: Negative; overruled by Lawrence; cited 754 times

## Connections

- [[Cases/Lawrence v Texas (2003)|Lawrence v Texas (2003)]] (overruling case): Lawrence expressly overruled Bowers seventeen years later, reframing the same liberty question from "fundamental right to homosexual sodomy" to "autonomy in intimate relationships" and rejecting moral disapproval as a sufficient state interest. The Bowers-Lawrence pairing is the canonical level-of-generality / framing-pivot exhibit in modern Substantive Due Process.
- [[Cases/Romer v Evans (1996)|Romer v Evans (1996)]] (ten-years-later counterweight): Romer strikes a Colorado amendment that targeted gay and lesbian Coloradans as a class, without overruling Bowers. The decade-long coexistence of Bowers (criminal sodomy upheld) and Romer (equal-protection animus limit) creates the internal doctrinal pressure Lawrence resolves by overruling Bowers outright.
- [[Cases/Griswold v Connecticut (1965)|Griswold v Connecticut (1965)]] (precedent recharacterized): Bowers' majority frames Griswold and the privacy line as protecting only the marital bedroom and procreation, refusing to extend the Griswold-Eisenstadt-Roe arc to non-procreative same-sex intimacy; Lawrence later rejects this reading and treats Griswold as the proper "beginning point" for the liberty inquiry.
- [[Cases/Washington v Glucksberg (1997)|Washington v Glucksberg (1997)]] (methodological inheritor): Glucksberg adopts Bowers' "deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition" formulation as the operative test for unenumerated rights, packaged as a two-step careful-description rule; the Bowers-Glucksberg methodological lineage is what Lawrence implicitly displaces and Dobbs later restores.
- [[Cases/Michael H v Gerald D (1989)|Michael H v Gerald D (1989)]] (level-of-generality formalization): Scalia's footnote 6 in Michael H crystallizes the Bowers framing-move into an explicit interpretive rule (define the asserted right at the most specific level of tradition supports), making the level-of-generality choice the central methodological lever for Bowers-style outcomes.
- [[Cases/Moore v City of East Cleveland (1977)|Moore v City of East Cleveland (1977)]] (countervailing pluralism precedent): Moore's plurality protects extended-family living arrangements under a less-history-bound liberty framing; Bowers' majority effectively repudiates Moore's open-textured approach in favor of the most-specific-tradition reading that Lawrence later rejects.
- [[Cases/Obergefell v Hodges (2015)|Obergefell v Hodges (2015)]] (post-Bowers high-water mark): Obergefell completes the autonomy-dignity arc Lawrence began, recognizing same-sex marriage as a fundamental right; the Bowers→Lawrence→Obergefell trajectory is the most-cited example of doctrinal reversal driven by reframing rather than new facts.
