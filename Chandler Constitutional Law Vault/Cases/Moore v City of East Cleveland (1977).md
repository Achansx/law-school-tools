# Moore v City of East Cleveland (1977)

## Memory Jogger

> A 5-4 Court struck down East Cleveland's narrow housing ordinance that prevented a grandmother from living with her two grandsons (who were cousins, not brothers), holding that substantive due process protects the extended family unit -- not merely the nuclear family -- as a fundamental liberty interest rooted in the Nation's history and tradition.

---

## Facts

Inez Moore lived in East Cleveland, Ohio with her son Dale Moore Sr. and two grandsons: Dale Moore Jr. (Dale Sr.'s son) and John Moore Jr. (the son of another of Moore's sons). Under East Cleveland's housing ordinance, the term "family" was defined narrowly to encompass only certain close relatives, and the definition excluded the particular combination of relatives living in Moore's home. Because John Jr. was not a sibling of Dale Jr. but a cousin, Moore's household did not qualify as a "family" under the ordinance. Moore received a notice of violation, failed to comply, and was criminally prosecuted. She was convicted and sentenced to five days in jail and a $25 fine. She challenged the ordinance as a violation of the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment, arguing that it unconstitutionally intruded on her right to live with her grandchildren and to structure her family as she saw fit.

---

## Procedural History

The Municipal Court of East Cleveland convicted Moore for violating the city's single-family housing ordinance. The Ohio Court of Appeals affirmed, rejecting Moore's constitutional claim. The Ohio Supreme Court dismissed her appeal for want of a substantial constitutional question. The U.S. Supreme Court noted probable jurisdiction and reversed by a 5-4 vote, with a plurality holding the ordinance unconstitutional as applied and as construed.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Plurality (4):** Powell (author), joined by Brennan, Marshall, Blackmun
- **Concurrence in judgment:** Brennan (joins plurality; writes separately to address dissent)
- **Concurrence in judgment:** Stevens (relying on property rights rather than substantive due process)
- **Dissent:** Stewart (joined by Rehnquist) (ordinance a legitimate land-use regulation)
- **Dissent:** White (substantive due process too unprincipled to extend beyond nuclear family)
- **Dissent:** Burger (C.J.) (legislative zoning judgments deserve deference)

---

## Holding

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment protects the right of members of an extended family to live together. East Cleveland's housing ordinance, which defines "family" so narrowly as to prohibit a grandmother from living with her two grandsons who are cousins rather than brothers, intrudes on a fundamental liberty interest that is deeply rooted in the Nation's history and tradition. Because the ordinance burdens that fundamental interest without serving compelling governmental interests, it cannot stand. The Constitution's protection of family liberty extends beyond the nuclear family to encompass the broader kinship bonds honored throughout American and Western tradition.

---

## Analysis

### Plurality Opinion (Powell)

Justice Powell's plurality invoked the history-and-tradition methodology while refusing to confine its reach to the nuclear family alone. The plurality acknowledged that the Court must exercise great caution in extending substantive due process protections, given the risk of substituting judicial for democratic preferences. But it held that the Nation's history reveals deep respect for the extended family -- grandparents, aunts, uncles, and cousins raising children together -- particularly in immigrant and minority communities where the extended household was the essential social unit. The plurality read Meyer v Nebraska (1923) and Pierce v Society of Sisters (1925) as establishing a tradition of protecting family autonomy that was not exhausted by the nuclear-family cases. East Cleveland's ordinance, rather than serving significant governmental objectives, merely attempted to standardize its households in ways that history and tradition could not support. The plurality declined to reduce due process liberty to a fixed catalog, insisting instead that courts must look to the depth and breadth of the Nation's traditions.

## Key Points

- Extended the constitutional protection for family life beyond the nuclear family to the extended family unit, drawing on deep historical traditions of grandparent- and cousin-based households
- Plurality opinion (4 votes) joined by concurrence in judgment (Stevens), for a 5-4 result; no majority rationale
- The standard of review is not clearly specified -- the plurality uses heightened scrutiny language but does not explicitly invoke strict scrutiny
- White's dissent is the most pointed methodological critique of SDP liberty, anticipating later objections by Scalia and Thomas
- Moore is a key precedent for both Troxel v Granville (parental rights) and for understanding the limits of Glucksberg's two-part test
