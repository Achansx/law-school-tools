# Jacobson v Massachusetts (1905)

## Memory Jogger

> The state's police power permits compulsory vaccination over individual liberty objections when the measure has a real and substantial relation to public health, establishing the foundational principle that constitutional liberty is regulated liberty, not absolute freedom.

---

## Facts

Henning Jacobson, a Swedish immigrant living in Cambridge, Massachusetts, refused to be vaccinated against smallpox under a municipal board of health order enacted pursuant to a Massachusetts statute authorizing such mandates when necessary to protect public safety. Jacobson objected on the grounds that vaccination was dangerous and unnecessary for him personally, and that the compulsory requirement violated his liberty under the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause. Cambridge was experiencing an increase in smallpox cases at the time the order was issued.

Under the statute, refusing vaccination subjected a person to a five-dollar fine. Jacobson was convicted in Massachusetts state court and appealed on federal constitutional grounds.

---

## Procedural History

Jacobson was convicted of violating the Cambridge vaccination order and fined five dollars. He challenged the conviction in the Superior Court of Suffolk County, Massachusetts, arguing the statute violated the Fourteenth Amendment. The court refused his offer of evidence about vaccination dangers and found against him. The Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court affirmed. The U.S. Supreme Court granted certiorari on the constitutional question.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority:** Harlan (joined by Fuller C.J., Brown, White, McKenna, Holmes, and Day -- 7 Justices)
- **Dissent:** Brewer and Peckham (no written opinion)

---

## Holding

A state's police power extends to reasonable public health regulations, including compulsory vaccination orders. A mandatory vaccination requirement enacted in response to a smallpox outbreak does not violate the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. Individual liberty is not an absolute right free from all restraint; it is liberty regulated by law for the common good. Courts may only strike down a health regulation if it has no real or substantial relation to public safety, or is a plain and palpable invasion of rights secured by fundamental law.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Justice Harlan's majority opinion rests on three interlocking principles. First, organized society requires that individuals surrender some measure of freedom for the safety and comfort of the whole; there is no absolute right to be wholly free from restraint at all times and in all circumstances. Second, the police power of a state embraces reasonable regulations to protect public health and safety, with the mode and manner of regulation within legislative discretion subject only to constitutional limits. Third, during a genuine smallpox outbreak, a community has the right to protect itself on the principle of "self-defense" and "paramount necessity," and courts would usurp legislative functions by second-guessing the chosen remedy.

Harlan drew the outer limit of review narrowly: courts may intervene only when a health regulation has no real or substantial relation to its stated purpose, or is, beyond all question, a plain and palpable invasion of a fundamental right. Harlan added a notable caveat: if vaccination would be cruel and inhuman in a particular individual's health circumstances, a court might intervene to prevent that specific application, indicating the rule is not absolute even as a general proposition.

Professor Chandler emphasizes the Lochner Same-Term Paradox: Jacobson and Lochner v New York were decided in the same term (1905). Jacobson upheld public health legislation deferentially; Lochner struck down maximum-hours legislation. The tension between these two cases structured the SDP debate for the next century.

### Dissent (Brewer and Peckham)

Brewer and Peckham dissented without written opinion, a practice common in the Fuller Court era but unusually pointed here given the stakes. Their reasoning must be reconstructed from their jurisprudential commitments and the surrounding docket. Peckham authored *Lochner v New York* the same term, striking down New York's bakery-hours law as an arbitrary interference with the liberty of contract. Brewer consistently sided with Peckham on liberty-of-contract questions and had authored *In re Debs* and other decisions treating property and personal liberty as co-extensive constitutional values presumptively shielded from state regulation.

The implied dissent likely rested on three points. First, on the reach of the police power: Peckham's framework in Lochner demanded that a regulation have a direct relation to a proper public purpose, and the Jacobson record offered only a general community interest in herd immunity rather than proof that this defendant's vaccination would prevent a specific contagion event. Second, on the bodily-intrusion premise: the Lochner-Peckham view of "liberty" in the Fourteenth Amendment was capacious enough to protect contract freedom, and the dissenters would have treated the forcible introduction of foreign matter into the body as a more severe liberty intrusion than a limitation on labor hours. Third, on the "paramount necessity" predicate: Peckham's Lochner opinion rejected pretextual invocations of health when the legislature's genuine motive was economic or paternalistic, and the dissenters presumably doubted whether Cambridge's smallpox prevalence rose to the crisis threshold that could justify physical compulsion. The absence of a written opinion leaves the dissent doctrinally thin, which ironically strengthened Harlan's framing by default and made Jacobson the Lochner-era precedent most frequently cited against Lochner itself.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

**Hypothetical 1: Mandatory Ebola quarantine during an active outbreak.** A county imposes a 21-day isolation order on every person arriving from a West African country experiencing community transmission of Ebola. A returning aid worker refuses the quarantine, sues under the Due Process Clause, and asks for an injunction on the ground that she is currently asymptomatic.

**Why the same result.** The record supplies exactly the "paramount necessity" predicate Harlan required in Jacobson: an ongoing outbreak of a virulent and often-fatal disease for which the only proven containment tool is interrupting person-to-person transmission. The regulation has a real and substantial relation to public safety; asymptomatic persons are known potential carriers of filoviruses; and the 21-day window tracks the documented maximum incubation period, so the means are not patently disproportionate. Under Jacobson's deferential standard courts do not substitute their judgment for the health authorities' on the specific mode of containment.

**Hypothetical 2: Compulsory TB testing for hospital clinical staff.** A state health department requires every clinician with direct patient contact to undergo annual tuberculosis screening. A pulmonologist refuses on the ground that she has no risk factors and the requirement is an unconstitutional invasion of bodily integrity.

**Why the same result.** Occupational exposure in a hospital creates a distinctive and well-documented disease-transmission pathway; the regulation targets a population that is both uniquely at risk and uniquely situated to transmit the disease to immunocompromised patients. Screening (as distinct from vaccination) is a minimal bodily intrusion that produces direct evidentiary value. The "real and substantial relation" test is easily met, and no Jacobson caveat applies because the screening does not pose any risk of cruel or inhuman application.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

**Hypothetical 1: Compulsory smallpox vaccination with no outbreak.** A state with no reported smallpox cases in forty years requires every adult to present proof of vaccination as a condition of renewing a driver's license. The program is justified as "historic prophylaxis." An adult without medical contraindications refuses and is denied his license.

**Why a different result.** Harlan's opinion was at pains to tie the holding to the Cambridge smallpox prevalence of the moment, resting the state's authority on a "self-defense" and "paramount necessity" premise that presupposes a current or imminent outbreak. Generalized historical prophylaxis collapses the predicate: without disease pressure, a mandatory bodily intrusion has no "real or substantial relation" to present public safety and is "beyond all question, a plain, palpable invasion" of liberty. The rationale also fails the narrower caveat Harlan floated: when the public-health evidence does not support the specific mode of intrusion, the regulation is arbitrary even on the deferential standard.

**Hypothetical 2: State-mandated cosmetic dental work for children receiving public assistance.** A state requires every child in its Medicaid program to undergo orthodontic treatment at public expense, justified as "preventing later infection" through general dental hygiene. A parent refuses on grounds of bodily integrity.

**Why a different result.** The "health" rationale is pretextual: orthodontic straightening treats aesthetics and alignment, not infection risk, and no epidemic or contagion is invoked. The regulation therefore has no real or substantial relation to public health; it is a paternalistic imposition on bodily autonomy dressed in health language. Even Lochner-era precedents like *Mugler v Kansas* respected the legislature's genuine police-power judgments, but Jacobson did not abolish the judicial duty to test whether a claimed health purpose is actually served. The case falls into Harlan's "plain, palpable invasion" carve-out.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

**Hypothetical: Mandatory COVID-19 booster for public-school teachers during a moderate winter wave.** A state department of education requires every classroom teacher to obtain an annual COVID-19 booster within 60 days of its FDA authorization, with only a narrow medical-exemption process. The state cites elevated transmission among school-age children, an increase in hospitalizations among elderly grandparents of students, and CDC guidance that boosters reduce transmission marginally for the first several weeks. A teacher with no medical contraindication objects on bodily-autonomy grounds and argues that his individual risk is low.

**Why this is genuinely unclear.** The case pulls Jacobson in two directions. On the one hand, there is a recognized public-health context, a legislature-delegated agency acting in good faith, and an occupational nexus between teachers and a vulnerable population, all of which Jacobson's "real and substantial relation" test would treat deferentially. On the other hand, the "paramount necessity" predicate is weaker than Cambridge's active smallpox outbreak in 1902; the marginal efficacy of annual boosters at interrupting transmission is contested in the peer-reviewed literature; and the burden-benefit comparison is closer than anything Harlan considered. Lower federal courts in post-COVID cases (for example *Klaassen v. Trustees of Indiana University* and *Kheriaty v. Regents of the University of California*) have divided on how much current scientific uncertainty Jacobson tolerates before the regulation loses its police-power footing. Reasonable judges could read Jacobson either to sustain the mandate as a classical police-power exercise or to narrow the precedent by invoking its own "cruel-and-inhuman application" caveat when the medical evidence is unsettled.

## Critique

**The Same-Term Paradox (Professor Chandler's framing).** Jacobson and Lochner were decided in the same October Term 1904, with overlapping majorities on the police-power question but opposite outcomes. Harlan wrote for seven Justices to uphold a bodily-intrusion mandate; Peckham wrote for five Justices to strike down a maximum-hours law the same year. The doctrinal rationalization offered at the time (health regulations are within the police power, economic regulations are not) never held up analytically: Lochner itself was pleaded as a health measure (bakery workers' lung exposure), and Jacobson was pleaded in part as an economic regulation of a professionalized medical product. The distinction that actually did the work was the Court's differential credulity: Justices believed the smallpox threat was real and doubted that flour dust made bakers sick. That credulity gap, dressed up as doctrine, is what West Coast Hotel later had to dismantle.

**The principled-distinction question.** If Jacobson and Lochner are read together, the Court in 1905 applied something like rational-basis review to health legislation and something approaching strict scrutiny to economic legislation, all within a single Due Process framework. The distinction cannot be sustained on the text of the Fourteenth Amendment, which protects "liberty" without disaggregating bodily liberty from contract liberty. The more honest reading is that Jacobson is the Lochner era's exception that swallowed the rule: once the Court conceded that "liberty" in each of its phases has its history and connotation and is subject to reasonable police-power regulation, the game of separating protected economic liberties from unprotected health regulations became a battle over which Justices counted the evidence.

**The Holmes-Buck v Bell extension.** Holmes's brutal opinion in *Buck v Bell* (1927) cited Jacobson as authority for Virginia's compulsory-sterilization law on the strength of the proposition that "the principle that sustains compulsory vaccination is broad enough to cover cutting the Fallopian tubes." That extension was doctrinally unfaithful: Jacobson rested on a specific outbreak, reversible bodily intrusion, a flat fine for refusal, and a judicial carve-out for cruel-and-inhuman application. Sterilization implicated none of those limits — no epidemic, irreversible intrusion, no meaningful exit option, and no individual-hardship escape valve. Buck v Bell's reliance on Jacobson is a cautionary tale about how a narrowly-framed holding can be abstracted into a general police-power license for coercive bodily intervention. It is one of the strongest arguments against reading Jacobson as a standalone permission slip.

**Progressive reading.** Progressive scholars (Minow, Parmet) treat Jacobson as the textbook illustration that constitutional liberty is "liberty within community," not a pre-political right to noninterference. On this reading the case anchors the communitarian strand of modern public-law theory and rightly subordinates individual preference to democratically-authorized disease control. The flip side of the same reading is that Jacobson supplies the infrastructure for progressive regulation generally: if the Court had taken Peckham's implied dissent, much of the twentieth-century administrative state would have foundered on individual-liberty objections.

**Originalist and textualist critiques.** From the originalist side, the case is vulnerable on the ground that the Fourteenth Amendment was drafted and ratified against a backdrop in which state compulsory-vaccination statutes were understood as classical police-power exercises, not as infringements of constitutionally-protected liberty — which cuts in favor of Harlan's outcome but against the substantive-due-process doctrinal vehicle he used to reach it. A strict textualist reading would reach Harlan's result through reserved state police powers rather than through a Fourteenth-Amendment balancing test. Libertarian scholars (Epstein, Barnett) treat Jacobson as the original sin that made the Lochner-era "liberty of contract" line doctrinally untenable by conceding that bodily autonomy can be overridden for community benefit.

**Pandemic-era revival and limits.** COVID-19 litigation from 2020 to 2023 relied heavily on Jacobson to sustain emergency closure orders, occupancy limits, and vaccine mandates. The Court's per-curiam stay opinions in *South Bay* and *Tandon v Newsom* and its merits decision in *NFIB v OSHA* signaled an important qualification: Jacobson does not license unlimited health-emergency power, and regulations that are not neutral as to religious exercise or that exceed statutory authority fail even when a public-health rationale is present. The modern pattern is that Jacobson survives as a deferential baseline but does not override the First Amendment, the major-questions doctrine, or clear-statement rules against agency overreach.

**Exam framing.** On an exam the Jacobson formula is deployable wherever the state asserts a health, safety, or morals rationale for restricting individual liberty. The moves are (1) name the "real or substantial relation" test and the "plain, palpable invasion" carve-out as the deferential Lochner-era standard; (2) surface the Jacobson caveat about cruel-and-inhuman application for the specific claimant; (3) check whether a First Amendment or neutrality overlay changes the analysis under *Lukumi* or *Fulton*; and (4) flag the Holmes-Buck v Bell misuse as the cautionary tale about over-abstracting the precedent.

## Key Quotations

> "But the liberty secured by the Constitution of the United States to every person within its jurisdiction does not import an absolute right in each person to be, at all times and in all circumstances, wholly freed from restraint. There are manifold restraints to which every person is necessarily subject for the common good. On any other basis organized society could not exist with safety to its members." (Harlan, J.) [197 U.S. at 26](https://app.midpage.ai/document/jacobson-v-massachusetts-96230?utm_source=mcp&lines=53-64)

> "If there is any such power in the judiciary to review legislative action in respect of a matter affecting the general welfare, it can only be when that which the legislature has done comes within the rule that, if a statute purporting to have been enacted to protect the public health, the public morals, or the public safety, has no real or substantial relation to those objects, or is, beyond all question, a plain, palpable invasion of rights secured by the fundamental law, it is the duty of the courts to so adjudge, and thereby give effect to the Constitution." (Harlan, J.) [197 U.S. at 31](https://app.midpage.ai/document/jacobson-v-massachusetts-96230?utm_source=mcp&lines=110)

> "We are not prepared to hold that a minority, residing or remaining in any city or town where smallpox is prevalent, and enjoying the general protection afforded by an organized local government, may thus defy the will of its constituted authorities, acting in good faith for all, under the legislative sanction of the state." (Harlan, J.) [197 U.S. at 37–38](https://app.midpage.ai/document/jacobson-v-massachusetts-96230?utm_source=mcp&lines=140-145)

## Key Points

- Establishes that constitutional liberty is not absolute but is "regulated by law" -- the foundational premise of police power jurisprudence.
- The "real or substantial relation" / "plain and palpable invasion" standard anticipates rational basis review but applies it to health regulations specifically.
- The Jacobson caveat (cruel-and-inhuman application) hints at an individual exception that later cases ignore.
- Holmes cited Jacobson directly in Buck v Bell to justify compulsory sterilization -- widely criticized as a misuse.
- The Lochner Same-Term Paradox is a recurrent exam topic: two cases decided the same term reach opposite outcomes on police power deference.
- Jacobson's standard has been invoked (controversially) in COVID-19 vaccination-mandate litigation; the Court has cautioned that it does not give states unlimited health-emergency power.
