# Texas v Johnson (1989)

## Memory Jogger

> Flag burning as political protest is protected expressive conduct under the First Amendment; the government may not prohibit expression merely because society finds the idea offensive or disagreeable.

---

## Facts

During the 1984 Republican National Convention in Dallas, Gregory Lee Johnson participated in a political demonstration called the "Republican War Chest Tour," protesting Reagan administration policies. While demonstrators marched through Dallas streets, chanting slogans and staging "die-ins," Johnson accepted an American flag taken from a flagpole near a corporate headquarters. Outside Dallas City Hall, Johnson doused the flag in lighter fluid and burned it while protesters chanted. No injuries or threats occurred. Johnson was charged under Texas Penal Code § 42.09(a)(3), which prohibited the desecration of a venerated object in a manner the actor knew would seriously offend one or more persons likely to observe or discover the action. He was convicted and sentenced to one year in prison and a $2,000 fine.

---

## Procedural History

The Dallas County Criminal Court convicted Johnson. The Fifth Court of Appeals of Texas reversed, holding the statute violated the First Amendment. The Texas Court of Criminal Appeals affirmed the reversal, finding that because Johnson's conduct was clearly expressive political protest, it was protected. Texas sought certiorari, framing the question as whether a state may prohibit the public desecration of the American flag as a means of preserving national unity and preventing breaches of the peace. The Supreme Court granted certiorari and decided 5-4 to affirm the Court of Criminal Appeals.

## Holding

Burning an American flag as political protest is expressive conduct protected by the First Amendment. Texas's interest in preventing breaches of the peace did not justify the conviction because no disturbance occurred. Texas's interest in preserving the flag as a symbol of nationhood is related to the suppression of expression and therefore fails outside the O'Brien framework; the State may not prohibit the expression of an idea simply because society finds it offensive or disagreeable.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion (Brennan, J.)

The majority applies a two-step framework. First, it asks whether Johnson's conduct was sufficiently imbued with communicative elements to implicate the First Amendment. Because the protest was overtly political and took place during the Republican National Convention, the expressive nature was both intentional and overwhelmingly apparent, satisfying the *Spence* test for symbolic speech. Second, the Court evaluates whether the government's interest is related to the suppression of free expression or is unrelated. Texas's breach-of-peace interest fails because no disturbance actually occurred or was threatened. Texas's interest in preserving the flag as a symbol of nationhood is necessarily content-based — it is only implicated when the flag's treatment communicates a message — so the O'Brien intermediate-scrutiny framework does not apply and the restriction must satisfy strict scrutiny, which it cannot. The "bedrock principle" underlying the First Amendment is that the government may not prohibit expression simply because society finds it offensive.

## Key Points

- Flag burning as political protest satisfies the *Spence* two-part test: intent to convey a particularized message and likelihood that viewers would understand it.
- The O'Brien test for expressive conduct (intermediate scrutiny) only applies when the government's interest is unrelated to the suppression of expression; here the interest in national unity is content-based, so O'Brien does not apply.
- Bedrock principle: government may not prohibit expression because of the viewpoint it conveys or because the audience finds it offensive.
- Notable vote alignment: Scalia and Kennedy joined the liberal bloc over the strong objection of Rehnquist, White, O'Connor, and Stevens.
- Congress subsequently passed the Flag Protection Act of 1989, which the Court struck down one year later in *United States v. Eichman* (1990) on identical reasoning.
