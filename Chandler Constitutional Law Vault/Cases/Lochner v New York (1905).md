# Lochner v New York (1905)

## Memory Jogger

> New York's 60-hour-per-week bakery labor law struck down as an unconstitutional interference with freedom of contract under the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause — the case that launched the "Lochner era" of economic substantive due process.

---

## Facts

In 1895, New York enacted the Bakeshop Act, which prohibited bakery employees from working more than ten hours per day or sixty hours per week. The legislature framed the statute as a health regulation, relying on evidence that long hours in hot, flour-dust-filled environments harmed workers' respiratory health.

Joseph Lochner owned a small bakery in Utica, New York. He was twice convicted under the Act for allowing an employee, Aman Schmitter, to work more than sixty hours in a single week. After a fine of $25 on the first offense and $50 on the second, Lochner appealed. He argued that the Act deprived both employers and employees of their liberty to contract for labor on terms they mutually agreed to, without due process of law, in violation of the Fourteenth Amendment.

New York defended the statute as a legitimate exercise of the police power to protect the health and safety of bakery workers, analogizing to prior decisions upholding mining regulations and other hazardous-trade restrictions.

---

## Procedural History

Lochner was convicted in the County Court of Oneida County and again on appeal in the Appellate Division of the New York Supreme Court, which affirmed on the ground that baking was a trade whose conditions posed a genuine health risk. The New York Court of Appeals affirmed by a 4-3 vote, sustaining the Act as a valid health regulation. Lochner then appealed to the U.S. Supreme Court, which noted probable jurisdiction.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority:** Peckham (joined by Fuller, Brewer, Brown, McKenna)
- **Dissent (First):** Harlan (joined by White and Day) — argued the Act bore a reasonable relation to health and that courts should defer to legislative judgments on such matters
- **Dissent (Second):** Holmes (alone) — argued the Constitution does not enact a particular economic theory and the majority confused constitutional interpretation with laissez-faire ideology

---

## Holding

New York's Bakeshop Act, limiting bakery employees to sixty hours per week, is an unconstitutional interference with the liberty of contract protected by the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause. The Act is not a legitimate health regulation because baking is not a sufficiently dangerous or unhealthy trade to justify the restriction. A statute limiting the hours of "grown and intelligent men" who are free to bargain for their own labor terms is a "meddlesome interference" with individual liberty unless there is "fair and reasonable ground" for a finding of material danger to public health.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Justice Peckham's majority began with the premise established in *Allgeyer v. Louisiana* (1897): the liberty protected by the Fourteenth Amendment includes the right to make contracts, specifically the right to purchase or sell labor. This economic liberty is not absolute — states may regulate under the police power to protect public health, safety, morals, and general welfare — but the Court insisted on substantive review of whether the regulation genuinely served those ends. The test Peckham articulated asked whether there was "a fair and reasonable ground" to say there was "material danger to the public health" absent the restriction. Reviewing the nature of bakery work, the majority concluded there was not: baking is a clean and ordinary occupation, not inherently more dangerous than any sedentary trade. The Act therefore crossed from legitimate health regulation into "a purely labor law" imposing maximum hours to benefit one class at the expense of another's liberty. The majority rejected New York's analogy to mining regulations, distinguishing those cases on the ground that underground mining presented genuine hazards that baking did not.

The internal logic of the majority opinion turns on what commentators have called the "mere pretext" test. Peckham acknowledges that the legislature labeled the Bakeshop Act a health regulation, but he refuses to accept that label at face value. Instead the Court asks, as an independent matter, whether the statute has "more than the most remote relation to the public health" of bakers. If not, the Court treats the health rationale as a pretext concealing a redistributive labor statute, and the real-purpose inquiry becomes the operative constitutional question. This move is what allows the majority to elevate its own empirical judgment about bakery work over the legislature's, and it is the mechanism Harlan attacks most directly in dissent.

The Court's empirical judgment rests on a surprising degree of judicial notice. Peckham offers no sustained analysis of the medical record in the New York courts below. He simply asserts that "to the common understanding the trade of a baker has never been regarded as an unhealthy one," and he draws contrasts with mining and smelting that are argued by intuition rather than evidence. Harlan in dissent points out that New York had assembled medical data, including testimony about respiratory disease, flour dust, and elevated mortality, which the majority effectively ignores. Holmes captures the same critique at a higher level of abstraction: the Court has read its own economic priors, which he identifies with Herbert Spencer's laissez-faire social theory, into the Fourteenth Amendment itself.

The limits implied by these criticisms are sharp. If the police power survives only where a statute genuinely serves a health or safety purpose, and if the Court is the final judge of "genuinely," then nothing in the doctrine prevents a later Court from reaching opposite empirical conclusions on identical facts. Lochner as a methodological matter is therefore unstable: it collapses back into judicial values under any sustained application, which is precisely what happened by 1937.

### Concurrence

No separate concurrence was filed. The majority opinion represents the full five-justice view (Peckham writing for Fuller, Brewer, Brown, and McKenna). This is worth noting because a concurrence along narrower lines would have materially weakened the precedent: a fifth vote written to tie the result more tightly to the specific record on baking conditions would have made Lochner harder to extend to minimum-wage cases like *Adkins v Children's Hospital* (1923). The absence of such a concurrence is part of why Lochner came to stand for the broad liberty-of-contract doctrine rather than for a narrower health-regulation holding.

### Dissent (Harlan)

Justice Harlan, joined by White and Day, argues that the record amply supported New York's legislative judgment. He marshals medical authority (most notably Professor Hirt's treatise on the diseases of workers, which the dissent quotes) to establish that bakers suffered disproportionate rates of respiratory illness, inflammation of the eyes, and rheumatism, and that long hours aggravated these conditions. On this empirical foundation Harlan builds a separation-of-powers argument: when reasonable minds can differ about the factual basis for a health regulation, courts must defer. The governing standard, he says, comes from *Mugler v Kansas* (1887): a statute passed under the police power is to be sustained unless it is "plainly and palpably" beyond the state's authority, or bears "no real or substantial relation" to a legitimate object. Measured by that test the Bakeshop Act was comfortably valid, and the majority's willingness to override the New York legislature, the state courts, and the published medical literature amounts to the Court substituting its own policy preferences for the judgment of the political branches. Harlan's dissent previews both the deferential posture of *West Coast Hotel v Parrish* (1937) and, more broadly, the modern rational-basis standard applied to economic regulation.

### Dissent (Holmes)

Justice Holmes's solo dissent is the most-quoted opinion in the case and arguably the most-quoted passage in twentieth-century American constitutional law. Holmes begins by stripping away the doctrinal vocabulary of liberty of contract and confronting the majority at the level of method. "This case is decided upon an economic theory which a large part of the country does not entertain," he writes, and the Fourteenth Amendment "is not intended to embody a particular economic theory, whether of paternalism and the organic relation of the citizen to the State or of laissez faire." The rhetorical centerpiece is his line that the Amendment "does not enact Mr. Herbert Spencer's Social Statics," a direct accusation that the majority has confused a contestable political philosophy with constitutional command.

Holmes's affirmative test is a proto-rational-basis standard. A statute should be upheld "unless it can be said that a rational and fair man necessarily would admit that the statute proposed would infringe fundamental principles as they have been understood by the traditions of our people and our law." On that test, the Bakeshop Act is plainly valid: reasonable people do believe maximum-hours laws protect workers, and that is the end of the constitutional inquiry. The "rational and fair man" formulation is a direct ancestor of the post-1937 rational-basis review articulated in *West Coast Hotel*, *United States v Carolene Products* (1938), and *Williamson v Lee Optical* (1955). Modern constitutional doctrine for economic legislation is, in essence, Holmes's Lochner dissent promoted to majority rule.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

**Hypo 1: Grocery stockers.** New York enacts a statute capping the hours of grocery store stockers at fifty per week and cites general worker-fatigue studies but no documented health hazards specific to shelving work. Under Peckham's analysis, the majority would strike the law. Stocking is a clean indoor trade, not inherently dangerous, and the absence of a specific health record makes the statute look like a pure labor-welfare measure. The "fair and reasonable ground" test is not met because nothing about the occupation presents "material danger to the public health." Grocery stockers are "grown and intelligent men" free to bargain their own terms, so the law is a "meddlesome interference" with liberty of contract.

**Hypo 2: Minimum rest periods for office clerks.** A state mandates that insurance-office clerks receive at least thirty minutes of rest every four hours. No specific health hazard is documented and the legislative history emphasizes worker morale rather than health. The Lochner majority would strike this down for the same reasons, with even less hesitation: office work is sedentary, climate-controlled, and presumptively safe, so the health rationale is transparently a cover for an employment-regulation measure. Under 1905 liberty-of-contract doctrine, the state has no authority to allocate working time between employers and employees absent a genuine public-health predicate.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

**Hypo 3: Underground coal miners.** Utah limits underground coal miners to eight hours per day. Even the Lochner majority upholds the statute, as it did in *Holden v Hardy* (1898), which Peckham distinguishes rather than overrules. Underground mining is genuinely dangerous work, marked by risks of asphyxiation, cave-in, and cumulative pulmonary disease. The "material danger to the public health" predicate is plainly satisfied, and the law passes the "fair and reasonable ground" test. The majority explicitly reaffirms *Holden* in the course of striking down the Bakeshop Act, treating mining as the paradigm of a trade for which hours regulation is valid.

**Hypo 4: Toxic-fume workers.** A state caps hours for workers in a chromium electroplating plant at forty-five per week, with an extensive legislative record documenting elevated cancer mortality and acute respiratory injury. The Lochner majority would uphold the statute. The health record goes well beyond anything New York compiled for bakers, and the hazard is both specific and severe. Under the majority's own logic, the "fair and reasonable ground" threshold is satisfied and the police power is validly invoked. This hypothetical illustrates that Lochner does not categorically reject hours legislation; it rejects hours legislation unsupported by a particularized health predicate the Court finds convincing.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

**Hypo 5: Laundry workers.** Oregon caps the hours of women working in steam laundries at ten per day, citing heat, humidity, extended standing, and documented rates of miscarriage and respiratory illness. This is the fact pattern of *Muller v Oregon* (1908), decided three years after Lochner, and it shows precisely how unstable the Lochner framework is at the margin. The Court in Muller sustained the statute, but only after accepting the now-famous "Brandeis brief," which compiled sociological and medical evidence to argue that women's physical constitution warranted special protection. The outcome is therefore overdetermined by two factors the Lochner majority would otherwise weigh against each other: a reasonably strong medical record (which by itself might not suffice under Peckham's skeptical standard), and the gender classification (which *Muller* treats as independently sufficient). If the record were identical but the statute applied to male laundry workers, it is a much closer question whether the Lochner majority would uphold it, and plausible that the Court would strike it down on the ground that laundry work, like baking, is not "plainly" hazardous enough to support the restriction.

## Critique

**Textualist critique.** The Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause protects "life, liberty, or property" from deprivation "without due process of law." Liberty of contract, as an economic right against state regulation of the terms of employment, finds no obvious textual home in that language. The Lochner majority builds its doctrine from *Allgeyer v Louisiana* (1897), which itself builds on earlier privileges-and-immunities cases, in a chain of authority that critics from Thayer onward have argued was essentially invented rather than derived. On this view, Lochner's procedural due-process clothing conceals a substantive judicial policy choice that the text does not authorize.

**Holmes's methodological critique.** Holmes's charge is not that the liberty-of-contract doctrine is textually indefensible but that it is philosophically contingent. The Court has read one particular school of political economy, the laissez-faire school associated with Spencer, Sumner, and the classical economists, into constitutional law. That choice pre-empts democratic deliberation over industrial-economy questions that the Constitution's framers did not answer, and it freezes a contested political program into constitutional command. The critique is devastating precisely because it does not depend on showing Spencer wrong; it shows that whether Spencer is right is not a constitutional question.

**Distributional critique (Harlan, later the Progressives).** Liberty of contract, as applied, protected the party with greater bargaining power, the employer, by disabling the legislature from equalizing terms of the employment relation. Harlan hints at this; later Progressive critics (Roscoe Pound, Louis Brandeis, Felix Frankfurter) develop it fully. The doctrine treats a structurally unequal bargaining relationship as if it were a negotiation between equals, and it assigns the cost of that fiction to the weaker party. By the 1930s, this critique helped generate the political conditions for the switch-in-time.

**Originalist critique.** The Reconstruction Amendment's drafters were focused on civil rights for freedmen, not on constitutionalizing laissez-faire. Evidence from the 1866 debates, the ratification record, and early post-ratification cases provides no support for a substantive right to be free of economic regulation. Even scholars who accept a substantive component to due process (for example, Randy Barnett on the Privileges or Immunities Clause) do not typically defend the Lochner Court's particular liberty-of-contract doctrine as historically grounded.

**Doctrinal repudiation and the deeper problem.** *West Coast Hotel v Parrish* (1937) formally repudiates Lochner by overruling *Adkins v Children's Hospital* (1923), and Carolene Products footnote 4 (1938) reallocates close judicial review from economic liberty toward discrete and insular minorities and fundamental personal rights. But the deeper problem Lochner raises survives the repudiation: any substantive due-process methodology requires judges to identify which liberties are "fundamental," and any such identification is open to the Holmesian charge that the judges are reading their own preferences into the Amendment. Modern abortion, sexual-autonomy, and marriage cases have repeatedly been compared to Lochner by critics who argue that the analogy holds whatever the subject matter.

**Chandler's Same-Term Paradox.** Professor Chandler flags a striking fact about the 1904 Term: *Jacobson v Massachusetts* (1905) upheld a state smallpox vaccination mandate over a Fourteenth Amendment liberty challenge, applying a deferential police-power analysis that is tonally closer to Harlan's Lochner dissent than to Peckham's majority. Reading Lochner and Jacobson together makes the doctrinal picture incoherent: same term, same Amendment, same state-police-power framework, opposite outcomes on opposite levels of deference. The paradox invites the question whether Lochner was the rule and Jacobson the aberration, whether the reverse was true, or whether the two cases reveal that the Fuller Court lacked any stable theory of when economic versus health-and-safety legislation would be sustained. The paradox also previews what modern substantive-due-process doctrine continues to struggle with: how to justify differential scrutiny across categories of liberty that the text does not itself categorize.

## Key Points

- Inaugurates the "Lochner era" (c. 1905–1937) of heightened judicial review of economic regulations under a substantive liberty-of-contract theory
- Overruled by *West Coast Hotel v Parrish* (1937), which rejected Adkins and embraced legislative deference on economic regulation
- Holmes's dissent is among the most-quoted passages in American constitutional law; his "rational and fair man" test anticipates modern rational-basis review
- Lochner-era analysis did not call itself "strict scrutiny" or "rational basis" — it used a sliding-scale "fair and reasonable relation" inquiry without those labels
- Professor Chandler's "Same-Term Paradox": *Jacobson v Massachusetts* (upholding vaccination mandates over individual liberty objections) was decided the same term
