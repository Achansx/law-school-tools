# Michael H v Gerald D (1989)

## Memory Jogger

> A 5-4 Court upheld California's marital presumption against a biological father's claim to visitation rights, holding that no fundamental liberty interest protects an adulterous biological father's relationship with his illegitimate child when the mother is married to another man; famous for Justice Scalia's plurality footnote 6 requiring courts to identify the tradition protecting or denying protection to an asserted right at its most specific level.

---

## Facts

Carole D. was married to Gerald D. but began an adulterous relationship with Michael H. and gave birth to Victoria D. in 1981. Blood tests established a 98.07% probability that Michael was Victoria's biological father. Carole and Victoria lived with Michael for periods of time and he held Victoria out as his daughter, but Carole reconciled with Gerald on several occasions. California law (Evidence Code sec. 621) created a near-irrebuttable presumption that a child born to a married woman cohabiting with her husband is the husband's legitimate child; only the husband or wife could rebut the presumption. Michael sought a declaration of paternity and visitation rights. A guardian ad litem was appointed for Victoria. The California courts applied the marital presumption and denied Michael's claim. Michael argued that his biological relationship and prior emotional bond with Victoria gave him a constitutionally protected liberty interest in continued contact.

---

## Procedural History

The California Superior Court granted Gerald's motion for summary judgment on the paternity claim, applying the marital presumption. The California Court of Appeal affirmed, rejecting Michael's due process claim. The California Supreme Court denied review. The U.S. Supreme Court affirmed by a 5-4 vote, with no majority opinion for the most critical portion of the reasoning.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Plurality (4 on part III):** Scalia (author), joined by Rehnquist (C.J.), O'Connor, Kennedy (parts I, II, III-A, III-B, IV); O'Connor and Kennedy did not join footnote 6 of Part II-B
- **Concurrence in judgment:** Stevens (would affirm on narrower statutory grounds)
- **Dissent:** Brennan (joined by Marshall, Blackmun) (tradition protects family relationships; biological paternity is sufficient)
- **Dissent:** White (joined by Brennan) (prior cases protect the unitary family regardless of adultery)

---

## Holding

California's marital presumption statute, as applied to deny a biological father the right to establish paternity and seek visitation with a child born to a married woman, does not violate the Due Process Clause. There is no fundamental liberty interest protecting an adulterous biological father's relationship with his child where the mother is married to another man and the marital family remains intact. The relevant tradition, properly described at the most specific level of generality, has consistently refused to recognize the rights of adulterous biological fathers against the marital presumption, not protected them.

---

## Analysis

### Plurality Opinion (Scalia)

Justice Scalia's plurality articulated the most restrictive version of the history-and-tradition test. The plurality began by assuming the existence of a protected liberty interest in some family relationships -- but immediately turned to the question of how to characterize the right claimed. Scalia insisted that courts must identify the tradition at the "most specific level at which a relevant tradition protecting, or denying protection to, the asserted interest can be identified." At that level, the plurality found not a tradition protecting the rights of adulterous biological fathers but rather a consistent tradition of denying them: the marital presumption had been a fixture of Anglo-American law for centuries, protecting the marital family unit and the child's legitimacy. When the question was framed at this specific level, the answer was straightforward: no fundamental right existed, rational basis review applied, and California's interests in protecting the marital family and avoiding paternity disputes easily satisfied that standard.

## Key Points

- The central methodological contribution is the "most specific level" requirement in Scalia's footnote 6: courts must characterize the claimed right at the most particularized level of tradition, not at an abstract level that could encompass any liberty claim
- Footnote 6 commanded only two votes (Scalia and Rehnquist); O'Connor and Kennedy explicitly refused to join it, leaving its precedential force uncertain
- Marks rule problem: when the Court fractures on methodology, lower courts face unresolved questions about which rationale controls
- The counterpoint to Moore v East Cleveland: both involve extended/non-nuclear family structures, but Moore struck down the restrictive ordinance while Michael H upheld the restrictive statute
- The level-of-generality problem is the central methodological battleground of the SDP methodology wars: Glucksberg's "careful description" requirement partially implements Scalia's footnote 6; Lawrence v Texas implicitly retreated from it
