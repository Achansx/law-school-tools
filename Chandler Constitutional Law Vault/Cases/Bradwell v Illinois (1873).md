# Bradwell v Illinois (1873)

## Memory Jogger

> Decided the same week as the Slaughter-House Cases, the Court held that the right to practice law is not a privilege of national citizenship protected by the Fourteenth Amendment, while Justice Bradley's notorious concurrence argued that women's "paramount destiny" was the domestic sphere and that the legislature could bar them from the professions on natural-law grounds.

---

## Facts

Myra Bradwell was a Chicago attorney, editor of the Chicago Legal News (the most widely circulated legal publication in the United States at the time), and the first woman admitted to the bar in Illinois on merit -- except that the Illinois Supreme Court refused to license her solely because she was a woman. After the Illinois Supreme Court confirmed its refusal on the grounds that the common law tradition excluded women from the legal profession and that married women had no independent legal existence, Bradwell appealed to the United States Supreme Court. She argued that the right to pursue a lawful occupation, including the practice of law, was a privilege of citizenship of the United States protected from state abridgment by the Privileges or Immunities Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. The case came up from the Illinois Supreme Court in 1872, one year after the Fourteenth Amendment's ratification, and was decided in 1873, the same week as the Slaughter-House Cases.

---

## Procedural History

Bradwell sought admission to the Illinois bar in 1869. The Illinois Supreme Court denied her application, initially citing her status as a married woman (and thus legally incapable of entering into binding contracts) and later, on reapplication, holding that state common law excluded women from professional occupations. Bradwell appealed directly to the United States Supreme Court, invoking the Privileges or Immunities Clause. The Supreme Court affirmed, 8-1, in an opinion by Justice Miller.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority:** Miller (joined by the majority of the Court) -- right to practice law is a state privilege, not a national one
- **Concurrence (Bradley, joined by Swayne and Field):** Agrees with the result but adds the "divine ordinance" / natural-law rationale for excluding women from the professions
- **Dissent (Chase):** Chief Justice Chase dissented, without a written opinion

---

## Holding

The right to admission to practice law in the courts of a State is not a privilege or immunity of national citizenship within the meaning of the Privileges or Immunities Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. The regulation of professional licensing belongs to the states and is not governed by national citizenship. Illinois may therefore exclude women from the bar.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Justice Miller's majority opinion was brief and entirely derivative of the Slaughter-House Cases decided simultaneously. Having just ruled in Slaughter-House that the Privileges or Immunities Clause protected only a narrow category of rights incident to national (as opposed to state) citizenship -- rights like access to federal offices, travel to Washington, D.C., and use of navigable waters -- Miller simply applied that holding to Bradwell's claim. The right to practice law in state courts, he held, falls entirely within the domain of state regulation and does not depend on federal citizenship at all. The Fourteenth Amendment did not transfer professional licensing from state to federal control.

The majority did not engage with the sex-discrimination dimension of the case at all. By disposing of Bradwell's claim on Privileges or Immunities grounds without reaching whether the Equal Protection Clause or the Due Process Clause might protect the right to practice law, the Court avoided the question that would occupy later courts: whether the Constitution prohibits exclusion of women from the professions on the basis of sex. Miller wrote as though the question were purely jurisdictional: who regulates bar admission? The answer was the state.

The Slaughter-House Cases, issued days before Bradwell, did most of the analytical work for Miller. Having just reasoned that the Fourteenth Amendment's Privileges or Immunities Clause protected only the narrow set of rights that "owe their existence to the Federal government, its National character, its Constitution, or its laws" — free access to seaports, protection on the high seas, petition of the federal government, habeas corpus — Miller had simply to restate that right-to-practice-law was a state-conferred privilege outside that band. Nothing in the Fourteenth Amendment, he wrote, transferred professional licensing to Congress or to the federal courts. On that framework the sex dimension of Bradwell's claim was irrelevant: the right she asserted was not a national-citizenship right regardless of who asserted it.

That silence was itself a doctrinal choice. Bradwell's counsel (Matthew H. Carpenter, a sitting United States Senator) pressed two distinct theories in her brief: first, that the right to pursue a lawful calling was a privilege of national citizenship that flowed from the Civil Rights Act of 1866 and the Fourteenth Amendment; second, that sex was not a disqualifying characteristic because the statute authorizing bar admission used the gender-neutral term "person." The Court could have engaged either theory and announced a principle about sex classifications under the new amendment; instead it disposed of the case entirely on the first, leaving the sex question untouched. The choice mattered because it cleared the field for Bradley's concurrence to supply what the Court withheld — a substantive theory of why a legislature could exclude women from the professions — and that concurrence, not Miller's opinion, became the doctrinal hook for later critics.

The timing reinforces the point. The Fourteenth Amendment had been ratified in 1868, five years before Bradwell was decided. The Reconstruction Framers in 1866 (when the core Privileges or Immunities language was drafted) plainly did not have women's bar admission in mind; the debates focused on protecting freedmen. Miller's narrow reading of the clause, whatever else can be said about it, is at least consistent with the historical record that no one in the Thirty-Ninth Congress thought they were constitutionalizing women's professional rights. The more contestable move is treating that silence as a reason to foreclose the question rather than to leave it open for future amendment or legislation.

### Concurrence (Bradley)

Bradley concurred in the judgment but not in Miller's reasoning, and the concurrence — joined by Justices Swayne and Field — is the passage the case is remembered for. Bradley offered three overlapping justifications for excluding women from the bar, and understanding why the passage is so often quoted requires disentangling them.

First, Bradley invoked coverture, the common-law doctrine that a married woman's legal existence merged into her husband's. A married woman could not make contracts binding on herself, he noted, and this "very incapacity was one circumstance which the Supreme Court of Illinois deemed important in rendering a married woman incompetent fully to perform the duties and trusts that belong to the office of an attorney." On its own this is a positive-law argument: Illinois had a coverture regime, the legal profession required contracting capacity, and the two could not be reconciled. By itself it would not justify excluding single women at all.

Second, Bradley invoked "nature." "The natural and proper timidity and delicacy which belongs to the female sex," he wrote, "evidently unfits it for many of the occupations of civil life." This is not a positive-law claim; it is an argument about intrinsic sex difference from which legal consequences are drawn. Bradley needed this move because coverture alone could not reach single women, and the Illinois rule excluded all women.

Third, Bradley invoked "the law of the Creator." The "paramount destiny and mission of woman are to fulfil the noble and benign offices of wife and mother. This is the law of the Creator. And the rules of civil society must be adapted to the general constitution of things, and cannot be based upon exceptional cases." This is the theological register — legislative regulation of the professions is valid because it tracks a divine ordinance that human law must follow. Combined with the naturalistic claim, it produced the concurrence's holding: "it is within the province of the legislature to ordain what offices, positions, and callings shall be filled and discharged by men."

The concurrence's rhetorical reach is why it became the doctrinal marker. Miller's opinion had only to apply Slaughter-House; if nothing else were said, Bradwell would have been an unremarkable P or I case. Bradley instead furnished a published federal-supreme-court opinion stating, in the Justices' own voice, that women were constitutionally excludable from professional life on natural-law and religious grounds. That set up the long arc of twentieth-century sex-discrimination doctrine as a direct repudiation of his reasoning.

The contrast with Bradley's own majority opinion ten years later in the Civil Rights Cases (1883) is instructive. There, Bradley argued that the Fourteenth Amendment reached only state action and that freedmen had reached a stage where they should "cease to be the special favorite of the laws" — an argument about formal equality used to foreclose civil-rights legislation. In Bradwell he used the opposite move: substantive sex difference was a legitimate ground for formal legal unequal treatment. The common thread is not consistency of method but a background instinct that the Reconstruction Amendments should not disturb the pre-existing social hierarchy more than their strict text required.

Whether the concurrence rests more on natural law or on common-law coverture is partly a distinction without a difference — Bradley moves freely between registers — but the rhetorical center of gravity is the naturalistic one. Coverture was visibly eroding by 1873 (Illinois itself had passed Married Women's Property Acts in 1861 and 1869 that undid major portions of the doctrine), and an opinion resting only on coverture would have had obvious internal pressure points. The natural-law and theological claims were designed to survive statutory reform, and they are the features critics have been attacking ever since.

### Dissent (Chase)

Chief Justice Chase dissented "from the judgment of the court, and from all the opinions," but did not write. By the time Bradwell was decided Chase was gravely ill — he died about a week after the decision was announced — and his silence has generally been attributed to that circumstance rather than to any doctrinal reticence. What can be inferred from his broader jurisprudence is consistent: in Slaughter-House he had joined the dissenters who read Privileges or Immunities as a substantive guarantee of the right to pursue a lawful calling, and he had a long record as an antislavery lawyer and politician of reading the Reconstruction Amendments expansively against state interference with individual economic rights.

The practical consequence of the unwritten dissent was doctrinal. A recorded Chase opinion arguing that the right to practice a profession was a privilege of national citizenship would have given later courts and advocates a Chief-Justice-level counter-text to quote against Miller and Bradley. Without it, women's professional-rights claims under the Fourteenth Amendment had no published judicial baseline to anchor on until Reed v Reed (1971) and Frontiero v Richardson (1973) reopened the question from the Equal Protection direction nearly a century later.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

1. **State bar examination content.** A state bar requires applicants to pass a state-law-heavy examination that differs substantially from other states, excluding some otherwise-qualified out-of-state attorneys from admission without further study. A rejected applicant sues, claiming the exam abridges his privilege of national citizenship to practice law anywhere in the country. Bradwell and Slaughter-House together answer this cleanly: bar admission is a state privilege, not a national one, and even a restrictive content scheme does not raise a federal Privileges or Immunities problem. The right has a federal dimension only when the Equal Protection or Dormant Commerce Clause analyses engage, neither of which the P or I Clause itself carries.

2. **Medical licensing experience requirement.** A state refuses to license a physician who completed a foreign medical program because the state's statutory scheme requires completion of an ACGME-accredited United States residency. The physician claims the scheme abridges her privilege of national citizenship to practice medicine. The same Slaughter-House/Bradwell logic holds: the right to practice medicine in state courts and state clinics is a privilege of state citizenship, not national, and restrictions on it that are unrelated to a substantive federal constitutional right trigger no federal Privileges or Immunities claim. Any attack on the scheme today would run through Due Process or Equal Protection, not P or I.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

3. **Modern women-excluding bar rule.** A state in 2026 adopts a rule barring married women from practicing law on Bradley-style "domestic sphere" grounds. Under today's Equal Protection doctrine, sex classifications draw intermediate scrutiny under Craig v Boren (1976) and must rest on an "exceedingly persuasive justification" under United States v Virginia (VMI) (1996). Bradley's "nature" and "divine ordinance" reasoning does not come close to an "exceedingly persuasive" non-stereotyped justification; the rule falls even without needing the Privileges or Immunities Clause at all. The doctrinal path Bradwell foreclosed (P or I) was replaced by the Equal Protection path that reaches the same result.

4. **Women-only professional track.** A state medical school reserves 100% of its obstetrics residency slots for women on the theory that women patients will be more comfortable with women obstetricians. Under VMI's "exceedingly persuasive justification" standard the state would need to show the classification does not rest on stereotyped generalizations about men's unfitness for this work. It cannot, because the justification is itself the stereotype the doctrine forbids. Reed v Reed (1971) and VMI reverse Bradley's presumption: sex classifications are constitutionally suspect, and "natural" differences are a reason to require tighter justification, not a reason to relax it.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

5. **Facially neutral rule with severe disparate impact.** A state bar requires 1,800 billable hours per year for the first three years of practice to keep a license active. The rule is sex-neutral on its face but in practice a disproportionately large number of women attorneys lose their licenses because they are more likely to take maternity leave and to be primary caregivers during the covered period. A rejected attorney sues. The doctrinal result is genuinely uncertain. Under Washington v Davis (1976) and Personnel Administrator of Massachusetts v Feeney (1979), disparate impact alone is not an Equal Protection violation without discriminatory purpose, so the EP claim fails unless evidence of intent emerges. Bradwell's holding that licensing is a state privilege with no federal P or I dimension still controls what the Privileges or Immunities Clause does NOT reach. But Title VII-style sex-discrimination law (if it applied) and state constitutional law might reach the impact directly. The hypo captures the real constraint of Bradwell today: it no longer forecloses the ultimate result (women can become lawyers), but it continues to mean that facially-neutral licensing rules with disparate impacts are not themselves federal constitutional problems absent discriminatory purpose.

## Critique

**Progressive feminist critique.** The orthodox feminist-legal-history reading (developed in work by Nancy Cott, Joan Hoff, Linda Kerber, and later Cornelia Dayton) treats Bradwell as the high-water mark of a Reconstruction-era retrenchment. The Fourteenth Amendment had just constitutionalized a new concept of national citizenship; Bradwell and Minor v Happersett (1875) together constricted that citizenship along sex lines before it could operate as a tool of women's advancement. The practical effect lasted for nearly a century: women's admission to the legal profession grew by individual-state statutory reform (which had to proceed piecemeal because no federal constitutional floor existed) rather than by direct constitutional guarantee. When Myra Bradwell herself was finally admitted to the Illinois bar in 1890, and to the United States Supreme Court bar in 1892, the admission came on motion, not by right. Commentators from Reva Siegel onward argue that Bradley's concurrence encoded a "separate spheres" ideology into constitutional rhetoric that continued to reappear (in Muller v Oregon (1908), in Goesaert v Cleary (1948)) for decades, and that it took VMI (1996) to finally dislodge the underlying presumption that "natural" sex differences are a permissible basis for exclusion.

**Historical-originalist critique.** From an originalist direction, Miller's result has more defenders than Bradley's. The Thirty-Ninth Congress that framed the Fourteenth Amendment was plainly focused on the civil-rights status of freedmen; whether its language was understood at ratification to reach women's professional rights is, at minimum, contested. Originalist scholars like Michael Kent Curtis and Kurt Lash have argued that the Privileges or Immunities Clause was designed to carry a broader set of substantive rights than Slaughter-House acknowledged, but even on their reading the historical evidence that the clause reached sex-based licensing is thin. Bradley's concurrence, by contrast, has no defenders on originalist grounds — its invocation of natural law and divine ordinance is not originalism but rather a pre-legal-positivist move that sits uneasily with any constitutional-textualist approach.

**Institutional critique.** The more durable complaint about Bradley's concurrence is not that it was substantively wrong (though it was) but that it was constitutionally the wrong kind of argument. A published federal Supreme Court opinion resting its reasoning on "the law of the Creator" as an authority separate from and prior to positive law effectively imported theology into constitutional adjudication. Critics from Oliver Wendell Holmes forward (Holmes's "The Path of the Law" would appear two decades later and was directly in reaction to this style of reasoning) treated Bradley's concurrence as an exemplar of the kind of natural-law moralizing that gave nineteenth-century constitutional doctrine its instability. Whatever the concurrence meant to do — defend a social order, anchor coverture, furnish a philosophical backstop — it did not do it in the register an appellate court is institutionally equipped to deploy.

**Bradwell's personal postscript.** The case is unusual in that its named plaintiff outlived the doctrine and witnessed its practical softening. Illinois amended its bar-admission statute in 1872 (between the argument and the decision) to remove the sex exclusion; the Illinois Supreme Court admitted Bradwell on its own motion in 1890, and she was admitted to the United States Supreme Court bar two years later. She died in 1894. The story is often told as closure; it is better read as a reminder that Bradwell's constitutional holding about what the Fourteenth Amendment does not require — that states treat sex-based licensing exclusions as abridgments of national citizenship — outlasted the particular Illinois rule by decades and was not formally displaced until Reed v Reed was decided in 1971, nearly a century later.

## Key Points

- **Doctrinal role.** Bradwell applied the Slaughter-House Cases framework to sex and professional licensing: the right to practice law is a state privilege, not a national one, so the Fourteenth Amendment's Privileges or Immunities Clause does not reach it. The holding foreclosed P or I as a path for women's professional-rights claims for the next century.
- **Sibling cases.** Slaughter-House supplied the framework; Minor v Happersett (1875) two years later extended the same logic to women's suffrage, holding that the right to vote was likewise not a privilege of national citizenship. The three cases together neutralized the Privileges or Immunities Clause as a vehicle for sex-equality claims and pushed later litigation onto the Equal Protection and Due Process tracks.
- **Exam-relevant hook.** Bradley's concurrence, not Miller's majority opinion, is what makes Bradwell memorable. The "law of the Creator" and "paramount destiny and mission of woman" passages are the canonical examples of substantive natural-law reasoning in a United States Supreme Court opinion and are cited as the counterpoint to the "exceedingly persuasive justification" standard articulated in United States v Virginia (1996).
- **Doctrinal lineage.** Reed v Reed (1971) displaced Bradley's sex-classification logic without formally overruling Bradwell, because Bradwell is formally a Privileges or Immunities case and Reed is formally an Equal Protection case. Craig v Boren (1976) and VMI (1996) then built out intermediate scrutiny and the "exceedingly persuasive justification" standard that render Bradley's reasoning unsustainable on modern doctrinal terms even though Bradwell's Privileges or Immunities holding remains on the books.
