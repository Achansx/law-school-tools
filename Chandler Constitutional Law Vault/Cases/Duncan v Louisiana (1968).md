# Duncan v Louisiana (1968)

## Memory Jogger

> The case that incorporates the Sixth Amendment right to jury trial against the states and reformulates the Palko test: a right is incorporated if it is fundamental to the American scheme of justice, a pragmatic standard rooted in American legal tradition rather than abstract philosophy about ordered liberty.

---

## Facts

Gary Duncan, a nineteen-year-old Black man from Plaquemines Parish, Louisiana, was driving along a highway when he saw his cousins standing on the roadside with several white boys. Duncan stopped to intervene in what he perceived as a confrontation. During the encounter he allegedly slapped one of the white youths, Herman Landry, on the elbow. Duncan maintained he had merely touched the boy while ushering his cousins into the car. He was arrested and charged with simple battery, a Louisiana misdemeanor punishable by a maximum of two years' imprisonment and a $300 fine.

Duncan sought a jury trial. Under the Louisiana Constitution at the time, jury trials in criminal cases were guaranteed only for offenses in which capital punishment or imprisonment at hard labor could be imposed. Simple battery did not qualify. The trial court denied Duncan's request. He was tried before a judge, convicted, sentenced to sixty days in prison, and fined $150. The Louisiana Supreme Court denied review. Duncan appealed to the United States Supreme Court on the ground that the Fourteenth Amendment, secured him the right to a jury trial that the Sixth Amendment would have guaranteed in federal court.

---

## Procedural History

Convicted by a Louisiana trial judge sitting without a jury; the Louisiana Supreme Court denied review for want of jurisdictional error. Duncan petitioned the United States Supreme Court. The Supreme Court granted certiorari to decide whether the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause guarantees the right to a jury trial in state criminal prosecutions that would, if federal, require a jury under the Sixth Amendment, and whether a crime punishable by up to two years counts as a serious offense for that purpose.

---

## Holding

The Fourteenth Amendment guarantees a right of jury trial in all state criminal cases that would, if prosecuted in federal court, come within the Sixth Amendment's guarantee. Because the right to jury trial in serious criminal cases is fundamental to the American scheme of justice, it is incorporated against the states. A crime punishable by as much as two years in prison is a serious crime, not a petty offense, and therefore entitles the defendant to a jury trial. Duncan's conviction, obtained without a jury for a two-year-maximum offense, must be reversed.

---

## Analysis

### Majority (Opinion of the Court, Justice White)

The Court restates the incorporation inquiry as whether the right in question is fundamental to the American scheme of justice, marking a shift from Palko's abstract ordered liberty language toward a concrete examination of the Anglo-American legal tradition. By that measure the jury right is fundamental: trial by jury in criminal cases carries impressive historical credentials from Magna Carta through the Declaration of Independence, which listed deprivation of jury trial among the colonists' grievances, and it is preserved in every State's criminal justice system. The jury guards against oppressive government and arbitrary adjudication by interposing the common sense of the community between accuser and accused. The Court declines to fix the precise petty-serious line but holds that a two-year maximum puts the offense clearly on the serious side, which entitles Duncan to a jury.

### Concurrence

**Black, J., joined by Douglas, J.** Black writes separately to restate his long-held total-incorporation position. Building on his dissent in Adamson v California (1947), he argues that the Fourteenth Amendment's Privileges or Immunities Clause was designed to make the first eight Amendments fully applicable to the States. The phrase "privileges or immunities of citizens of the United States" is, on Black's reading, "an eminently reasonable way of expressing the idea that henceforth the Bill of Rights shall apply to the States." Any reading that excludes the Bill of Rights' safeguards, Black contends, renders Section One meaningless. He concurs in the judgment because jury trial plainly falls within what he sees as wholesale incorporation, but he treats the majority's selective fundamental-to-American-scheme test as a halfway house: it reaches the right result here but leaves the clause-selection problem unsolved and preserves the Court's discretion to refuse incorporation on a case-by-case basis. Black's concurrence is the most explicit judicial endorsement of total incorporation in the modern era.

**Fortas, J.** Fortas concurs in the holding that the Sixth Amendment jury-trial right binds the states but writes separately to urge that incorporation should not automatically import every federal jury-procedure rule. The States should retain flexibility on ancillary features (jury size less than twelve, unanimity less than full, and waiver procedures) so long as the core right to trial by jury in serious criminal cases is preserved. Fortas's distinction between the core right and its implementing rules anticipates the later retreat from the no-daylight principle in cases like Williams v Florida (1970) (six-member juries permissible in state court) and Apodaca v Oregon (1972) (non-unanimous state juries upheld, later overruled by Ramos v Louisiana (2020)).

### Dissent

**Harlan, J., joined by Stewart, J.** Harlan's dissent is the fullest statement of the incorporation-skeptic position in the modern canon. His argument runs in three steps. First, he rejects the mechanical-incorporation premise: the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires "that those procedures be fundamentally fair in all respects," but "it does not impose on the States the rules that may be in force in the federal courts except where such rules are also found to be essential to basic fairness." Second, he argues that jury trial, while deeply embedded in Anglo-American tradition, is not essential to fair adjudication; a bench trial conducted with professional integrity is fundamentally fair, and many civilized legal systems operate without juries. Third, he warns that selective incorporation distorts federalism by freezing state criminal procedure into the federal-court mold, foreclosing state experimentation that the Tenth Amendment tradition was designed to preserve. His preferred approach is the "gradual process of judicial inclusion and exclusion" used throughout the 19th and early 20th centuries, seeking "those immutable principles of free government which no member of the Union may disregard." On Harlan's view, Palko's ordered-liberty framework was correct, and Duncan's refinement toward "fundamental to the American scheme of justice" smuggles in exactly the mechanical incorporation Palko resisted.

## Hypothetical Applications

**Same-side 1: State misdemeanor carrying eighteen-month maximum, jury denied.** State X charges a defendant with felony-theft-by-deception carrying up to eighteen months' imprisonment. The state permits bench trial absent a jury request and denies the defendant's timely jury request on the ground that the offense is a misdemeanor under state taxonomy. Duncan disposes of the state-label argument: the inquiry turns on the authorized penalty, not the state's classification. Eighteen months falls within the Duncan serious-crime zone that the Court expressly left open to be fixed below the two-year threshold; subsequent cases (Baldwin v New York (1970)) would set the line at more than six months, which an eighteen-month maximum plainly crosses. Jury trial required.

**Same-side 2: Three-year-maximum drug offense prosecuted by judge alone.** A defendant faces state prosecution for possession-with-intent carrying three years' imprisonment. The state constitution guarantees juries only for capital and life cases, tracking the pre-Duncan Louisiana regime. Direct application of Duncan: three years is squarely serious, the state-constitutional limitation cannot override the Fourteenth Amendment jury guarantee, and denial of a jury requires reversal. This is structurally identical to Duncan himself.

**Opposite-side 1: Traffic infraction with $200 fine.** State prosecutes a defendant for speeding, authorized penalty $200 and no jail. Duncan's petty-offense exception controls: the Court in Duncan expressly declined to require juries for genuinely minor offenses, and a fine-only prosecution with no incarceration is the paradigm petty offense. Callan v Wilson (1888) (cited in Duncan) and District of Columbia v Clawans (1937) supported this line before Duncan and remain good law. No jury required.

**Opposite-side 2: Contempt conviction sentencing of ninety days.** State court summarily convicts a contemnor and imposes ninety days. Bloom v Illinois (decided the same day as Duncan) applied Duncan to contempt but only for serious contempts; ninety days falls below the six-month threshold Baldwin would later fix, and Cheff v Schnackenberg (1966) treated similar sentences as petty. No jury required on these facts.

**Fence-sitter: Six-month maximum with mandatory immigration consequences.** State X charges a lawful permanent resident with a misdemeanor carrying a six-month maximum. The conviction triggers automatic removal under federal immigration law and a lifetime bar on reentry. Is this "serious" for Duncan purposes? On the authorized-penalty test, six months falls right at the Baldwin line and would most likely count as petty. But the collateral consequence is catastrophic and foreseeable; Padilla v Kentucky (2010) treats such consequences as core to the criminal process for Sixth Amendment counsel purposes. A good-faith Duncan argument would press the Court to consider aggregate severity rather than only incarceration. The doctrinal answer is almost certainly no jury required (Blanton v North Las Vegas (1989) requires a specific showing of additional penalties sufficient to elevate the offense), but the fact pattern exposes Duncan's reliance on a single measurement stick (incarceration) that does not capture modern criminal-system severity.

---

## Critique

**Progressive critique.** Duncan's reformulation reads less like a principled refinement than a pragmatic repackaging. The fundamental-to-the-American-scheme test is still indeterminate: it licenses the Court to incorporate jury trial, speedy trial, confrontation, and compulsory process while refusing to incorporate grand jury indictment, civil jury, and (historically) unanimous verdicts, all of which have identical Anglo-American pedigrees. The result is a two-tier Bill of Rights where the unincorporated rights are doctrinally orphaned not because they fail any coherent test but because the Court declined to extend them. Critics in the Amar tradition (Akhil Reed Amar, The Bill of Rights: Creation and Reconstruction (1998)) argue that refined incorporation tied to Fourteenth Amendment original meaning would either incorporate all the jury-related rights or treat them uniformly; Duncan's selective approach produces the worst of both worlds and entrenches the Palko-era Court's power to control the pace of rights application.

**Originalist critique.** The Thomas McDonald-style objection runs deeper than the progressive one: the entire apparatus of selective incorporation through Due Process is textually incoherent. "Process" describes procedure, not substance; incorporation makes substantive guarantees (jury trial, speech, the like) binding on the states and therefore cannot sit comfortably in a Due Process Clause. The correct vehicle is the Privileges or Immunities Clause, whose original public meaning at Reconstruction plainly encompassed the enumerated Bill of Rights guarantees. Duncan's fundamental-to-American-scheme formulation compounds the problem by substituting a natural-law standard for the Clause-based text: the Court asks whether a right is deeply embedded in our tradition, which is precisely the standard that produces substantive-due-process indeterminacy. Black's concurrence partially captures this critique (Privileges or Immunities as vehicle) but reads the Clause as requiring total incorporation across the board; Thomas and Gorsuch in McDonald and Timbs narrow the critique to the Clause-selection point without insisting on total incorporation as a matter of Fourteenth Amendment original meaning.

## Key Points

- Incorporates the Sixth Amendment jury-trial right against the states through the Fourteenth Amendment Due Process Clause.
- Reformulates Palko's implicit-in-the-concept-of-ordered-liberty test into the American-scheme-of-justice standard, anchoring the inquiry in concrete Anglo-American legal tradition.
- Holds that an offense punishable by up to two years in prison is serious, not petty, and therefore triggers the jury guarantee; declines to fix the precise line below that.
- Establishes that incorporated rights apply with full federal force against the states (no watered-down state versions), a principle later reaffirmed in McDonald and Timbs.
- Black's concurrence previews the Thomas McDonald position: the correct vehicle is Privileges or Immunities, and total incorporation is historically warranted.
- Harlan's dissent preserves the old Palko view that due process imposes an independent fundamental-fairness test that does not mechanically mirror the Bill of Rights.

- **Doctrinal contrast with Palko.** Palko (1937) frames the inquiry philosophically: is the right implicit in the concept of ordered liberty? The reformulation matters because it shifts the Court's evidence base from abstract moral reasoning toward concrete Anglo-American legal tradition, giving lower courts a more tractable test and (in practice) speeding the pace of incorporation. By 1972 the Court would have incorporated virtually every procedural criminal-process right under Duncan's test, a result Palko's formulation had blocked for three decades. The cost is the one Harlan identifies: Duncan's mechanical-incorporation result flattens state procedural variation in a way Palko's flexible test preserved.
