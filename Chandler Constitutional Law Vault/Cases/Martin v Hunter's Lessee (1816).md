# Martin v Hunter's Lessee (1816)

## Memory Jogger

> Martin is Marbury's twin at the federal-state seam. The Virginia Court of Appeals refused to obey a Supreme Court mandate and declared Section 25 of the Judiciary Act of 1789, which authorized Supreme Court appellate review of state-court decisions on federal questions, unconstitutional. Justice Story, writing for the Court (Marshall was recused because of a personal interest in the underlying land dispute), held that Section 25 is constitutional: Article III extends the judicial power of the United States to cases arising under the Constitution, treaties, and laws of the United States, and it is the case and not the court that gives jurisdiction. The decision cements Supreme Court supremacy over state courts on federal-law questions and establishes the structural premise for every later incorporation and supremacy ruling.

---

## Facts

The dispute arose from the complicated title of the Northern Neck of Virginia, a 5.2-million-acre land grant that passed from the Fairfax family, Virginia loyalists, through a pre-Revolution British grant. During the Revolution, Virginia passed acts confiscating Loyalist lands and purporting to seize the Fairfax interest. After the war, Denny Martin (an heir of Lord Fairfax, a British subject) claimed title under the Fairfax grant. David Hunter claimed title under a Virginia state grant made after the purported confiscation. The Treaty of Paris (1783) and the Jay Treaty (1794) protected British-subject landholders against confiscation by the several states.

Hunter brought ejectment against Martin in Virginia state court. The Virginia Court of Appeals ruled for Hunter, giving effect to the state confiscation statute. Martin appealed to the United States Supreme Court under Section 25 of the Judiciary Act of 1789, which authorized Supreme Court review by writ of error of state-court judgments that held federal treaties, statutes, or constitutional provisions invalid or that denied a right asserted under them. The Supreme Court, in Fairfax's Devisee v. Hunter's Lessee (1813), ruled for Martin and remanded with a mandate directing the Virginia Court of Appeals to enter judgment accordingly.

The Virginia Court of Appeals refused. In an opinion written by Judge Spencer Roane, the Virginia court held that Section 25 was unconstitutional, that the Supreme Court had no appellate jurisdiction over state courts, and that the compact theory of the Constitution meant state courts were equal sovereigns whose judgments on federal-law questions could not be revised by any federal tribunal. The Supreme Court took the case on a second writ of error to address that refusal and the constitutional challenge to Section 25.

---

## Procedural History

Ejectment action in Virginia state court. Virginia District Court judgment for Martin. Virginia Court of Appeals reversed for Hunter on the ground that state confiscation had vested title before any treaty protection attached. Supreme Court reversed in Fairfax's Devisee v Hunter's Lessee (1813), ruling the treaties protected Martin and remanding with mandate. Virginia Court of Appeals refused the mandate, declaring Section 25 of the Judiciary Act of 1789 unconstitutional. Supreme Court granted a second writ of error to review Virginia's refusal and the validity of Section 25.

## Holding

The appellate power of the United States extends to cases pending in the state courts. Section 25 of the Judiciary Act of 1789, which authorizes Supreme Court review of state-court judgments on federal-law questions by writ of error, is supported by the letter and spirit of the Constitution. Article III's judicial power extends to all cases arising under the Constitution, laws, and treaties of the United States, and it is the case and not the court that gives jurisdiction: once a case falls within the judicial power, the tribunal in which it originates does not remove it from federal appellate reach. The Supremacy Clause and the need for uniformity of federal law confirm that state-court determinations of federal questions are subject to Supreme Court review. The Virginia Court of Appeals' refusal of the mandate is overridden; Section 25 is constitutional.

---

## Analysis

### Majority (Justice Story)

Story's structural argument proceeds from Article III's text and the Supremacy Clause. Article III grants the judicial power to the Supreme Court and such inferior courts as Congress may ordain, and that power extends to enumerated categories of cases, most importantly cases arising under the Constitution, laws, and treaties of the United States. It is the case, not the court, that gives jurisdiction. If the judicial power reaches the case, the Constitution contains no qualification as to the tribunal in which the case originates. To read Article III as excluding cases that begin in state courts would be to let state courts insulate federal-law questions from federal appellate review, which the Supremacy Clause forbids and which would fracture federal law into as many interpretations as there are state supreme courts. The Framers anticipated that state attachments, state prejudices, and state interests might obstruct the regular administration of justice on federal questions; Supreme Court appellate supervision is the constitutional solution. Section 25 is therefore a permissible exercise of Congress's authority to regulate the Court's appellate jurisdiction.

### Concurrence

**Johnson, J.** Agrees with the result and with Section 25's constitutionality but would reach it through a narrower structural argument. Where Story's majority rests on the broad case-not-court principle (Article III's enumerated categories of federal cases carry federal appellate jurisdiction into whichever tribunal they originate in), Johnson emphasizes the more modest uniformity-of-federal-law rationale: the Supreme Court must be able to review state-court interpretations of federal law, not because state courts are structurally subordinate in some general sense, but because the federal system would fracture into as many readings of federal law as there are state supreme courts if state judgments on federal questions were unreviewable. The practical import is similar: Section 25 is constitutional, and state-court federal-law rulings are subject to Supreme Court review. But Johnson's framing leaves more room for state-court coordinate dignity on state-law matters and is accordingly thought by modern commentators to be more palatable to compact-theory skeptics than Story's more assertive structural account. Johnson's concurrence has also drawn scholarly attention because it foreshadows the modern adequate-and-independent-state-ground doctrine: if the state-court judgment rests on state-law grounds sufficient to sustain it, there is no federal question for the Supreme Court to review, and the uniformity-of-federal-law rationale does not compel appellate interference.

### Dissent

No formal dissent on the bench. The argument Story and Johnson refute is the Virginia Court of Appeals opinion of Judge Spencer Roane, which is treated by commentators as the doctrinal "dissent" against which Story writes.

Roane's compact-theory argument proceeds in three steps. First, the Constitution is a compact among sovereign states, not an instrument establishing a unitary national government that subordinates state institutions. Second, state courts are coordinate rather than subordinate tribunals: each state's judicial department exercises the state's own sovereign judicial power, and the Supremacy Clause binds state judges (Article VI, clause 2) but does not authorize federal appellate supervision of state courts. Third, Section 25 of the Judiciary Act of 1789 is therefore unconstitutional because it subordinates state courts to a federal appellate tribunal in violation of that coordinate status. Roane's proposed remedy for state-court errors on federal law is political rather than judicial: constitutional amendment, elections, or, in the most assertive strand of the tradition, state nullification.

Doctrinal lineage: Roane's argument is continuous with the Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions of 1798 (Madison and Jefferson), which asserted that states retain the power to resist federal overreach, and it feeds forward into the nullification crisis of the 1830s (Calhoun) and into Confederate-era secession arguments. The Marshall Court's rejection of compact theory in Martin and then in Cohens v Virginia (1821) did not extinguish it as a political tradition; it simply settled the question as a matter of federal judicial doctrine.

Scholarly note: the modern adequate-and-independent-state-ground doctrine is in one sense a concession to Roane. Federal appellate review of state-court judgments is limited to the federal question, and where a state-court judgment rests on adequate and independent state-law grounds, Supreme Court review is unavailable. That doctrine preserves the space for state-law autonomy that Roane's compact theory sought to protect, without yielding the federal-question ground Story secured.

## Hypothetical Applications

**Hypothetical 1 (same-side, state judgment contradicts federal treaty).** A state supreme court upholds a state statute taxing imports from a country with which the United States has a most-favored-nation trade treaty. The losing party seeks Supreme Court review under Section 25. Under Martin, the federal-question element (treaty interpretation) gives the Supreme Court appellate jurisdiction, and the case-not-court principle forecloses the argument that the state-court origin insulates the judgment. Result: Supreme Court review lies.

**Hypothetical 2 (same-side, state-court Fourteenth Amendment interpretation at odds with precedent).** A state supreme court reads the Fourteenth Amendment's Equal Protection Clause to permit a classification the Supreme Court has previously held suspect. The losing party petitions for certiorari on the federal constitutional question. Martin's holding applies directly: the state-court judgment's interpretation of the Fourteenth Amendment is a federal question subject to Supreme Court appellate review. Result: jurisdiction lies, and on the merits the Supreme Court is free to reverse.

**Hypothetical 3 (opposite-side, purely state-law property dispute).** A state supreme court resolves a boundary dispute under a state adverse-possession statute with no federal element. The losing party invokes Martin to press Supreme Court review. Martin's holding is framed around federal questions (cases "arising under the Constitution, laws, and treaties of the United States"). A pure state-law question falls outside Article III's enumerated categories, so neither Section 25 nor Martin's case-not-court reasoning reaches it. Result: no federal appellate jurisdiction.

**Hypothetical 4 (opposite-side, interlocutory posture).** A state trial court issues a pretrial evidentiary ruling implicating the Fourth Amendment. The losing party attempts to invoke Martin for immediate Supreme Court review before the state appellate courts rule. Martin addresses final judgments from state courts of last resort; interlocutory state-court rulings do not satisfy the finality requirement implicit in Section 25 and its successors. Result: no jurisdiction, without prejudice to a later appeal once the state courts' judgment is final.

**Hypothetical 5 (fence-sitter, adequate and independent state ground).** A state supreme court rules for defendant on both a state constitutional ground and a federal constitutional ground, either of which is independently sufficient to sustain the judgment. Prosecution seeks Supreme Court review on the federal ground. The modern adequate-and-independent-state-ground doctrine limits Martin's reach: if the state ground is adequate to sustain the judgment and independent of the federal ground, the Supreme Court's reversal of the federal holding would not change the judgment, so the Court declines review. Result turns on whether the state court's reliance on the state ground is "adequate" (sufficient to sustain the judgment on its own) and "independent" (not interwoven with federal law); this fact-specific determination is the modern workhorse limitation on Martin.

## Critique

**Progressive/structural critique.** Martin is a structural necessity. Without Supreme Court appellate review of state-court federal rulings, federal law would fracture into as many readings as there are state supreme courts, and the Supremacy Clause would be an empty proclamation. Every incorporation case, every preemption case, and every dormant-commerce-clause ruling rests silently on the Martin premise. Progressive-minded commentators also note that Martin was, in context, a ruling against state judicial obstruction of federal treaty obligations (the Treaty of Paris and the Jay Treaty protections for Loyalist property), which is a structural precedent that later incorporation and civil-rights cases quietly depended on.

**Originalist/compact-theory critique.** The arguments Judge Spencer Roane pressed in the Virginia Court of Appeals were not frivolous. Compact theory was a serious strand of Founding-era constitutional thought, reflected in the Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions and in sustained sectional political tradition. Story's case-not-court reasoning papers over a genuine textual ambiguity: Article III extends the judicial power "to all cases, in law and equity, arising under this Constitution, the laws of the United States, and treaties," but does not explicitly address whether that extension reaches cases that originate in state courts. The Madisonian reading (judicial power reaches the case regardless of origin) won, and is almost certainly correct as a matter of constitutional structure, but Martin is not the textually self-evident result its reputation sometimes suggests.

**Scholarly critique.** The modern adequate-and-independent-state-ground doctrine is the necessary complement to Martin. It softens Martin's reach by preventing federal appellate interference with state judgments that rest on independently sufficient state-law grounds, while preserving the federal-question principle at Martin's core. Without that limiting doctrine, Martin's reach would be both doctrinally cleaner and practically more intrusive; with it, the federal system tolerates a wide zone of state-law autonomy alongside federal-question supremacy. The Hart and Wechsler tradition treats this two-step arrangement (Martin plus adequate-and-independent-state-ground) as the structural architecture of federal-state judicial relations.

**Contingency note.** Marshall's recusal matters more than the conventional telling allows. Because Marshall's brother held a contract to purchase part of the Fairfax lands, the Chief Justice could not sit, and Story had to write the opinion. Modern commentators read Story's Martin as the fullest Marshall-era statement of federalism structural reasoning delivered by a non-Marshall voice, which is doctrinally useful: Martin's premises cannot be dismissed as idiosyncratic to Marshall, and they recur in broadly Madisonian and Washingtonian terms in Story's text.

## Key Points

- Establishes Supreme Court appellate jurisdiction over state-court judgments on federal-law questions and upholds Section 25 of the Judiciary Act of 1789.
- Story's case-not-court principle: Article III extends the judicial power to categories of cases, and the tribunal of origin does not remove a federal case from federal appellate reach.
- The Supremacy Clause and the need for uniformity of federal law together require Supreme Court review of state-court federal-law rulings.
- Rejects the compact theory (as articulated by Virginia's Judge Spencer Roane) that state courts are coordinate sovereigns immune from federal appellate revision.
- Marshall's recusal underscores Story's independent authorship and makes Martin a rare chance to see Marshall-era federalism arguments made by another voice.
- Structural predicate for every later incorporation, preemption, and dormant-commerce case; without Martin, Marbury would reach only federal-court judgments and state-court federal-law rulings would be insulated.
- The modern adequate-and-independent-state-ground doctrine is the limiting principle that preserves state-law autonomy while leaving Martin's federal-question holding intact.

## Connections

**Doctrinal families:** [[Topics/Judicial Review]], [[Topics/Federalism|Federalism]], [[Supremacy Clause]], [[Article III]], [[Supreme Court Appellate Jurisdiction]], [[Topics/Separation of Powers]]. The [[Topics/Federalism|Federalism topic page]] treats Martin as the judicial-federal-supremacy anchor alongside McCulloch's legislative-federal-supremacy face.

**Prior cases:** [[Cases/Marbury v Madison (1803)]] supplied the foundational commitment to judicial review of federal action and Article III supremacy as against Congress. [[Cases/Fairfax's Devisee v Hunter's Lessee (1813)]] is the direct procedural antecedent: Story's Martin opinion reviews Virginia's refusal of the Fairfax's Devisee mandate.

**Contemporary companions:** [[Cases/McCulloch v Maryland (1819)]] supplies the Marshall-era federalism complement on the legislative side (Necessary and Proper Clause plus Supremacy Clause); Martin is its judicial-branch counterpart. [[Cases/Cohens v Virginia (1821)]] extends Martin's federal-question appellate jurisdiction from civil to criminal state-court judgments, and is sometimes called Martin's "criminal docket" companion.

**Forward connections.** Every incorporation case (Palko, Duncan, McDonald, Timbs) depends on Martin: without Supreme Court appellate review of state-court federal-constitutional rulings, the Fourteenth Amendment would operate only through original federal-court actions, which is not how incorporation has ever functioned. Dormant commerce clause, preemption, and civil-rights cases all rest on the same premise. The modern adequate-and-independent-state-ground doctrine (Michigan v. Long (1983) is the doctrinal fulcrum) is the necessary limiting principle on Martin's reach. The [[Topics/Commerce Clause|Commerce Clause Topic]] treats Martin as the structural predicate of Commerce Clause doctrine: without SCOTUS appellate authority over state-court federal-question judgments, a Commerce Clause decision such as Gibbons or NFIB could not bind state courts uniformly, and Commerce Clause law would fragment into fifty state readings.

**Contrast on the two judicial-review foundations.** Marbury addresses Article III as against an Act of Congress: the Court will decline to enforce a statute that conflicts with Article III. Martin addresses Article III as against state sovereignty: the Court will review a state-court judgment that conflicts with federal law. Together the two cases establish the outward-facing faces of Article III judicial power, one facing the political branches of the federal government, the other facing the coordinate states. Every modern separation-of-powers and federalism case operates in the architecture these two opinions set up.
