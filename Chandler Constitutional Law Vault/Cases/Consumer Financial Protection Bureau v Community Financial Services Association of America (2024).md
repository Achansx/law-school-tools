# Consumer Financial Protection Bureau v Community Financial Services Association of America (2024)

## Memory Jogger

> The CFPB funding case: the Appropriations Clause requires only that Congress enact a law designating a source and purpose for spending -- no annual cycle, no time limit, and no fixed nominal amount is constitutionally required -- so the CFPB's standing draw on Federal Reserve earnings satisfies the Clause.

---

## Facts

The Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act of 2010 created the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) and gave it an unusual funding structure. Rather than receiving discretionary appropriations through the annual congressional budget process, the CFPB is authorized by statute to draw funds from the earnings of the Federal Reserve System, up to a statutory cap, for the purposes of carrying out its consumer-protection mandate. Congress does not need to pass a new appropriation each year for the CFPB to operate; the standing statutory authorization constitutes its funding authority.

The CFPB used this funding to promulgate a 2017 payday lending rule restricting certain short-term lending practices. The Community Financial Services Association of America (CFSA), a payday lender trade group, challenged the rule and argued that the CFPB's entire funding structure violated the Appropriations Clause of Article I, Section 9: "No Money shall be drawn from the Treasury, but in Consequence of Appropriations made by Law." The Fifth Circuit agreed, holding that the CFPB's funding mechanism was facially unconstitutional because it insulated the Bureau from the congressional power of the purse by allowing it to self-fund without annual legislative approval.

---

## Procedural History

The CFSA challenged the payday lending rule in the Northern District of Texas, which upheld the CFPB's funding structure. A Fifth Circuit panel reversed in a widely criticized decision, holding the mechanism facially unconstitutional -- the first appellate court to invalidate a federal agency's funding structure on Appropriations Clause grounds in decades. The Supreme Court granted certiorari and reversed 7-2.

## Holding

The CFPB's funding mechanism -- a standing statutory authorization to draw from Federal Reserve earnings up to a cap, for designated consumer-protection purposes -- satisfies the Appropriations Clause. An appropriation "made by Law" requires only: (1) a statutory enactment, (2) identifying a specified source of public money, (3) for designated purposes. The Clause does not mandate annual appropriations, impose a time limit, or require a fixed nominal amount. Historical practice since the First Congress confirms that permanent, indefinite appropriations are constitutional.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Justice Thomas wrote for seven Justices. The majority began with textual analysis: the Appropriations Clause requires that money be drawn "in Consequence of Appropriations made by Law." An "appropriation made by Law" is, at its core, a statutory enactment that authorizes expenditures from a specified source for designated purposes. Nothing in the text requires the appropriation to be time-limited, subject to annual renewal, or confined to the Treasury's general fund. The Federal Reserve's earnings are "public money" within the meaning of the Clause because they flow from a government-chartered institution operating under federal law.

Thomas then marshaled historical evidence. The First Congress -- sitting contemporaneously with the Constitutional Convention -- enacted numerous permanent, standing appropriations: for military pensions, Post Office operations, and revenue-sharing with the states. These were not time-limited or subject to annual review. That practice continued through the nineteenth century without serious constitutional objection. This history, Thomas concluded, powerfully confirms that the Clause was never understood to mandate the annual appropriations cycle that modern practice has made the norm.

The majority acknowledged the structural concern animating the CFSA's challenge: an agency that does not depend on annual appropriations is shielded from one of Congress's most potent oversight tools. But Thomas held this is a policy problem, not a constitutional defect. Congress created the CFPB's funding structure; Congress retains the power to change it. The Constitution requires that spending be authorized by law -- it does not dictate what form that law must take.
