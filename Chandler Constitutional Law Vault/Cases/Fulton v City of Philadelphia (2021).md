# Fulton v City of Philadelphia (2021)

## Memory Jogger

> A unanimous Court held that Philadelphia's refusal to contract with Catholic Social Services for foster care placements -- because CSS would not certify same-sex couples as foster parents on religious grounds -- violated the Free Exercise Clause, because the City's anti-discrimination contract contained a discretionary exemption mechanism, making the policy not "generally applicable" under Employment Division v Smith and therefore subject to strict scrutiny.

---

## Facts

Catholic Social Services (CSS), a foster-care agency affiliated with the Archdiocese of Philadelphia, contracts with the City of Philadelphia to certify prospective foster families. CSS's religious beliefs prohibit it from certifying same-sex married couples as foster parents, as its understanding of marriage excludes same-sex unions. Philadelphia's standard foster-care contract required contractors to comply with the City's non-discrimination Fair Practices Ordinance. When the City learned of CSS's policy after a news story, it suspended new foster-care referrals to CSS and ultimately declined to renew the contract unless CSS agreed to certify same-sex couples. CSS sued, arguing the City's condition violated the Free Exercise Clause of the First Amendment. The City defended on the grounds that its non-discrimination requirement was a neutral, generally applicable law under Employment Division v Smith (1990), which does not require religious exemptions.

---

## Procedural History

The district court denied CSS's motion for a preliminary injunction. The Third Circuit affirmed, holding that the City's non-discrimination requirement was neutral and generally applicable under Smith and therefore required no religious exemption. The Supreme Court granted certiorari and reversed 9-0, though the Justices split significantly over the reasoning.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority:** Roberts (joined by Breyer, Sotomayor, Kagan, Kavanaugh, and Barrett) -- contract's discretionary exemption clause makes policy non-generally-applicable; strict scrutiny applies; City fails strict scrutiny
- **Concurrence (Barrett, joined by Kavanaugh; Breyer joins in part):** Acknowledges uncertainty about whether Smith should be reconsidered; declines to reconsider it here because the case can be resolved on narrow grounds
- **Concurrence (Alito, joined by Thomas and Gorsuch):** Would overrule Smith; lengthy historical and doctrinal critique of Smith's departure from pre-1990 Free Exercise doctrine
- **Concurrence (Gorsuch, joined by Thomas and Alito):** Argues the majority's narrow holding invites gamesmanship; Smith should be reconsidered now

---

## Holding

Philadelphia's refusal to contract with CSS violated the Free Exercise Clause. The City's anti-discrimination policy was not "generally applicable" under Smith because it gave the Commissioner of Human Services discretionary authority to grant exemptions to the non-discrimination requirement. A law that creates a mechanism for individualized exemptions is subject to strict scrutiny when applied to religious conduct. Under strict scrutiny, the City's interest in preventing discrimination did not justify its refusal to grant CSS an exemption, because the City had already granted secular contractors exemptions and had not applied the non-discrimination requirement to the few placements CSS made in any given year.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Chief Justice Roberts's majority opinion is deliberately narrow, resolving the case on the specific text of the City's foster-care contract rather than reconsidering Employment Division v Smith. The key textual pivot: the contract gave the Commissioner of Human Services discretion to "grant exceptions to this contract." Under Smith, a law is subject only to rational basis review if it is "neutral" (not targeting religion) and "generally applicable" (not selectively imposing burdens on religious conduct). But Roberts held that a law is not generally applicable if it creates a mechanism for individualized exemptions -- because in that case the government is already inviting consideration of particular reasons for non-compliance, and religious reasons must be treated at least as well as secular ones.

Applying strict scrutiny, the majority found the City's interest insufficient. The non-discrimination interest was undercut by the City's own past exemption practices, the minuscule number of foster placements CSS handled, and the City's failure to demonstrate that refusing an exemption to CSS was narrowly tailored to any concrete harm.

The "mechanism for individualized exemptions" rule comes from the Sherbert-Lukumi line and from Employment Division v Smith itself. Smith acknowledged that where a state has created a system for granting individual exemptions from a general rule, it could not then deny an exemption to a religious claimant without justifying the refusal by reference to the state's interest in denying the exemption. That carve-out was treated as a narrow safety valve for thirty years. Chief Justice Roberts used it to decide Fulton. Philadelphia's foster-care contract contained Section 3.21, which gave the Commissioner of the Department of Human Services discretion to "grant exceptions to this contract" in her "sole discretion." The clause did not name religion. It did not set out criteria. It simply told anyone reading the contract that individual waivers were available if the Commissioner chose to grant them. Once such a mechanism exists, Roberts held, the rule is not generally applicable within the meaning of Smith and strict scrutiny applies when the government refuses an exemption to a religious claimant.

That conclusion was a significant narrowing of Smith without overruling it. Smith had left "generally applicable" a fuzzy term; Fulton gave it an operational definition that will often dispose of cases at the threshold. When a government contract, ordinance, or licensing scheme contains any discretionary waiver authority, the presumption shifts. The government must show either that it has denied the religious exemption for a reason of the highest order or that the waiver mechanism does not in fact exist in the form the text suggests. The majority did not address what happens when the waiver clause is invoked only rarely or is formal rather than substantive, leaving those questions for lower courts.

Roberts distinguished a categorical rule from the Philadelphia contract by pointing to what the contract did not say. It did not say "no exceptions." It did not tie exceptions to objective criteria. It gave a single official a personal power of dispensation. That feature alone was enough. The majority was careful not to describe its holding as a global rule about anti-discrimination policies. It is about Philadelphia's specific contract text.

The deliberate narrowness is why the Court did not reach the Smith overrule question. Three Justices (Alito, Thomas, Gorsuch) wrote separately to press that question. Three others (Barrett, Kavanaugh, Breyer) wrote a concurrence raising their own doubts about Smith but concluding the case did not require its reconsideration. The result is a 9-0 judgment on a decidedly narrow textual peg.

Whether the majority offers workable guidance is a point of active disagreement in lower courts and in legal academia. On one hand, the rule is easy to apply: if there is a discretionary exemption clause, scrutiny is heightened. On the other hand, the rule invites drafters to simply remove such clauses from future contracts and ordinances, which would effectively allow governments to foreclose Fulton's path to strict scrutiny by boilerplate revision. Justice Gorsuch's concurrence pressed this point sharply. Whether lower courts will read Fulton's rule broadly enough to catch functional equivalents of discretionary exemption clauses, or narrowly enough to require the specific clause Philadelphia had, will determine how much the decision actually narrows Smith in practice.

### Concurrence (Alito)

Justice Alito's concurrence in the judgment, joined by Justices Thomas and Gorsuch, runs to 77 pages and is really a full opinion making the case that Smith should be overruled. Alito marches through the history of Free Exercise doctrine from the Founding to 1990, arguing that the Court's pre-Smith cases consistently held that facially neutral laws could burden religious practice only if the government had a compelling interest in applying the law to the religious claimant. The Sherbert v Verner (1963) balancing test, in Alito's telling, was the natural application of a doctrine that went back to Reynolds v United States (1879) and that treated religious exemption claims as constitutional questions rather than matters of legislative grace.

Alito argues that Smith was a sudden and unjustified departure from that tradition. Justice Scalia's opinion in Smith, he notes, rewrote the Free Exercise Clause to mean only what "neutral, generally applicable" law allowed it to mean. It set aside a body of precedent without explicitly overruling it. And it produced thirty years of unreliable application because "neutral" and "generally applicable" are deeply contested terms in practice. The concurrence cites Justice O'Connor's Smith concurrence, which rejected the majority's rule and stuck with Sherbert balancing, as an early signal that the Court's Free Exercise jurisprudence was unstable.

Why now? Alito's answer is that Fulton is the right vehicle because both parties briefed the Smith question, because the ten Justices who have voted in Smith-era Free Exercise cases have produced inconsistent outcomes, and because the Court's narrow textual ground in the majority opinion invites continued gamesmanship by governments that will write their exemption clauses differently in future. The concurrence argues that leaving Smith on the books guarantees a steady stream of cases where the Court must parse contract language to decide whether religion is burdened, which is precisely the inquiry Sherbert balancing was designed to provide an answer to.

The textual and historical arguments are forceful, and Alito's review of the Founding-era debates about free exercise is extensive. The strongest objection is one Alito does not fully meet: the pre-Smith case law he relies on is itself doctrinally thinner than he suggests. Sherbert was an unemployment-compensation case with narrow facts. Wisconsin v Yoder was decided on a mix of religious-exemption and parental-rights grounds. The Court's mid-twentieth-century Free Exercise holdings were often dicta in cases decided on other grounds. Smith may have been a sharp break from rhetoric, but the historical pre-Smith record of actual exemption-granting is mixed. The concurrence reads best as a normative argument for what Free Exercise doctrine should be rather than as a definitive account of what it always was. Nonetheless, the six Justices who express some Smith skepticism on this record suggest that Smith's days as settled doctrine may be numbered.

### Concurrence (Gorsuch)

Justice Gorsuch's concurrence in the judgment, joined by Thomas and Alito, is more concise than Alito's but sharper in one respect. Gorsuch's central complaint is practical. The majority's narrow textual approach, he argues, is a roadmap for governments to defeat religious-liberty claims by simply deleting discretionary exemption clauses from their contracts. Once Philadelphia's foster-care contract is revised to read "no exceptions," the religious claimant loses because Fulton will no longer apply. A rule that depends on the presence of a particular word in a particular contract is not really a constitutional rule. It is, Gorsuch writes, an invitation to drafting around the Free Exercise Clause.

Gorsuch agrees with Alito that the right answer is to overrule Smith. His additional point is that the Court cannot avoid that question for much longer. Each attempt to resolve a Free Exercise case on narrow textual grounds produces guidance that the next government's lawyers will use to evade review. The result will be a steady stream of Free Exercise cases arriving at the Court and a steady erosion of religious liberty claims in the interim as governments learn which drafting patterns escape strict scrutiny under Fulton's rule.

The textualist connection is worth noting. Gorsuch, who authored Bostock v Clayton County (2020) reading "sex" in Title VII to cover sexual orientation and gender identity on a textualist theory, approaches Fulton with the same tool in the other direction. If the text of the Free Exercise Clause means something, its protection cannot turn on whether a city includes or omits a particular contract clause. The textual methodology that reached a liberal-friendly result in Bostock reaches what conservatives often treat as a conservative-friendly result in Fulton. Gorsuch is consistent across both: the text does the work, not the contingent vocabulary of the legislative or administrative instrument at issue.

The Gorsuch concurrence is doctrinally the sharpest of the separate opinions because it forecasts not just what the Court should do but what governments will do next. Whether the prediction proves correct will depend on how lower courts apply Fulton in ordinances that lack the specific discretionary-exemption clause the majority emphasized.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

*State Contract with Optional Waiver Clause.* A state contracts with private charities to operate homeless shelters and includes a non-discrimination requirement covering sexual orientation, along with a clause giving the state director discretion to waive any contract provision in "exceptional circumstances." A religious charity that serves only men based on its interpretation of Scripture applies for a waiver, which the state refuses. Under Fulton, strict scrutiny applies. The discretionary waiver mechanism puts the contract outside Smith's general-applicability safe harbor. The state must show a compelling interest in denying this particular exemption, not merely a legitimate interest in the general non-discrimination rule.

*Municipal Licensing with Discretionary Exception Authority.* A city requires all food-service vendors to obtain a license with a clause prohibiting the sale of any meat slaughter method other than those the health department approves. The health department retains discretion to grant exceptions on a case-by-case basis. A religious group that requires shechita slaughter seeks an exception, which the department refuses. Under Fulton, strict scrutiny applies because the licensing scheme contains a discretionary exemption authority. The department's refusal must survive compelling-interest review on the specific question of denying shechita rather than on the generalized interest in slaughter regulation.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

*Categorical Ban on Peyote Without Exemptions.* A state enacts a comprehensive drug-control statute banning the possession of certain hallucinogens. The statute does not provide for individualized exceptions. A member of the Native American Church seeks an exception to use peyote in religious ceremonies. Under Smith as not narrowed by Fulton, the categorical rule is neutral, generally applicable, and unchallengeable under the Free Exercise Clause (this is effectively the Smith facts themselves). The absence of a discretionary exemption clause keeps Fulton's rule out of play.

*Flat No-Exception Anti-Discrimination Rule in Public Accommodations.* A state enacts a public-accommodations statute prohibiting sexual-orientation discrimination and expressly forecloses any exemptions: "No person or entity, regardless of motive, shall be exempt from the requirements of this section." A religiously motivated wedding vendor challenges the law. Without a discretionary exemption clause, Fulton's narrow rule does not trigger. The claim would have to rely on Masterpiece Cakeshop / 303 Creative-style reasoning or on arguments to overrule Smith, not on Fulton's individualized-exemption hook.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

*Secular-Hardship-Only Exemption Clause.* A state contract with private foster-care agencies includes a non-discrimination rule covering sexual orientation and a narrow exemption clause that reads: "The Department may grant limited exceptions to this non-discrimination requirement upon documentation of severe administrative hardship." Religious hardship is not mentioned. A Catholic agency requests an exception on religious grounds; the department refuses. Under Fulton, does the exemption clause count as an "individualized exemption" mechanism triggering strict scrutiny, even though it facially restricts the category of exemption-eligible hardships to secular administrative issues? Roberts's majority opinion does not directly address a religion-neutral but substantively narrow exemption clause. The Alito / Gorsuch concurrences would treat this as a transparent workaround and apply strict scrutiny. The Barrett concurrence might read Fulton's rule more narrowly and require more than a narrow hardship clause to trigger heightened scrutiny. This is exactly the kind of case where governments are likely to test Fulton's margins.

## Critique

*LGBTQ-rights critique.* From an LGBTQ-rights perspective, Fulton permits the City of Philadelphia to fund, through contract, the certification of foster parents by an agency that refuses to certify same-sex married couples. The rule that government money can flow to a certifying body that categorically excludes a class of eligible parents on religious grounds is a step backward from the anti-discrimination norms embodied in Obergefell v Hodges and in federal funding non-discrimination clauses. The decision's narrow reasoning does not soften that effect for the same-sex couples who, in practice, must travel to a different agency and bear the dignitary cost of being formally excluded by a city-funded provider. Whether the rule's narrowness will be contained in future cases or will expand into broader religious exemptions in public accommodations, employment, and housing is an open question that critics treat with alarm.

*Religious-liberty critique.* From a religious-liberty perspective, the majority's narrow opinion is a win that is not wide enough. For three decades after Smith, minority religious groups lost Free Exercise claims under a rule that treated any facially neutral law as unchallengeable regardless of the burden on religious practice. The results included denials of exemptions for Sikh officers wearing turbans, Native American hair-length requirements, and Hasidic employers' scheduling needs. Fulton rescued one religious claimant through a textual hook tied to Philadelphia's specific contract language. The rule does not help a minority religion confronting a categorical ordinance with no exemption clause. The Alito / Gorsuch critique that only an overrule of Smith can restore the pre-1990 balancing approach has independent force from this side of the question.

*Doctrinal critique.* The most common academic critique of Fulton is that its "discretionary exemption clause" test is a rule without a principled stopping point. It treats a contract with a single boilerplate waiver clause as constitutionally different from a contract without one, even though the practical religious burden on the claimant is identical. It invites governments to delete such clauses from future contracts, thereby immunizing identical substantive policies from Free Exercise challenge. And it offers no guidance about whether the rule covers implicit exemption authorities (inherent prosecutorial discretion, for example) or applies only to textually explicit ones. Justice Gorsuch's concurrence called this out; Justice Barrett's concurrence acknowledged the difficulty. The academic consensus is that Fulton is transitional rather than stable: it narrows Smith in a way that will require further clarification and likely further doctrinal redrawing in cases to come.

## Key Points

- Unanimous 9-0 judgment on narrow textual grounds. The Court declined to reconsider Employment Division v Smith (1990) despite six Justices expressing doubts about it across the separate opinions.
- Operational rule: a law with a discretionary individualized-exemption mechanism is not "generally applicable" under Smith. Strict scrutiny applies when the government refuses to extend such a mechanism to a religious claimant.
- Doctrinal pedigree traces to the Sherbert-Lukumi line. Smith itself acknowledged a narrow carve-out for individualized-exemption systems; Fulton promotes that carve-out into an operational test.
- Textual hook was Section 3.21 of Philadelphia's foster-care contract giving the Commissioner "sole discretion" to grant exceptions. The clause did not name religion, did not set out criteria, and empowered a single official to dispense waivers.
- Three-way concurrence split on Smith's future: Alito (joined by Thomas and Gorsuch) would overrule it and restore pre-Smith Sherbert balancing; Barrett, Kavanaugh, and Breyer flag Smith's problems but decline to reach them; Roberts's majority avoids the question entirely.
- Gorsuch's concurrence warned of drafting-around gamesmanship: a government can immunize its policies from Fulton review by deleting discretionary exemption clauses and writing "no exceptions" language instead.
- Compelling-interest analysis required denial of the specific exemption to be narrowly tailored. The City's past exemption practices and the small number of CSS foster placements undermined its asserted anti-discrimination interest as applied to CSS.
- Doctrinally transitional: Fulton narrows Smith without overruling it and invites further clarification in cases where exemption authority is implicit or functionally narrow rather than textually explicit.

## Connections

- [[Cases/Employment Division v Smith (1990)]]: The rule Fulton narrows without overruling. Smith held that neutral, generally applicable laws do not require religious exemptions. Fulton operationalizes the "generally applicable" prong to exclude contracts with discretionary exemption clauses.
- [[Cases/Church of Lukumi Babalu Aye v City of Hialeah (1993)]]: The "targeted at religion" side of Smith doctrine. Lukumi developed the individualized-exemption exception that Fulton applied. Roberts cites Lukumi's treatment of the Hialeah ordinances as a precedent establishing that mechanism-for-exemption laws trigger strict scrutiny.
- [[Cases/Sherbert v Verner (1963)]]: The pre-Smith balancing framework that Alito's concurrence argues should be restored. Sherbert itself is the origin of the "individualized exemption" concept in Free Exercise doctrine.
- [[Cases/303 Creative LLC v Elenis (2023)]]: Subsequent decision on the intersection of non-discrimination laws and First Amendment claims, though on free-speech rather than Free Exercise grounds. The two cases together define the post-Obergefell terrain on religious and expressive exemptions from anti-discrimination rules.
- [[Topics/Free Exercise of Religion]]: Topic-level context for the Smith doctrine, its exceptions, and Fulton's narrowing.
- [[Cases/Reynolds v United States (1879)|Reynolds v United States (1879)]]: the belief-conduct foundation Alito's Fulton concurrence traces the doctrinal arc back to; Alito argues that the Reynolds-to-Sherbert line supports restoring compelling-interest review, while Roberts's majority leaves Reynolds intact by narrowing Smith from within rather than overruling it. Either reading keeps Reynolds as the original authority that modern Free Exercise doctrine answers to.
