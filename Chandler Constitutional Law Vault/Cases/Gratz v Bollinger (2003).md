# Gratz v Bollinger (2003)

## Memory Jogger

> Decided the same day as Grutter, Rehnquist's majority struck down the University of Michigan's undergraduate admissions point system -- which automatically awarded 20 of the 100 points needed for admission to every underrepresented minority applicant -- because the mechanical bonus made race decisive for virtually every qualified minority applicant and precluded the individualized consideration the Constitution requires.

---

## Facts

Jennifer Gratz and Patrick Hamacher, both white Michigan residents, were denied undergraduate admission to the University of Michigan's College of Literature, Science, and the Arts. The College used a 150-point selection index to rank applicants. Demographic factors worth points included: geographic residency (up to 16 points), socioeconomic disadvantage (20 points), alumni relationship (4 points), and -- at issue -- membership in an underrepresented racial or ethnic minority group (20 points). Because 100 points was effectively a guarantee of admission, the automatic 20-point racial bonus meant that virtually every minimally qualified underrepresented minority applicant would be admitted. The College argued the point system was a practical, administratively workable way to achieve the same diversity goal as the Law School's holistic review. Gratz and Hamacher challenged the policy under the Equal Protection Clause.

---

## Procedural History

The district court held that the College's admissions policies violated the Equal Protection Clause. The Sixth Circuit reversed in part. The Supreme Court granted certiorari and decided the case alongside Grutter v Bollinger, reversing 6-3 and holding the undergraduate point system unconstitutional.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority:** Rehnquist (joined by O'Connor, Kennedy, Scalia, and Thomas, plus Breyer on standing) -- 20-point bonus not narrowly tailored; fails strict scrutiny
- **Concurrence (O'Connor):** Distinguishes the Law School's holistic review upheld in Grutter; the automatic nature of the bonus is what dooms the program
- **Concurrence (Thomas):** Agrees with striking down the bonus; would go further and hold all race-conscious admissions unconstitutional
- **Concurrence (Breyer):** Joins on standing and the constitutional holding; writes separately on standing
- **Dissent (Stevens, joined by Souter):** Would not reach the merits for procedural reasons; Hamacher's claim was moot
- **Dissent (Souter, joined by Ginsburg):** Would uphold the program; the 20-point bonus is functionally equivalent to the kind of "diversity bonus" that Justice Powell approved in Bakke
- **Dissent (Ginsburg, joined by Souter):** Argued the point system is no more mechanical than covert racial practices that elite institutions use covertly; the Constitution does not require universities to pursue diversity through less transparent means

---

## Holding

The University of Michigan's undergraduate admissions program, which automatically awarded 20 points -- one-fifth of the 100 points needed to guarantee admission -- to every underrepresented minority applicant solely on the basis of race, violates the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. The program is not narrowly tailored because the mechanical, automatic bonus makes race decisive for virtually every minimally qualified minority applicant and fails to provide the individualized consideration that strict scrutiny requires.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Chief Justice Rehnquist's majority opinion is best understood as the companion to and limit upon Grutter. Where Grutter held that student body diversity is a compelling interest and that a genuine holistic review can be narrowly tailored, Gratz held that a mechanical numerical proxy for race cannot be narrowly tailored to that same interest.

The constitutional defect the majority identified was the automatic character of the 20-point bonus. Because the bonus applied to every underrepresented minority applicant regardless of any individual's particular background, circumstances, or actual contribution to campus diversity, it treated race not as one factor in a complex assessment but as a near-decisive classification. The majority cited Justice Powell's Bakke opinion for the proposition that race may operate as a "plus" factor in an individualized review -- but that the factor of race must not be made decisive for virtually any individual. By awarding 20 automatic points, the College made race decisive for nearly all qualified minority applicants without considering whether any particular applicant would contribute to diversity in any meaningful individual way.

The majority also rejected the argument that administrative convenience justified the mechanical approach. Echoing the reasoning in Frontiero v Richardson (1973), the Court held that the difficulty of implementing individualized review at scale cannot render constitutional a program that otherwise violates equal protection.
