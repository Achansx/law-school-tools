# Wickard v Filburn (1942)

## Memory Jogger

> Wheat grown for a farmer's own chickens can still be regulated by Congress, because in the aggregate home-consumed wheat competes with wheat in the interstate market.

---

## Facts

The Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938 authorized the Secretary of Agriculture to set national marketing quotas on wheat to stabilize prices, which had cratered during the Depression. For the 1941 crop year the Secretary apportioned Roscoe Filburn, an Ohio dairy and poultry farmer, a planting allotment of 11.1 acres, yielding 20.1 bushels per acre. Filburn sowed 23 acres and harvested 462 bushels of "excess" wheat. He intended to consume the excess on his own farm, feeding it to his livestock, using it to make flour for home use, and saving some for seed.

The Act, as amended in 1941, imposed a penalty of 49 cents per bushel on production above quota regardless of whether the wheat ever entered interstate commerce. Filburn refused to pay the penalty of $117.11 and sued to enjoin enforcement of the marketing quota.

The statutory scheme rested on findings that wheat prices were depressed by surplus and that home consumption accounted for roughly twenty percent of the wheat crop, displacing demand that would otherwise show up in the open market.

---

## Procedural History

Filburn brought suit in the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of Ohio against Secretary of Agriculture Claude Wickard, seeking to enjoin enforcement of the marketing penalty. A three-judge district court granted injunctive relief, holding that the Secretary's speech to farmers in a referendum campaign had been improper and implicitly questioning the reach of the Act. The district court did not squarely sustain the Commerce Clause challenge, but its ruling was grounded in part on a narrow reading of federal power over production.

The Secretary appealed directly to the Supreme Court. The Court heard oral argument in May 1942 and, unusually, ordered reargument in October 1942 after Justice Jackson was reassigned to the majority. Justice Jackson, writing for a unanimous Court, reversed.

- **Majority:** Jackson (joined by Stone, Roberts, Black, Reed, Frankfurter, Douglas, Murphy, Byrnes) — 9-0
- **Concurrence:** None

## Holding

Congress's Commerce Clause power extends to activity that is purely local and not itself commercial, so long as the activity, aggregated with similar conduct across the nation, exerts a substantial economic effect on interstate commerce. Wheat grown for on-farm consumption falls within that power because it displaces wheat that would otherwise be purchased in the interstate market.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Justice Jackson rejected the formalist distinction between "production" and "commerce" that had constrained earlier cases like Hammer v Dagenhart and Carter Coal. The question was not whether home-grown wheat was itself commerce but whether Congress could reach it as part of a broader regulatory scheme directed at interstate markets. Jackson stipulated that wheat consumed on the farm accounted for roughly twenty percent of total production, a quantity that, if left unregulated, would substantially undermine the price-stabilization machinery of the Agricultural Adjustment Act. He introduced what came to be called the aggregation principle: even a trivial individual contribution becomes substantial when multiplied across many similarly situated actors. The Court also brushed aside the Fifth Amendment due process challenge, observing that it is hardly a deprivation of due process for the federal government to regulate activity it simultaneously subsidizes through price supports and quotas.

### Concurrence

No separate concurrence was filed; the opinion was 9-0. The unanimity is itself historically significant. Justice Jackson had drafted internal memoranda during the 1942 reargument exploring how far the aggregation rationale could extend — drafts that later scholars (including Barry Cushman) have examined to reconstruct the intellectual movement from the direct/indirect test toward a purely economic-effects analysis. Jackson's published opinion deliberately avoided any doctrinal ceiling on the aggregation principle, leaving the question of limits entirely to future cases. The absence of a concurrence signals that all nine Justices accepted, or declined to challenge, the broadest possible reading of the Commerce Clause's economic reach.

### Dissent

No dissent was filed. The absence of any dissent from Owen Roberts and Felix Frankfurter — two Justices associated with prior skepticism about expansive New Deal readings — is instructive. Roberts had provided the decisive vote in West Coast Hotel v Parrish (1937), the "switch in time that saved nine" that ended the Lochner era; by 1942 he had largely abandoned his earlier formalism. Frankfurter, despite occasional narrower readings of federal power in dicta and academic writing, was a committed institutional pragmatist who believed the Court should defer to Congress on economic matters. The unanimity in Wickard reflects the post-1937 constitutional settlement: the Court had conceded to the political branches on economic regulation, and no sitting Justice was willing to re-fight the battle. United States v Darby (1941), decided the year before and also unanimous, had already overruled Hammer v Dagenhart and repudiated the earlier production/commerce distinction; Wickard simply extended Darby's logic one step further.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

1. **Home-fed corn under a federal feed-grain quota.** A dairy farmer grows all his corn for his own cattle during a period when Congress has enacted a federal feed-grain marketing quota program, and never offers a bushel for sale. Under Wickard's aggregation logic the answer is clear: corn grown for own-livestock consumption competes with corn in the commercial feed market by displacing purchases the farmer would otherwise make. The economic fungibility of the commodity and the existence of a comprehensive congressional price-stabilization scheme supply both conditions Jackson required. The home-grown corn falls within the Act.

2. **Backyard egg production under a federal egg marketing order.** A small-scale hobbyist raises forty hens and consumes all eggs at home, never selling any. Congress has enacted a federal egg marketing order regulating commercial egg prices and production volumes. Even though any individual's home-flock production is negligible, Wickard teaches that the aggregation inquiry is class-wide: if all similarly situated small-flock owners consumed home-produced eggs rather than purchasing commercial eggs, the aggregate effect on the egg market could be substantial. The controlling question is whether the class of activity, viewed collectively, substantially affects interstate commerce — and congressional findings would likely support that conclusion.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

3. **Purely private herb gardens with no market substitution.** Congress passes an act purporting to regulate home cultivation of culinary herbs — basil, oregano, thyme — grown in backyard gardens for personal use. Unlike wheat, these herbs have no established national commodity market to protect; the statute is not embedded in a broader commercial regulatory scheme; and the aggregation claim is implausible because herb prices are not set by federal marketing orders and home-grown herbs do not competitively displace commercial herb sales in a way that undermines any federal price-stabilization program. The Lopez Court's requirement that the regulated activity be economic in nature and connected to a broader scheme of commercial regulation would defeat the statute. This falls outside Wickard precisely because the economic-effects story cannot plausibly be told.

4. **No comprehensive regulatory scheme.** Congress enacts a standalone federal statute regulating one farmer's practice of storing grain in his own barn, without any surrounding program of marketing quotas, price supports, or commodity-level stabilization. Wickard's aggregation principle operates within Jackson's implicit assumption that the regulated activity fits into a broader interstate commercial scheme — the entire Agricultural Adjustment Act machinery. Without that scheme, the constitutional hook disappears. Lopez's Commerce Clause categories require that intrastate activity, even if aggregated, be part of a broader regulation of interstate economic activity. A naked single-site directive with no surrounding regulatory program would fail.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

5. **Home craft beer production under a federal alcohol quota.** A hobbyist brews fifty gallons of craft beer for personal consumption under a period when Congress has enacted alcohol volume-control regulations administered by the TTB. The Wickard framework is facially applicable: beer is an economic commodity, home brewing reduces commercial beer purchases, and there is a comprehensive federal scheme regulating alcohol production. Gonzales v Raich (2005) confirmed that Wickard survives for economic activity — and home brewing of an alcoholic product seems economic in the relevant sense. But there is a live counterargument grounded in the personal-use quantity and the structure of modern alcohol regulation, which largely exempts small-batch home production rather than treating it as a quota problem. Whether a court would follow Raich's broad aggregation reasoning or credit a personal-use exemption on constitutional grounds is genuinely uncertain.

## Critique

**Originalist critique.** Clarence Thomas's concurrence in United States v Lopez (1995) offers the sharpest originalist challenge to Wickard: the founding-era Commerce Clause was understood to reach trade and exchange — traffic — not production, manufacture, agriculture, or other activities that precede and enable commerce. On this reading, Wickard was wrong the day it was decided because the aggregation principle effectively erases the constitutional distinction between what Congress can regulate (interstate commerce itself) and what the States reserve to themselves (local economic life). Thomas would require Congress to show that regulated activity is itself commercial exchange, not merely that it might affect commercial prices. The New Federalism concern is related but distinct: if "substantial effects on interstate commerce" is a judicially unreviewable aggregate finding, the enumerated-powers structure of Article I loses any limiting function. Congress could reach anything, since virtually any local activity has some aggregate economic effect somewhere.

**Progressive / functionalist response.** The progressive defense of Wickard, associated with Justice Breyer's Lopez dissent and the academic work of Norman Redlich and Bruce Ackerman, is that Jackson's economic-effects test is the only constitutionally workable standard in a nationally integrated economy. The pre-1937 direct/indirect distinction was a legal fiction that frustrated reasonable federal economic management, as the Great Depression demonstrated. If home-grown wheat is substitutable for market wheat, it is economically indistinguishable from market wheat, and the Constitution does not require Congress to ignore economic reality. The aggregation principle is not a blank check — Jackson required a substantial economic effect, not a merely plausible one — but critics of the originalist position argue that Lopez and Morrison reintroduced the very formalism (economic vs. noneconomic activity) that Wickard repudiated, with no better principled foundation.

## Key Quotations

> "That appellee's own contribution to the demand for wheat may be trivial by itself is not enough to remove him from the scope of federal regulation where, as here, his contribution, taken together with that of many others similarly situated, is far from trivial." (Jackson, J.) [317 U.S. at 127–28](https://app.midpage.ai/document/wickard-v-filburn-103716?utm_source=mcp&lines=117)

> "But even if appellee's activity be local and though it may not be regarded as commerce, it may still, whatever its nature, be reached by Congress if it exerts a substantial economic effect on interstate commerce, and this irrespective of whether such effect is what might at some earlier time have been defined as 'direct' or 'indirect.'" (Jackson, J.) [317 U.S. at 125](https://app.midpage.ai/document/wickard-v-filburn-103716?utm_source=mcp&lines=88)

> "But if we assume that it is never marketed, it supplies a need of the man who grew it which would otherwise be reflected by purchases in the open market. Home-grown wheat in this sense competes with wheat in commerce." (Jackson, J.) [317 U.S. at 128](https://app.midpage.ai/document/wickard-v-filburn-103716?utm_source=mcp&lines=123-124)

> "It is hardly lack of due process for the Government to regulate that which it subsidizes." (Jackson, J.) [317 U.S. at 131](https://app.midpage.ai/document/wickard-v-filburn-103716?utm_source=mcp&lines=148)

## Key Points

- High-water mark of New Deal-era Commerce Clause doctrine; remains the touchstone for aggregation analysis.
- Vote: 9-0. Unanimous opinion by Jackson on reargument.
- Doctrinal pair with NLRB v Jones and Laughlin Steel (1937) and United States v Darby (1941).
- Later limited in scope by Lopez and Morrison for noneconomic activity, but reaffirmed in Gonzales v Raich (2005) for economic activity in a regulatory scheme.

- [[Topics/Enumerated Powers|Enumerated Powers]] — Wickard is the central case for the proposition that enumerated commerce power is broad enough to reach purely local, non-commercial production; Thomas's Lopez concurrence targets Wickard as the source of a constitutionally unmoored reading of Article I.
- [[Topics/Federalism|Federalism]] — Wickard's elimination of the production/commerce distinction is the principal doctrinal move that post-New Deal federalism scholarship reacts against; Lopez and Morrison's category tests are partially an attempt to restore Wickard-era limits that Wickard itself removed.
- [[Cases/United States v Lopez (1995)|United States v Lopez (1995)]] — Lopez limits Wickard to economic activity embedded in a broader commercial regulatory scheme; Rehnquist's majority and Thomas's concurrence each read Wickard as reaching its outer boundary and declining to extend the aggregation principle to non-economic local acts.
- [[Cases/United States v Morrison (2000)|United States v Morrison (2000)]] — Morrison reaffirms and extends Lopez's economic-activity requirement; the VAWA provision failed even though Congress had assembled extensive findings, demonstrating that Wickard's aggregation rationale does not supply an unlimited congressional fact-finding power.
- [[Cases/Gonzales v Raich (2005)|Gonzales v Raich (2005)]] — Raich reaffirms Wickard for regulated economic commodities: homegrown marijuana for personal medical use is economically indistinguishable from market marijuana and fits within a comprehensive federal drug regulatory scheme, making the Wickard aggregation analysis controlling.
- [[Cases/NFIB v Sebelius (2012)|NFIB v Sebelius]] — Ginsburg's dissent invokes Wickard's aggregation principle to defend the individual mandate; Roberts's majority distinguishes Wickard on the ground that Wickard regulated existing wheat cultivation while the mandate compels entry into commerce, drawing the activity-inactivity line that limits the aggregation logic.
