# Tennessee v Lane (2004)

## Memory Jogger

> ADA Title II validly abrogates state sovereign immunity as applied to courthouse access — because the right of access to judicial proceedings is a fundamental due process right, Congress has broader prophylactic authority in that domain even under Boerne's congruence-and-proportionality framework.

---

## Facts

George Lane is a paraplegic who used a wheelchair. He was required to appear in a second-floor courtroom in Polk County, Tennessee, at a courthouse that had no elevator. For his first hearing, he crawled up the stairs rather than miss his court date. When summoned to the same courtroom a second time, he refused to crawl up again or to be carried, and was arrested and jailed for failure to appear. Beverly Jones, a certified court reporter who uses a wheelchair, alleged that she was effectively prevented from working in various Tennessee courthouses because they were inaccessible.

Lane and Jones sued Tennessee under Title II of the ADA, which prohibits public entities — including states — from discriminating against qualified individuals with disabilities in the provision of services, programs, or activities. Title II expressly abrogates state sovereign immunity. Tennessee moved to dismiss the claims on Eleventh Amendment grounds, and the district court denied the motion.

---

## Procedural History

The district court denied Tennessee's motion to dismiss. The Sixth Circuit affirmed, upholding Title II's abrogation of sovereign immunity. Tennessee petitioned for certiorari. The Supreme Court had previously held in Board of Trustees v Garrett that Title I of the ADA did not validly abrogate state sovereign immunity. Tennessee argued that Title II should fare no better.

## Holding

Title II of the ADA, as applied to the class of cases implicating the fundamental right of access to the courts, constitutes a valid exercise of Congress's Section 5 authority to enforce the guarantees of the Fourteenth Amendment. The abrogation of state sovereign immunity in that application is congruent and proportional to documented violations of the Due Process Clause's fundamental right of court access.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Justice Stevens distinguished this case from Garrett by focusing on which constitutional guarantee Congress was enforcing. In Garrett, Congress targeted disability-based employment discrimination under the Equal Protection Clause — and because disability classifications only require rational basis review, the prophylactic bar was set low, and Congress's record was insufficient. Here, the Court assessed Title II as applied to courthouse access, which implicates the Due Process Clause's guarantee of a fundamental right: access to judicial proceedings. When Congress legislates to protect fundamental rights (rather than against mere rational-basis equal protection violations), it has significantly broader prophylactic authority under Section 5.

The majority reviewed the legislative record and found extensive evidence that states had systematically excluded disabled individuals from courthouses and courtrooms. This pattern of inaccessible facilities constituted an actual or potential deprivation of the constitutional right of court access. Title II's requirement of reasonable modifications to afford access was therefore congruent and proportional to this documented violation. The majority emphasized the as-applied nature of its holding — it upheld Title II only as applied to court-access cases, leaving open Title II's validity in other contexts. This as-applied methodology allowed the Court to avoid either wholesale invalidation or wholesale validation of Title II.
