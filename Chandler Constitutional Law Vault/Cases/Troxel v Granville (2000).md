# Troxel v Granville (2000)

## Memory Jogger

> The Supreme Court struck down Washington's expansive third-party visitation statute as unconstitutionally applied because it permitted a court to override a fit mother's visitation decisions without according any special weight to her parental judgment, reaffirming the fundamental right of parents to direct the upbringing of their children.

---

## Facts

Tommie Granville and Brad Troxel had two daughters together but never married. After the relationship ended, the children visited Brad's parents, Jenifer and Gary Troxel, regularly. After Brad's suicide in 1993, Granville limited the grandchildren's visits with the Troxels to one short visit per month. The Troxels petitioned under Washington's nonparental visitation statute, Rev. Code Wash. sec. 26.10.160(3), which provided that "any person" may petition a court for visitation "at any time" and that courts may grant such visitation whenever "visitation may serve the best interest of the child." The trial court awarded the Troxels significantly more visitation than Granville had proposed. Granville appealed, challenging the statute's constitutionality. The statute was sweeping in scope: it allowed any person at any time to petition, placed no threshold requirements on the petitioner, and required courts to grant visitation whenever the judge determined it was in the child's best interest, without any presumption in favor of the fit parent's judgment.

---

## Procedural History

The Washington Superior Court awarded the Troxels substantially more visitation than Granville offered, rejecting her proposed schedule. The Washington Court of Appeals reversed, finding the statute unconstitutional as applied and on its face. The Washington Supreme Court affirmed the reversal on the narrower ground that the statute violated the Due Process Clause as applied to Granville -- as a wholesale delegation of power to state courts to substitute their judgment for that of fit parents -- without reaching facial invalidity. The U.S. Supreme Court granted certiorari and affirmed the Washington Supreme Court by a 6-3 vote, though without a majority opinion.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Plurality:** O'Connor (author), joined by Rehnquist (C.J.), Ginsburg, Breyer (as-applied holding only)
- **Concurrence in judgment:** Souter (would invalidate facially as overbroad)
- **Concurrence in judgment:** Thomas (would apply strict scrutiny; rational basis inappropriate for fundamental right)
- **Dissent:** Stevens (statute constitutional; disagrees no fundamental right is absolute)
- **Dissent:** Scalia (no unenumerated parental rights enforceable against democratic majorities by courts)
- **Dissent:** Kennedy (disputes plurality's approach; would remand for best-interest analysis)

---

## Holding

As applied to a fit custodial parent, Washington's nonparental visitation statute is unconstitutional under the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. The Due Process Clause protects the fundamental right of parents to make decisions concerning the care, custody, and control of their children. A visitation statute that places the best-interest determination solely in the hands of the judge, without according any presumption of validity or special weight to the fit parent's own determination, impermissibly infringes on that fundamental right. A state court may not disregard and override the decisions of a fit parent concerning visitation merely because the judge believes a different arrangement would be better.

---

## Analysis

### Plurality Opinion (O'Connor)

Justice O'Connor's plurality took an as-applied rather than facial approach. She reaffirmed the long-established fundamental right of parents, rooted in Meyer v Nebraska (1923) and Pierce v Society of Sisters (1925) and extending through the Due Process Clause, to make decisions concerning the care, custody, and control of their children. The critical defect in Washington's statute was its "breathtaking breadth": it allowed any person at any time to petition, and placed the best-interest determination entirely in the judge's hands with no deference to a fit parent's choice. When Granville agreed to some Troxel visitation but proposed a more limited schedule, the trial court simply substituted its own judgment without asking why the parent's decision was insufficient. The plurality held that fit parents are presumed to act in their children's best interests, and "if a fit parent's decision of the kind at issue here becomes subject to judicial review, the court must accord at least some special weight to the parent's own determination." The plurality declined to hold that all third-party visitation statutes are facially unconstitutional or to specify the standard of review.

## Key Points

- Reaffirmed the fundamental liberty interest of fit parents to direct the upbringing and care of their children, rooted in Meyer and Pierce
- The plurality's as-applied approach left constitutional law on third-party visitation statutes uncertain
- No majority opinion: 4 pluralists, 2 concurrers in judgment applying different standards, 3 dissenters
- Troxel highlights the Marks rule problem: what holding binds lower courts when the majority agrees on a result but not a rationale?
- Thomas's concurrence explicitly raises the question the plurality avoids: what standard of review governs infringement of parental rights?
- Scalia's dissent presents the most systematic originalist challenge to unenumerated fundamental rights as a category
