# McCulloch v Maryland (1819)

## Memory Jogger

> The case that established Congress's implied powers under the Necessary and Proper Clause and barred states from taxing federal instrumentalities -- the foundation of broad federal power.

---

## Facts

In 1816, Congress chartered the Second Bank of the United States. The Bank established a branch in Baltimore, Maryland. In 1818, the Maryland legislature enacted a statute imposing a tax on all banks not chartered by the state -- effectively targeting the Bank of the United States as the only such institution operating in Maryland. The tax required either payment of an annual fee of $15,000 or the use of stamped paper (issued by the state at a cost) for all bank notes. James McCulloch, the cashier of the Baltimore branch, issued bank notes without complying with the Maryland law. Maryland sued McCulloch for the penalties. The state courts upheld the tax, and the case was appealed to the Supreme Court.

The constitutional provisions at issue were Article I, Section 8 (the Enumerated Powers, particularly the Necessary and Proper Clause) and Article VI (the Supremacy Clause). Maryland argued that the Constitution was a compact of sovereign states, that no enumerated power authorized Congress to create a corporation, and that states retained the sovereign power to tax entities within their borders.

---

## Procedural History

The case originated in Baltimore County Court, Maryland, where McCulloch was found liable for failing to comply with the state tax law. The Maryland Court of Appeals affirmed. McCulloch appealed to the United States Supreme Court, which took the case on a writ of error. The central questions were (1) whether Congress had the constitutional power to incorporate the Bank, and (2) whether Maryland could constitutionally tax its operations.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority:** Marshall, C.J. (unanimous Court)
- **Concurrence:** None
- **Dissent:** None

---

## Holding

The Court held unanimously that (1) Congress possesses the implied power to incorporate the Bank of the United States under the Necessary and Proper Clause, and (2) the State of Maryland may not impose a tax on the operations of the Bank because the power to tax involves the power to destroy, and states cannot use taxation to impede the constitutional operations of the federal government. The Maryland tax was declared unconstitutional and void under the Supremacy Clause.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Chief Justice Marshall structured the opinion around two great questions.

**First Question: Congress's Power to Create the Bank.** Marshall began by noting the practical weight of historical practice -- the first Congress debated and authorized the original Bank, and after its charter lapsed, the government's experience of fiscal difficulties led even prior opponents to support rechartering. But Marshall did not rest on stare decisis alone.

He rejected Maryland's theory that the Constitution was a compact among sovereign states whose agent (the federal government) must be subordinate to its principals. Instead, Marshall grounded federal authority in popular sovereignty: the Constitution was ratified by the people acting through state conventions, not by state governments. The government of the Union is "emphatically and truly a government of the people" -- it derives its powers from them and acts directly on them.

Marshall acknowledged that the federal government is one of enumerated powers. But he introduced a crucial interpretive principle: "we must never forget that it is a constitution we are expounding." A constitution, unlike a legal code, marks only "great outlines" and "important objects," leaving minor details to be deduced from the nature of those objects. The Tenth Amendment's deliberate omission of the word "expressly" (which had appeared in the Articles of Confederation) confirmed that implied powers were contemplated.

Turning to the Necessary and Proper Clause, Marshall dismantled Maryland's argument that "necessary" meant "indispensable." He showed through textual comparison that the Constitution itself uses the word with varying force -- Article I, Section 10 requires imports duties to be "absolutely necessary," while the Necessary and Proper Clause uses "necessary" without that qualifier. In common usage, "necessary" means convenient, useful, or conducive to an end, not strictly indispensable. Moreover, the Clause is placed among Congress's powers, not among the limitations in Section 9, indicating it was meant to enlarge rather than restrict legislative discretion.

This yielded the celebrated test for implied powers: "Let the end be legitimate, let it be within the scope of the constitution, and all means which are appropriate, which are plainly adapted to that end, which are not prohibited, but consist with the letter and spirit of the constitution, are constitutional."

Marshall paired the test with an anti-pretext limit: if Congress were to adopt measures prohibited by the Constitution, or were to pass laws "under the pretext of executing its powers" for the accomplishment of objects not entrusted to the federal government, it would be the Court's painful duty to declare such an act not the law of the land. The limit is a safety valve rather than an active check in modern doctrine (the Court has rarely invalidated federal legislation on pretext grounds alone; narrowing has usually come through the substantive scope of the enumerated power itself), but it remains an internal feature of the McCulloch test.

Applying this test, Marshall found that Congress's enumerated powers over taxation, borrowing, commerce, and military affairs made a national bank an appropriate and plainly adapted means of fiscal administration. The degree of necessity was a question for Congress, not the courts.

**Second Question: Maryland's Power to Tax the Bank.** Marshall reasoned from the Supremacy Clause. If the states could tax the Bank, the power to tax -- which involves the power to destroy -- would enable states to defeat and render useless the power Congress had constitutionally exercised. A state that taxes a federal instrumentality effectively controls that instrument and thereby controls the operations of a government declared supreme over it. The people of one state, acting through their legislature, would be taxing the people of all other states who had no voice in that legislature. This violated the principle that the power to tax should be exercised only by those whose constituents bear the burden.

Marshall carefully limited the holding: the immunity did not extend to ordinary property taxes on the Bank's real estate or to taxes on citizens' interests in the Bank, so long as such taxes applied equally to all similar property. The vice of Maryland's law was that it singled out a federal instrumentality for a discriminatory burden.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

1. **Hypo:** Congress creates a federal student loan corporation to administer education lending programs, and a state imposes a special tax on its operations. **Why:** Under McCulloch's test, if education lending is connected to Congress's spending power and the corporation is an appropriate and plainly adapted means of executing that power, the corporation is constitutional. The state tax on its operations would be struck down under the anti-tax principle because the power to tax is the power to destroy a federal instrumentality.

2. **Hypo:** Congress establishes a federal digital currency system administered by a chartered entity, and states argue no enumerated power authorizes digital currency. **Why:** Marshall's broad reading of the Necessary and Proper Clause means Congress need not point to an express power to create a digital currency -- it need only show the system is an appropriate means of executing enumerated powers over coinage, taxation, and commerce.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

3. **Hypo:** A state imposes a general property tax that applies equally to all real property within its borders, including land owned by the Bank of the United States. **Why:** Marshall explicitly carved out this scenario -- states retain the power to tax real property of the Bank "in common with the other real property within the State." The tax is not discriminatory and does not single out a federal instrumentality.

4. **Hypo:** Congress charters a corporation to operate amusement parks and claims the Necessary and Proper Clause authorizes it. **Why:** Operating amusement parks is not plainly adapted to any enumerated power. McCulloch requires the end to be "legitimate" and "within the scope of the constitution." A corporation with no connection to an enumerated power fails the test.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

5. **Hypo:** Congress creates a federal broadband corporation to provide internet service in rural areas, and a state imposes the same telecom franchise fees it charges private companies. **Why:** The corporation likely passes the McCulloch test (connected to commerce power), but the state fee is not discriminatory -- it applies to all telecom providers equally. The question is whether the fee operates as a tax on federal operations (barred) or as a generally applicable regulatory fee (potentially permitted under Marshall's carve-out for nondiscriminatory taxation).

---

## Critique

McCulloch's sweeping language has been both celebrated and criticized for over two centuries. The opinion's great achievement is providing a workable framework for implied powers that allowed the federal government to adapt to unforeseen circumstances. But several tensions run through the reasoning.

From an originalist perspective, Marshall's popular sovereignty argument -- that the Constitution derives from "the people" rather than the states -- oversimplifies the complex ratification process. The people ratified in state conventions, and the Constitution required state-by-state approval, suggesting the compact theory was not as easily dismissed as Marshall made it appear.

From a structural perspective, critics note that Marshall's test ("appropriate," "plainly adapted," "not prohibited") is so permissive that virtually any congressional action connected to an enumerated power passes muster. Justice Thomas and other modern originalists have argued this reading effectively reads the enumerated powers doctrine out of the Constitution. The question of where "appropriate means" ends and naked federal overreach begins has never been satisfactorily answered.

The taxation holding raises its own questions. Marshall's maxim that "the power to tax involves the power to destroy" proves too much -- as Justice Holmes later observed in *Panhandle Oil Co. v. Mississippi*, "the power to tax is not the power to destroy while this Court sits." The real principle is intergovernmental tax immunity, which later doctrine has significantly narrowed.

## Key Points

- Established the doctrine of implied powers: Congress may use means not expressly enumerated so long as they are appropriate and plainly adapted to executing enumerated powers
- Defined the scope of the Necessary and Proper Clause broadly -- "necessary" means useful or conducive, not indispensable
- Established intergovernmental tax immunity: states cannot tax federal instrumentalities because the power to tax is the power to destroy
- Grounded federal authority in popular sovereignty rather than state compact theory
- Created the foundational test for whether congressional action exceeds enumerated powers, later cited in cases from Gibbons through NFIB v. Sebelius
- Marshall's interpretive principle -- "it is a constitution we are expounding" -- remains the cornerstone of living constitutionalism arguments
- The Tenth Amendment's omission of "expressly" (present in the Articles of Confederation) signals that implied powers were contemplated by the framers
