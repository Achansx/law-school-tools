# Seminole Tribe of Florida v Florida (1996)

## Memory Jogger

> The Court holds that Congress cannot abrogate state sovereign immunity under Article I powers (here, the Indian Commerce Clause), overruling Pennsylvania v Union Gas Co; and that Ex parte Young prospective relief is unavailable where Congress has enacted a detailed remedial scheme -- leaving tribes without a federal forum to enforce the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act against unconsenting states.

---

## Facts

The Indian Gaming Regulatory Act of 1988 (IGRA) required states to negotiate in good faith with Indian tribes seeking to conduct class III gaming on tribal lands. IGRA authorized tribes to sue states in federal court to compel good-faith negotiation. The Seminole Tribe of Florida brought suit against Florida and its governor after the state refused to negotiate.

Florida raised Eleventh Amendment immunity. The Tribe argued that Congress had validly abrogated state sovereign immunity through IGRA pursuant to the Indian Commerce Clause. The threshold questions were: (1) whether Article I alone -- specifically the Indian Commerce Clause -- could serve as constitutional authority for Congress to abrogate state sovereign immunity; and (2) if not, whether the Ex parte Young doctrine permitted suits for prospective injunctive relief against state officials.

---

## Procedural History

The district court denied Florida's motion to dismiss, holding that IGRA validly abrogated state immunity. The Eleventh Circuit reversed, concluding that Congress lacked authority to abrogate immunity under the Indian Commerce Clause. The Supreme Court affirmed the Eleventh Circuit and further held that Ex parte Young was unavailable on the facts.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority:** Rehnquist (joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas)
- **Concurrence:** None

- **Dissent:** Souter (joined by Ginsburg, Breyer)

---

## Holding

First, Congress cannot abrogate the Eleventh Amendment immunity of states by exercising its Article I powers, including the Indian Commerce Clause. Pennsylvania v Union Gas Co (1989) is overruled. Only the enforcement provisions of the Fourteenth Amendment (under the Fitzpatrick exception) provide a valid constitutional basis for abrogation of state immunity. Second, the Ex parte Young doctrine -- which permits suits against state officers for prospective relief -- is unavailable where Congress has enacted a detailed remedial scheme that does not authorize suit against the state itself; courts may not supplement that scheme through equity.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Chief Justice Rehnquist's majority reasoned from the text and history of the Eleventh Amendment. The Eleventh Amendment reflects a structural principle of state sovereign immunity that predates the Constitution: states are immune from unconsented suit in federal court by private parties. Congress may abrogate that immunity only when acting pursuant to a constitutional provision that itself limits state sovereignty -- the Fourteenth Amendment, ratified after the Eleventh, expressly restricts what states may do to their citizens, and Section 5 expressly grants enforcement power to Congress. Article I grants no equivalent authority; it creates federal regulatory power over commerce but does not displace the background principle of state immunity.

The majority expressly overruled Pennsylvania v Union Gas Co (1989), a fractured plurality holding that the Commerce Clause authorized abrogation. That decision lacked a majority rationale and was in tension with the broader immunity structure.

On Ex parte Young, Rehnquist held that where Congress has prescribed a detailed remedial structure -- here IGRA's specific negotiation-and-mediation mechanism -- courts must respect Congress's judgment about the appropriate remedy. Supplementing the scheme with Young-style equitable relief would rewrite Congress's work.

## Key Points

- Congress cannot abrogate Eleventh Amendment state sovereign immunity under Article I powers alone; Fitzpatrick v Bitzer's Section 5 exception is the only valid abrogation path.
- Overrules Pennsylvania v Union Gas Co (1989), which had held Commerce Clause could support abrogation.
- The Ex parte Young doctrine does not apply where Congress has enacted a detailed remedial structure for the right at issue.
- Completes the constitutional map: Section 5 = abrogation permitted (Fitzpatrick); Article I = abrogation not permitted (Seminole Tribe).
- Subsequent cases (Alden v Maine, Florida Prepaid, Kimel) extend Seminole Tribe to state court suits and other federal rights.

### Abrogation lineage at a glance

Seminole Tribe is the choke point: every later abrogation case turns on whether Congress is acting under Article I (barred) or Section 5 (permitted but bounded by congruence and proportionality).

| Case | Year | Constitutional vehicle Congress invoked | Outcome | Why |
|------|------|-----------------------------------------|---------|-----|
| Fitzpatrick v Bitzer | 1976 | Section 5 (Fourteenth Amendment, Title VII back pay) | Valid abrogation | Section 5 postdates and expressly limits the states; sovereign immunity yields. |
| Seminole Tribe of Florida v Florida | 1996 | Article I (Indian Commerce Clause, IGRA) | Invalid abrogation; overrules Union Gas | Article I cannot circumvent sovereign immunity; only Section 5 can. |
| City of Boerne v Flores | 1997 | Section 5 (RFRA against states) | Invalid as substantive redefinition | Section 5 is remedial; Congress cannot expand the substance of rights. Sets the congruence-and-proportionality test. |
| Florida Prepaid v College Savings Bank | 1999 | Article I and Section 5 (Patent Remedy Act) | Invalid on both grounds | Article I bar follows Seminole Tribe; Section 5 fails Boerne because the legislative record is too thin. |
| Allen v Cooper | 2020 | Article I and Section 5 (Copyright Remedy Clarification Act) | Invalid on both grounds | Re-applies Seminole Tribe and Florida Prepaid; stare decisis carries the same result twenty years later. |
