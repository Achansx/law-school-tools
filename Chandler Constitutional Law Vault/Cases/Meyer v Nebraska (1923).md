# Meyer v Nebraska (1923)

## Memory Jogger

> Nebraska's post-WWI law banning the teaching of foreign languages to children before eighth grade struck down as a violation of teachers' liberty to teach and parents' liberty to direct their children's education — the Court's first catalog of unenumerated liberty interests protected by the Fourteenth Amendment.

---

## Facts

In the wake of World War I, a wave of anti-German sentiment swept American states. Nebraska enacted the Siman Act in 1919, which prohibited any person from teaching a subject in any language other than English to any student who had not successfully passed the eighth grade. The law applied to all schools, public and private alike.

Robert Meyer was a teacher at Zion Parochial School in Hampton, Nebraska. In May 1920, he was found in the act of teaching reading in German from a Lutheran Bible stories text to ten-year-old Raymond Parpart. Meyer was convicted under the Siman Act and fined $25. He challenged the conviction on the ground that the Act violated the liberty protected by the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause, both his own liberty as a teacher and the liberty of parents to direct their children's education.

Nebraska argued that teaching foreign languages before eighth grade impeded the development of an American identity and hindered the formation of citizens with undivided loyalty to the United States. The state pointed to the Plato-inspired ideal of state-directed education and the exigencies of wartime assimilation.

---

## Procedural History

The District Court of Hamilton County, Nebraska convicted Meyer. The Nebraska Supreme Court affirmed on the ground that the Act was a valid exercise of the police power, reasoning that the suppression of foreign-language instruction served the legitimate state goal of fostering patriotism and civic unity. Meyer petitioned the U.S. Supreme Court, which heard the case together with related challenges from Iowa and Ohio involving similar foreign-language restrictions on private schools.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority:** McReynolds (joined by seven justices — 7-2 decision)
- **Dissent:** Holmes (joined by Sutherland) — argued that the Fourteenth Amendment was not intended to restrict the states' power to foster a common language and that the experiment of standardized public instruction was "within the realm of experiment," citing "social welfare" as justification

---

## Holding

Nebraska's Siman Act, as applied to prohibit the teaching of German in a private school to children who had not yet passed the eighth grade, unconstitutionally interferes with the liberty of teachers to teach and the liberty of parents to engage teachers to instruct their children. Both interests fall within the liberty protected by the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause. No emergency justified the blanket prohibition, and the law bore no reasonable relation to any legitimate state end: knowledge of a foreign language by a child is not "clearly harmful." The statute is arbitrary and without reasonable relation to any purpose within the competency of the state.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Justice McReynolds opened with the most significant passage in the opinion: a catalog of the liberty interests the Fourteenth Amendment protects. Going well beyond the Lochner-era liberty of contract, McReynolds announced that "liberty" under the Due Process Clause "denotes not merely freedom from bodily restraint but also the right of the individual to contract, to engage in any of the common occupations of life, to acquire useful knowledge, to marry, establish a home and bring up children, to worship God according to the dictates of his own conscience, and generally to enjoy those privileges long recognized at common law as essential to the orderly pursuit of happiness by free men." This catalog became foundational for later privacy and parental-rights jurisprudence: it appears in *Pierce*, *Griswold*, *Loving*, *Roe*, and many subsequent cases. The majority then applied a "reasonable relation" test — asking whether the restriction bore a reasonable relation to a legitimate state end — and found none. The Court acknowledged the state's power to require English instruction and to regulate school content generally, but drew the line at a categorical ban on teaching a living modern language during a period of domestic peace.

Three features of the opinion reward close reading. First, the "no emergency" qualification is doing real work. McReynolds does not hold that wartime restrictions on foreign-language instruction are categorically impermissible; he holds that peacetime restrictions supported by diffuse assimilation concerns fail the reasonable-relation test. The holding is narrower than its sweeping rhetoric implies, and it deliberately leaves space for the genuine wartime emergency the majority found absent in 1923 Nebraska. Second, there is the authorship paradox: Justice McReynolds, whose anti-Semitism and general nativism are extensively documented in extra-judicial sources, produced the century's most capacious catalog of non-economic liberties. The irony is not that he broke character; it is that the methodological move (reading substantive content into the Fourteenth Amendment's "liberty") proved more enduring than the particular animosities of its author, which is why the catalog outlived the Lochner era even as Lochner itself was repudiated. Third, the "reasonable relation" test Meyer applies is not the same as either modern rational-basis review or strict scrutiny. It has real bite: the Court looks past the legislature's asserted justification without demanding a compelling state interest or narrow tailoring. Post-1937 doctrine eventually collapsed this intermediate category into the rational-basis / strict-scrutiny binary, but Meyer's intermediate posture reappears in the Griswold-era privacy decisions before the Glucksberg Court reoriented the inquiry around history and tradition.

### Dissent (Holmes)

Justice Holmes's dissent (filed in the companion case Bartels v Iowa, 262 U.S. 404, and joined by Justice Sutherland) operated on a different plane than the majority. Holmes accepted that the state laws were unwise and would have voted against them as a legislator, but argued that the constitutional question was different: Fourteenth Amendment liberty did not bar the states from experimenting with a policy that the predominant spoken language in Nebraska classrooms should be English. Where McReynolds treated substantive liberty as a constraint on legislative choice, Holmes treated it as a narrow prohibition on outright physical restraint and plainly irrational confiscatory legislation, with educational regulation sitting comfortably inside the state's plenary authority over schools. The philosophical stakes echoed Holmes's Lochner dissent: if judicial deference to legislative experimentation was principled, it had to extend to cultural and educational experiments just as much as to labor-and-wage regulation. Sutherland's decision to join is historically striking because in the same Term he wrote Adkins v Children's Hospital, one of the period's most aggressive protections of economic liberty against legislative incursion; his vote in Meyer suggests he drew a line between legislative bars on contract (which Adkins treated as presumptively suspect) and legislative experiments with schooling (which he accepted as squarely within the police power). The asymmetry exposes the Lochner era's selective libertarianism, which is precisely the point Holmes was driving at.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

1. **Hypo:** A state law bans private tutors from teaching any non-English language to children under 12, citing the asserted benefit of cultural assimilation. **Why:** The bar reaches private instruction of modern foreign languages in peacetime conditions on an asserted benefit no more concrete than the "foster American identity" rationale Nebraska offered and that Meyer rejected. The private tutor's liberty to pursue a common occupation, and parental liberty to engage that tutor, sit squarely inside McReynolds's catalog. Meyer's "reasonable relation" test has enough bite to look past the diffuse assimilation rationale.

2. **Hypo:** A state law permits private schools to teach any subject in any language, but bars them from offering supplemental foreign-language instruction to any child who has not passed the eighth grade (mirror of the Siman Act as applied, but with Hebrew and Latin also proscribed). **Why:** This is the facts of Meyer with the dead-language carve-out removed, and if anything it is a stronger case for unconstitutionality because the law no longer even pretends to distinguish between disfavored and favored ancient languages. Parental liberty to direct the child's education and teacher liberty to engage in a lawful occupation both apply; the reasonable-relation test fails for the same reason it failed in 1923.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

3. **Hypo:** A state law requires that the primary language of instruction in all state-funded public schools be English, with foreign-language classes offered as electives. **Why:** Meyer expressly preserves the state's power to regulate its own public schools, including the curriculum and language of instruction. The law does not reach into private schools, does not forbid private foreign-language instruction outside public classrooms, and does not interfere with parental choice of school. It operates within the zone Meyer leaves to legislative control.

4. **Hypo:** A state law requires all teachers in both public and private schools to demonstrate English-language proficiency as a condition of licensure. **Why:** Meyer notes the state's legitimate power to require "that teachers shall be of good moral character and patriotic disposition" and to regulate the qualifications of those who instruct children. A facially neutral proficiency requirement that does not bar foreign-language instruction is a qualification rule, not a substantive curricular prohibition; it survives reasonable-relation review because requiring teachers to be able to communicate with English-speaking administrators, parents, and students is obviously germane to teaching competence.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

5. **Hypo:** Following a sustained campaign of attacks by foreign-speaking extremist networks, a state temporarily prohibits instruction in the relevant foreign language in private elementary schools during the period of the declared state-level emergency, framing the rule as parallel to wartime restrictions. **Why (unclear):** Meyer's "no emergency" qualification cuts both ways. The majority acknowledged that an actual emergency — not residual wartime sentiment — might support more restrictive rules, which is why the opinion rejected Nebraska's 1919 law as a peacetime measure but did not foreclose wartime regulation. The hard question is whether a declared state-level emergency is the kind of emergency Meyer contemplated (plausibly yes, given the opinion's broad gesture at "clearly harmful" circumstances) or whether Meyer's suspicion of diffuse assimilation rationales applies with equal force because the state's fear of the language rather than any concrete proof of harm is doing the work. Modern doctrine would probably demand more than a diffuse security rationale, but Meyer itself leaves a genuine crack in the rule for emergency conditions.

---

## Critique

**Progressive / common-good critique.** The opinion's best feature, from a modern civil-libertarian perspective, is its willingness to look past the legislature's asserted justification and identify the real animus: anti-German sentiment dressed up as assimilation policy. But the opinion's methodological gift — substantive reading of "liberty" — has no textual anchor in the Fourteenth Amendment's terse guarantee that "no state shall deprive any person of life, liberty, or property, without due process of law." The same methodology that produced Griswold, Loving, and Lawrence produced Lochner and the freedom-of-contract cases that Meyer's Term neighbors upheld. Critics on the left have argued that Meyer's unenumerated-liberty approach is too dependent on the justices' catalog of which liberties count; a democratic-process approach that trusts legislative majorities to set cultural policy, subject only to equal-protection and representation-reinforcement limits, would avoid freezing the Court's aesthetic preferences into constitutional law.

**Originalist / textualist critique.** Holmes's dissent points in the direction originalists later developed: if the Fourteenth Amendment's ratifiers did not intend "liberty" to cover specific cultural or educational choices, the Court is legislating. The "reasonable relation with bite" test that Meyer applies is especially vulnerable to the originalist objection because it is not anchored in any specific constitutional text; it is a judge-made scrutiny level that requires the judge to decide when legislative justifications are "real enough." Originalist commentators have also noted that Meyer's reliance on the common law of England for the content of "liberty" is selective, citing Blackstone on parental authority while ignoring the broader English tradition that accepted state power to regulate schooling and language. The same catalog that yields Meyer's parental-liberty holding could be narrowed or widened by judicial choice, and originalists argue that the choice should be made by ratifying constitutional texts, not by nine justices.

**Holmes's principled-deference critique.** Holmes's anti-Lochner position was not selective libertarianism; it was a commitment to legislative experimentation that cut across economic and cultural domains. If the Court defers to wage-and-hour regulation because the Constitution does not embed a particular economic philosophy, it should equally defer to educational-and-cultural regulation because the Constitution does not embed a particular cultural philosophy. The Meyer majority, Holmes suggests, wants the benefit of anti-Lochner deference without its burden: they accept legislative choice on economic matters (sometimes) while policing it on cultural ones. A principled jurisprudence would choose one rule or the other. Modern scholars (including Cass Sunstein and John Hart Ely in different registers) have echoed this point.

## Key Points

- First Supreme Court decision to catalog specific unenumerated liberty interests protected by the Fourteenth Amendment; this catalog reappears verbatim in *Pierce*, *Griswold*, and subsequent family-privacy cases
- Together with *Pierce v Society of Sisters* (1925), establishes the parental-rights strand of substantive due process distinct from Lochner-era economic liberty
- The "reasonable relation" test here is not the same as modern rational-basis review — it has more bite, as evidenced by the Court's willingness to look behind the legislature's asserted justification
- Both Meyer and Pierce survived the Lochner era repudiation in *West Coast Hotel* because they protect personal and family liberty, not economic liberty — the Carolene Products footnote 4 later explained this distinction

## Connections

- [[Topics/Substantive Due Process|Substantive Due Process]] — Meyer is the earliest canonical non-economic SDP decision and the methodological template the modern privacy and autonomy cases rely on; McReynolds's expansive Fourteenth Amendment "liberty" catalog (engagement in common occupations, acquisition of useful knowledge, marriage, home, children, worship, recognized common-law privileges) supplies the root vocabulary that Griswold, Loving, Roe, Lawrence, and Obergefell later draw from.
- [[Cases/Pierce v Society of Sisters (1925)|Pierce v Society of Sisters (1925)]] — the companion parental-rights decision two years later; Pierce reaches the same result on related facts (parental authority over education) and pairs with Meyer as the two-case unit routinely treated as "Meyer-Pierce" in modern doctrine.
- [[Cases/Lochner v New York (1905)|Lochner v New York (1905)]] — contemporary of Meyer that shares the methodological move (reading substantive content into Due Process "liberty") but points it at economic rather than familial choices; Meyer's survival of the 1937 repudiation while Lochner's economic-liberty branch was retired is the central doctrinal puzzle of the Lochner-Lochnerism arc.
- [[Cases/West Coast Hotel Co v Parrish (1937)|West Coast Hotel Co v Parrish (1937)]] — the 1937 decision that retires freedom of contract; Meyer is conspicuously preserved because it protects non-economic liberty and becomes a building block for the post-1937 privacy line rather than a target of the switch.
- [[Cases/United States v Carolene Products (1938)|United States v Carolene Products (1938)]] — Stone expressly cites Meyer in Footnote Four Paragraph 3 as an example of legislation directed at a "national" minority, converting Meyer from a Lochner-era artifact into a load-bearing citation in the discrete-and-insular-minorities framework; this is the textual bridge by which the post-1937 Court re-conceptualized Meyer's parental-rights holding as a representation-reinforcement case rather than a freedom-of-contract case.
- [[Cases/Griswold v Connecticut (1965)|Griswold v Connecticut (1965)]] — Douglas's penumbras-and-emanations opinion treats Meyer and Pierce as the textual anchors for a general zone of marital and familial privacy; Meyer is the first step in the doctrinal ladder from Meyer-Pierce to Griswold to Roe and beyond.
- [[Cases/Loving v Virginia (1967)|Loving v Virginia (1967)]] — draws on Meyer's "right to marry" as a fundamental-liberty component in striking down Virginia's anti-miscegenation statute; Meyer's catalog of fundamental liberties provides the SDP side of Loving's equal-protection-plus-due-process holding.
- [[Cases/Troxel v Granville (2000)|Troxel v Granville (2000)]] — O'Connor plurality treats Meyer-Pierce as foundational for parental rights against intrusive state visitation statutes; the modern application of Meyer's parental-authority principle.
- [[Lectures/Lochner and Lochnerism|Lectures/Lochner and Lochnerism]] — lecture frames Meyer as a bridge case from the Lochner era to modern substantive due process, pairing it with Pierce as the methodological machinery that survived the 1937 repudiation and was later redeployed in Griswold, Roe, Lawrence, and Obergefell.
- [[Cases/Washington v Glucksberg (1997)|Washington v Glucksberg (1997)]] — Rehnquist's Glucksberg majority places Meyer at the head of the canonical "long line" of substantive-due-process precedents recognizing fundamental liberties (right to direct the upbringing and education of one's children); the Glucksberg "deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition" two-step is presented as the methodology that recovers and disciplines Meyer's catalog rather than expanding it.
