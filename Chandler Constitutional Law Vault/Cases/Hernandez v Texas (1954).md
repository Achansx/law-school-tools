# Hernandez v Texas (1954)

## Memory Jogger

> The Fourteenth Amendment's Equal Protection guarantee is not confined to a two-class black-white framework; it protects any identifiable group singled out for disparate treatment, and systematic exclusion from jury service of such a group violates the Constitution.

---

## Facts

Pete Hernandez, a Mexican-American agricultural laborer, was indicted for murder in Jackson County, Texas, and convicted by an all-Anglo jury. He moved to quash the indictment and the petit jury panel on the ground that persons of Mexican descent had been systematically excluded from grand and petit juries in the county. Evidence showed that while Jackson County's population included roughly fourteen percent persons with Spanish surnames, no person of Mexican descent had served on a jury in the county for at least twenty-five years.

The community maintained de facto separation of Mexican-Americans in public life: segregated restaurants, a separate "colored men" bathroom at the courthouse designated for "hombres aqui," and a school district that segregated Mexican-American children through the fourth grade. Texas conceded the disparity but argued that persons of Mexican descent are "white" for constitutional purposes and therefore any exclusion implicated no Fourteenth Amendment concern.

---

## Procedural History

Hernandez was convicted in Texas state court. He appealed to the Texas Court of Criminal Appeals, which affirmed the conviction, reasoning that the Fourteenth Amendment contemplated discrimination only between whites and blacks, and that because Mexican-Americans were considered white, no constitutional violation occurred. The Supreme Court granted certiorari.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority:** Warren (unanimous, 9-0)

---

## Holding

The Fourteenth Amendment's Equal Protection Clause is not limited to discrimination between the "two classes" of white and black. Whenever a defendant shows that an identifiable, distinct class of persons has been systematically excluded from jury service, the Equal Protection Clause requires the state to justify the exclusion. Community attitudes and treatment can establish that a group constitutes a distinct class. Hernandez established through statistical, testimonial, and documentary evidence that Mexican-Americans were a distinct class in Jackson County and that their exclusion from juries was systematic. The conviction must therefore be reversed.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Chief Justice Warren's unanimous opinion rejects Texas's "two-class theory" that limited equal protection to white-black discrimination. The opinion holds that the Fourteenth Amendment's reach turns not on fixed racial categories but on whether an identifiable class has been singled out for different treatment. A class's distinctness can be shown by community attitudes, including segregated public facilities, separate schools, and social exclusion, all of which Hernandez established in Jackson County. Having shown the existence of a distinct class, Hernandez then established systematic exclusion through the twenty-five-year absence of any Mexican-American juror. The burden shifted to the state to justify or rebut the inference of purposeful discrimination, and Texas offered no adequate justification. Warren rejects Texas's circular argument that because jury commissioners claimed to select "qualified" jurors without regard to descent, the absence of Mexican-Americans must reflect merit rather than exclusion. The conviction falls.

### Concurrence / Dissent

Hernandez was unanimous. No Justice filed a separate opinion, and Chief Justice Warren wrote for the full Court. The unanimity is historically significant for two reasons. First, the case was decided on May 3, 1954, exactly two weeks before Brown v Board of Education, and the Court that would produce Brown's unanimous reading of the Fourteenth Amendment was simultaneously working out a class-based equal protection framework for non-racial groups. Second, the absence of a dissent forecloses any later contention that Texas's two-class theory retained doctrinal life. For the predicate law on systematic jury exclusion, see Strauder v West Virginia (1880), Norris v Alabama (1935), and Cassell v Texas (1950), where Justice Frankfurter's concurrence had already signaled that jury-exclusion analysis turned on whether an identifiable class had been systematically barred, not on race alone. Hernandez completes that arc.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

1. A Japanese-American defendant in 1940s California is tried for theft before an all-white jury. Records show that despite a substantial Japanese-American population in the county, no person of Japanese descent has served on a jury in twenty years. County officials maintain segregated schools and restrict Japanese-Americans from holding local office. Under Hernandez the defendant establishes class distinctness through community attitudes and systematic exclusion through the statistical disparity. The state must offer more than jury commissioners' testimony that they selected "qualified" jurors. Conviction reversed on the same rationale as Hernandez.

2. An LGBT defendant in a modern jurisdiction with documented official animus (public statements by officials disparaging LGBT residents, discriminatory licensing decisions, and exclusion from local boards and commissions) is tried before a jury panel from which every openly LGBT member of the venire has been removed by for-cause challenge or struck through administrative procedures. A twenty-year pattern of non-service is established. If the defendant can show the group is an identifiable class under community attitudes and that exclusion is systematic, Hernandez's framework applies. Same outcome.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

1. A defendant who is one of three lawyers admitted to the local bar is tried before a jury with no practicing lawyers on it. He claims lawyers are a class systematically excluded from jury service. Hernandez requires a showing that the community treats the putative class as distinct through social separation, segregated facilities, or comparable community attitudes. Professional status alone, unaccompanied by community separation, does not satisfy the class-distinctness requirement. The claim fails.

2. A defendant shows that in the last year his county's jury pool included no members of his ethnic group, but the group constitutes only 1% of the county population and the total number of jurors impaneled was 60. Random variation adequately explains the absence. Without the multi-year statistical pattern or the "taxes our credulity" inference Hernandez drew from twenty-five years of complete absence among thousands of jurors, the defendant cannot establish systematic exclusion. The claim fails.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

A Muslim defendant in a post-2001 county argues that Muslims are an identifiable class and that systematic exclusion from jury service has occurred over the past decade. Evidence shows some community separation (separate cultural events, reported employment discrimination) but also significant integration (Muslim attorneys, business owners, and officeholders in the county). The statistical disparity is real but contested: Muslim-surname identification is less reliable than Spanish-surname identification, and the baseline population is disputed. The case turns on whether community-attitude evidence is strong enough to establish class distinctness, and whether the statistical showing matches Hernandez's "taxes our credulity" threshold. The outcome is genuinely uncertain.

## Critique

Hernandez is the decisive doctrinal bridge from the race-binary conception of equal protection to modern identifiable-class analysis. For seventy-five years after ratification, the Fourteenth Amendment's reach had been litigated almost exclusively in the context of discrimination against Black Americans. Strauder v West Virginia (1880) framed jury exclusion as a racial wrong, and the Court's early equal protection cases treated "race" as the governing classification. Hernandez refuses Texas's invitation to treat that history as the amendment's limit and instead articulates a functional test: an identifiable class singled out for disparate treatment triggers constitutional scrutiny, regardless of whether the class is defined by race, ancestry, national origin, or another community-recognized distinction.

Progressive critique reads Hernandez as anticipating modern anti-subordination doctrine. The opinion's focus on community attitudes, segregated facilities, and social exclusion treats class distinctness as a sociological fact rather than a biological or legal category. That move is the ancestor of later cases recognizing national origin, alienage, and (with qualifications) sex as classifications warranting scrutiny. Scholars of Mexican-American legal history, notably Michael Olivas and Ian Haney Lopez, argue Hernandez also marks the first time the Supreme Court recognized Mexican-Americans as a constitutionally cognizable group, a recognition that shaped decades of subsequent civil rights litigation including Keyes v Denver School District No. 1 (1973) and Plyler v Doe (1982).

Originalist critique responds that the Reconstruction Amendments were drafted and ratified to address the post-emancipation status of Black Americans, and that extending equal protection to groups defined by ancestry or national origin reads the amendment beyond its original understanding. On the originalist view, any extension of the clause beyond the formerly enslaved should come from constitutional amendment or legislation, not judicial decree. The universalist response, grounded in textualism, observes that the clause speaks in terms of "any person," not "any freedman," and that the Court's consistent practice since Yick Wo v Hopkins (1886) has been to read the text at face value. Warren's opinion in Hernandez is best understood as a textualist rejection of a narrow historical gloss rather than a progressive expansion of clause meaning.

A doctrinal tension noted by later commentators is that Hernandez's class-based framework does not map cleanly onto the tiered-scrutiny analysis the Court developed in San Antonio Independent School District v Rodriguez (1973) and the post-Washington v Davis (1976) intent-effect framework. Hernandez treats community attitudes and statistical disparity as sufficient to establish a prima facie case of purposeful exclusion, whereas modern equal protection doctrine demands discriminatory intent beyond disparate impact. Whether Hernandez's evidentiary threshold survives Washington v Davis is a contested question that law-school examinees should be prepared to discuss.

Chandler-exam framing: Hernandez is both a foundational equal protection case and a reminder that the doctrine's historical foundation is not limited to black-white discrimination. The case reliably appears on exams paired with Brown to show the Warren Court's parallel commitments to racial desegregation and broader class-based equality.

## Key Points

- Decided two weeks before Brown v Board of Education, often overlooked but doctrinally foundational.
- Rejects the two-class theory and opens equal protection to any identifiable class.
- Establishes community attitudes (segregated facilities, schools, social exclusion) as evidence of class distinctness.
- Anchors later cases extending equal protection beyond race to ethnicity, national origin, and other classifications.
- Often cited in Mexican-American civil rights litigation as the first SCOTUS recognition of the group as constitutionally cognizable.
- Chandler emphasis: Hernandez is the bridge from race-specific to class-based equal protection analysis.
