# Sherbert v Verner (1963)

## Memory Jogger

> The government cannot condition unemployment benefits on willingness to work on the Sabbath — denying benefits to a person who refuses Saturday work for religious reasons substantially burdens free exercise and requires a compelling justification.

---

## Facts

Adell Sherbert was a Seventh-day Adventist employed at a textile mill in South Carolina. When her employer introduced Saturday shifts, she refused to work on Saturdays because Saturday was her Sabbath — a day of religious observance central to her faith. Her employer terminated her employment as a result. Sherbert applied for unemployment compensation benefits under South Carolina's Unemployment Compensation Act. South Carolina denied her benefits, finding that she had failed without good cause to accept "suitable work" offered by her employer. The state's position was that her refusal to accept Saturday work — regardless of its religious basis — disqualified her from benefits under the statutory scheme applicable to all claimants who decline available employment.

The denial effectively confronted Sherbert with a forced choice: either abandon her Sabbath observance and accept Saturday shifts, or maintain her religious practice and forfeit her eligibility for unemployment benefits. No exemption from the Saturday-availability requirement existed for observant Seventh-day Adventists or other religious minorities whose faiths prohibited Saturday work.

---

## Procedural History

Sherbert sought review before the South Carolina Supreme Court, which upheld the denial of benefits, finding no constitutional violation. The United States Supreme Court granted certiorari to consider whether the denial violated the First Amendment's Free Exercise Clause as incorporated against the states by the Fourteenth Amendment.

## Holding

South Carolina's denial of unemployment compensation benefits to Sherbert violated the Free Exercise Clause of the First Amendment. A state may not condition public benefits on the relinquishment of religious practice, and any substantial burden imposed on the exercise of religion must be justified by a compelling state interest pursued through the least restrictive means available.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Justice Brennan's majority opinion established what became known as the Sherbert test or the compelling interest test for free exercise claims. The analysis proceeds in two stages. First, the Court asks whether the government action substantially burdens the claimant's religious practice. Here, the burden was clear: South Carolina forced Sherbert to choose between her faith and her eligibility for a public benefit she had contributed to and was otherwise entitled to receive. This coercive pressure on religious observance was functionally equivalent to a direct fine on Saturday worship — the state penalized religious exercise even if it did not prohibit it outright.

Second, having identified a substantial burden, the Court asked whether the state had a compelling interest sufficient to justify it. South Carolina argued that a religious exemption would invite fraudulent claims and burden the unemployment compensation system. The majority found these concerns speculative and insufficient. The state had not demonstrated that accommodating religious objectors would cause the system to fracture, and the absence of any accommodation option reflected an unjustified prioritization of administrative convenience over First Amendment rights.

### Stewart Concurrence

Justice Stewart concurred in the result but wrote separately to highlight what he viewed as an unresolved doctrinal tension. The majority required South Carolina to grant a religious exemption from its neutral unemployment-eligibility rule, yet the Court's Establishment Clause jurisprudence at that time (Engel v Vitale had been decided the previous year; Schempp was decided the same day) had been reading the clause to forbid state preferences for religion. Stewart asked how a constitutional regime that bars religious accommodations under the Establishment Clause can simultaneously require them under the Free Exercise Clause. His answer was that the Court's Establishment Clause doctrine had become unduly strict and that a proper reading of the two clauses would harmonize them around a core principle of state neutrality toward religion. Stewart's concurrence is an early statement of the "play in the joints" problem — the space between what the Free Exercise Clause requires and what the Establishment Clause forbids — that Locke v Davey (2004) and Trinity Lutheran v Comer (2017) would later have to map explicitly.

### Harlan Dissent (joined by White)

Justice Harlan's dissent made two points. First, the majority created an unequal treatment between religious and secular refusals to accept suitable work: a secular worker who refused Saturday shifts for purely personal reasons would be disqualified from benefits, but a religious worker who refused for Sabbath reasons would qualify. This, Harlan argued, is an affirmative preference for religious over secular motivations — an Establishment Clause problem in the guise of a Free Exercise remedy. Second, Harlan challenged the majority's substantial-burden analysis: the state had not prohibited Sherbert's religious practice but had merely declined to subsidize her choice to observe Saturday Sabbath, and a refusal to subsidize is not the same as a prohibition. On Harlan's view, the state is constitutionally free to tell all workers (religious and secular alike) that benefits are conditioned on availability for suitable employment, and a person whose religion renders them unavailable bears the cost of that unavailability in the same way that a person whose personal preferences render them unavailable does. The dissent anticipates by nearly three decades the reasoning that Justice Scalia would adopt for the majority in Employment Division v Smith (1990), which effectively overruled Sherbert for most free exercise claims.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

**Hypothetical 1: Muslim worker denied unemployment for refusing Friday shifts.** A Muslim employee is fired after refusing to accept shifts that conflict with Friday noon Jumah prayer, explaining that Friday congregational prayer is central to his religious practice. The state denies unemployment benefits on the ground that he refused "suitable work." He sues under the Free Exercise Clause.

**Why the same result.** Sherbert maps directly: the state conditions a public benefit on the relinquishment of a sincerely held religious practice, creating a substantial burden equivalent to a fine on Friday worship. The only question is whether the state can articulate a compelling interest served by the least restrictive means, and the fraud/administrative concerns South Carolina raised in Sherbert are the same concerns a modern state would raise. Post-Smith, Sherbert still governs this fact pattern because unemployment compensation schemes contain individualized exemptions (the "good cause" and "suitable work" determinations), which takes the case outside Smith's neutral-and-generally-applicable rule and into the individualized-assessment doctrine that Smith itself preserved.

**Hypothetical 2: Orthodox Jewish worker fired for refusing late-Friday or Saturday shifts in a jurisdiction with a statutory accommodation requirement.** An Orthodox Jewish retail worker is dismissed after declining any shift that begins after sundown Friday or runs through Saturday. The state denies unemployment benefits; he sues.

**Why the same result.** This is Sherbert on a different Sabbath day and — importantly — in a state with a parallel statutory accommodation regime that demonstrates the state's capacity to accommodate religious observance without administrative collapse. The parallel regime is a least-restrictive-alternative that the state must use; denying benefits while the accommodation regime is available is a paradigm Sherbert violation.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

**Hypothetical 3: State denial of benefits to a worker who refused Saturday shifts on the ground that Saturday is his personal "self-care" day.** The worker sincerely believes in a secular self-care practice that requires him to decline Saturday work. The state denies benefits.

**Why a different result.** Sherbert protects religious practice, not personal preference. The "substantial burden on religious exercise" trigger requires a religious basis for the refused conduct, and a secular self-care commitment — however sincere — does not qualify. Thomas v Review Board (1981) confirms that the relevant inquiry is whether the claimant's objection is religious in character, not whether it is reasonable or idiosyncratic. The worker has no Free Exercise claim and is disqualified under the state's neutral "suitable work" rule.

**Hypothetical 4: State prosecution of a religious group for large-scale tax evasion, where the group claims tax payment violates their theological commitments.** The Amish community in Lee, an Amish employer, paid the Social Security taxes required by federal law but then sought a refund on Free Exercise grounds, arguing that the Amish community believes in self-sufficiency and cannot participate in public welfare systems.

**Why a different result.** United States v Lee (1982) decided this fact pattern against the Amish. The Court identified a compelling governmental interest (the integrity of the Social Security system, which depends on mandatory universal participation and cannot function with piecemeal religious exemptions) and concluded that no less-restrictive alternative was available. Even under Sherbert's compelling-interest framework, a claim that would require the state to fragment a universal public-finance scheme fails at the compelling-interest step — the state interest is concrete and not speculative, and the least-restrictive-alternative analysis forecloses a religious carve-out. The contrast with Sherbert itself is instructive: Sherbert involved a speculative fraud concern in the unemployment context, while Lee involved a concrete structural concern in the tax context.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

**Hypothetical 5: Public school teacher fired for refusing to teach evolution, citing religious objections.** A public school biology teacher in a state that has adopted the Next Generation Science Standards is fired after she announces that she will teach only intelligent-design creationism. She applies for unemployment benefits; the state denies them on the ground that she refused to perform the essential duties of her employment.

**Why genuinely unclear.** Sherbert's substantial-burden analysis arguably applies — she was fired for conduct rooted in her religious commitments and the state is now conditioning unemployment benefits on her willingness to teach curriculum that violates her faith. But two countervailing factors distinguish the case from Sherbert proper. First, the state has a substantial interest in the integrity of the public school science curriculum (Edwards v Aguillard (1987) holds that the Establishment Clause bars schools from teaching creationism as science, so the state may have a compelling Establishment-Clause-driven reason to require evolution instruction), which creates a genuine compelling-interest argument that was absent in Sherbert. Second, the teacher's claim is not to refuse a single shift for religious reasons but to refuse to perform a central element of her job indefinitely, which makes her unlike Sherbert and more like an Amish farmer refusing to collect social-security taxes. The case implicates both Sherbert and Lee and might also trigger Smith's neutral-and-generally-applicable rule, depending on whether the state's curriculum requirements are viewed as individualized (Fulton-style) or as a generally applicable professional standard (Smith-style). Different lower courts have reached different results on analogous fact patterns.

## Critique

**The Smith displacement.** The most important post-Sherbert development is Employment Division v Smith (1990), which held that neutral, generally applicable laws do not trigger strict scrutiny under the Free Exercise Clause even when they substantially burden religious practice. Smith did not formally overrule Sherbert but confined its compelling-interest framework to two narrow settings: (a) cases involving "hybrid" free-exercise-plus-other-right claims, and (b) cases involving individualized exemption schemes (the "individualized assessment" carve-out that preserves Sherbert in the unemployment-compensation context itself because unemployment adjudicators exercise case-by-case judgment about what counts as "suitable work" and "good cause"). The Smith displacement was enormously consequential: Sherbert's strict-scrutiny framework went from governing every substantial-burden claim to governing only the residual category of laws that either fail neutrality / generality or require individualized judgment. RFRA (1993) and its state RFRA counterparts restored the Sherbert framework as a statutory matter for federal action (City of Boerne v Flores (1997) held RFRA unconstitutional as applied to states), recreating Sherbert strict scrutiny in federal cases while leaving state free-exercise claims under Smith's rational-basis-equivalent rule.

**Sherbert survives for unemployment cases.** Despite Smith, Sherbert itself remains good law in the unemployment-benefits context because unemployment adjudication is inherently individualized. Thomas v Review Board (1981), Hobbie v Unemployment Appeals Commission (1987), and Frazee v Illinois Department of Employment Security (1989) all applied Sherbert strict scrutiny to deny-benefits-for-religious-refusal cases, and Smith explicitly preserved that line. Professor Chandler emphasizes this as a partial-survival doctrine: Sherbert is the governing rule in its original context and in any setting where government adjudicators exercise case-by-case judgment, which pulls a surprisingly wide swath of modern regulation back into the compelling-interest framework.

**The Fulton revival.** Fulton v City of Philadelphia (2021) read Sherbert's individualized-exemption doctrine broadly, holding that a city's foster-care contract that contained a case-by-case discretion clause was not "generally applicable" under Smith and therefore triggered Sherbert-style strict scrutiny. Barrett's Fulton concurrence (joined by Kavanaugh, in relevant part by Breyer) openly invited a reconsideration of Smith itself, signaling that several members of the current Court may be willing to restore Sherbert as the default rule for all Free Exercise claims. The significance of Sherbert has therefore grown rather than diminished since Smith, as the individualized-exemption doctrine has proved more elastic than Smith's drafters anticipated.

**Administrability critique.** Critics of Sherbert (including Smith's author, Justice Scalia) argued that the compelling-interest framework is unadministrable: courts must assess the sincerity of every religious claim, weigh the substantiality of every burden, and evaluate the necessity of every regulatory choice against a least-restrictive-alternatives benchmark. This inquiry, the critique goes, requires judges to make theological and political judgments they are institutionally incompetent to make, and creates a religious-exemption regime that systematically advantages well-organized religious groups over diffuse, less-coordinated believers (and over nonbelievers with comparable conscientious objections). The Sherbert framework is therefore simultaneously overinclusive (protects religious claims that lack constitutional weight) and underinclusive (fails to protect secular conscience claims that raise the same liberty concerns).

**Equal-treatment critique (Harlan's dissent, extended).** Harlan's dissent voiced an equality concern that has become central to modern critiques of the Sherbert framework: by singling out religious claimants for special exemption, the doctrine creates a hierarchy in which religious conscience receives greater constitutional protection than secular conscience. Under the modern post-Smith framework, this concern is partially answered by RFRA and state RFRAs, which in many states extend protections only to religious claims and thereby institutionalize the hierarchy. The originalist critique — advanced by Justice Kagan in Masterpiece Cakeshop-adjacent discussions and by scholars like Andrew Koppelman — is that the Free Exercise Clause, properly read, protects religious practice from targeted government hostility but does not create an affirmative right to exemptions from neutral laws, which is what Sherbert had to assume. The progressive answer (Laurence Tribe, Kent Greenawalt) is that the unequal protection of religious versus secular conscience is a feature of the constitutional text itself and therefore cannot be remedied by doctrine without amendment.

**Progressive reading.** Progressive scholars (Douglas Laycock, Michael McConnell) celebrate Sherbert as the doctrinal foundation of robust religious-liberty protection against majoritarian regulation. On this reading, Sherbert correctly identified the coercive power of public benefits as a tool for pressuring religious minorities, and its compelling-interest framework provides a workable constitutional check on state action that burdens minority religious practice. The Smith displacement, on this view, was a doctrinal error that Fulton is in the process of correcting. Professor Chandler frames Sherbert as a case study in how a constitutional doctrine can survive formal displacement by preserving itself through its own doctrinal carve-outs — the individualized-assessment exception has turned out to be a capacious escape hatch from Smith's bright-line rule.

## Key Points

- Sherbert establishes the **two-step compelling-interest test** for free exercise claims: (1) substantial burden on religious practice, then (2) compelling state interest pursued through least restrictive means.
- Conditioning a **public benefit** on the relinquishment of religious practice is functionally equivalent to a direct fine on that practice — the "unconstitutional conditions" logic carries into Free Exercise doctrine.
- **Employment Division v Smith (1990)** displaced Sherbert as the default rule for free exercise claims, preserving Sherbert only for (a) hybrid claims combining free exercise with another right, and (b) laws involving individualized assessment (which covers unemployment compensation itself).
- **RFRA (1993)** restored the Sherbert framework as a statutory matter for federal action; **City of Boerne v Flores (1997)** struck RFRA as applied to states under Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment; state RFRAs now mirror the Sherbert framework in roughly half the states.
- **Fulton v City of Philadelphia (2021)** read Smith's individualized-exemption carve-out broadly, effectively reviving Sherbert strict scrutiny in many state-regulation contexts; Barrett's concurrence signals possible reconsideration of Smith itself.
- Professor Chandler emphasizes Sherbert as the doctrinal benchmark against which modern free exercise cases are measured; the key exam move is to identify whether the regulation is neutral-and-generally-applicable (Smith rule) or involves individualized assessment (Sherbert rule).
