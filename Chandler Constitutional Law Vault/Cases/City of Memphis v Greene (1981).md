# City of Memphis v Greene (1981)

## Memory Jogger

> The Court holds that Memphis's closing of a street through a white neighborhood -- which inconvenienced Black motorists -- did not violate section 1982 or the Thirteenth Amendment, because the action caused no impairment of property rights and the disparate impact was a routine burden of citizenship rather than a badge of slavery.

---

## Facts

The City of Memphis closed West Drive -- a street running through a predominantly white residential neighborhood (Hein Park) -- to through traffic. The closure required motorists traveling north-south in the area to detour around the neighborhood, adding an estimated two to five minutes to the trip. The Hein Park Civic Association requested the closure, citing safety and tranquility concerns; the main through-traffic was primarily Black residents of a nearby neighborhood who used West Drive as a shortcut.

A group of Black citizens and residents of the adjacent Black neighborhood brought suit challenging the street closing under 42 U.S.C. section 1982 and the Thirteenth Amendment, arguing that the closure reflected racial animus and stigmatized the Black community by symbolically walling off a white enclave from Black access.

The district court found that Memphis acted without discriminatory purpose but that the closure had a racially disparate impact, and ruled for the plaintiffs on section 1982 and Thirteenth Amendment grounds. The Sixth Circuit affirmed. The Supreme Court reversed.

---

## Procedural History

The district court ruled for the plaintiffs. The Sixth Circuit affirmed. The Supreme Court reversed, finding no violation of either section 1982 or the Thirteenth Amendment, and declining to reach the question of whether section 1982 required proof of discriminatory intent.

## Holding

The city's closing of a street does not violate 42 U.S.C. section 1982 because the closure did not impair any property interest within the meaning of that statute. A minor increase in travel time for motorists is not a deprivation of the right to inherit, purchase, lease, sell, hold, or convey property. Nor does the closure violate the Thirteenth Amendment: the dispropionate inconvenience to Black motorists cannot be equated to an actual restraint on liberty comparable to the odious practice the Thirteenth Amendment was designed to eradicate, and the discriminatory impact is a routine consequence of racially homogeneous urban neighborhoods, not a badge or incident of slavery.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Justice Stevens's majority analyzed section 1982 first. The statute protects specific property interests -- inheritance, purchase, lease, sale, hold, and conveyance of real and personal property. The Court had broadly construed this language in prior cases (Jones v Alfred H Mayer Co, Hurd v Hodge, Sullivan v Little Hunting Park) but always in the context of impairment of the right to hold or acquire property itself. A street closure that slightly increases motorists' travel time does not impair any property interest. Residents in the area retained full property rights; the closure affected their use of a public street, not their ownership or acquisition of real estate.

On the Thirteenth Amendment, Stevens held that the disparate impact was a routine and inevitable consequence of urban geography: because urban neighborhoods are often racially homogeneous, almost any traffic regulation will have a disparate racial impact. To treat such inevitable consequences as badges of slavery would "trivialize" the Thirteenth Amendment. The Fourteenth Amendment's equal protection claim also failed under Washington v Davis because there was no proof of discriminatory intent.

## Key Points

- Section 1982 protects specific enumerated property interests (purchase, sale, hold, convey); mere increase in travel time does not impair any such interest.
- The Thirteenth Amendment's badges-and-incidents doctrine has limits: routine disparate impacts from facially neutral laws do not constitute badges of slavery.
- No discriminatory intent means no Fourteenth Amendment equal protection violation (Washington v Davis applied).
- This case represents a significant limitation on Jones v Alfred H Mayer Co's broad reading of section 1982.
- The majority and concurrence disagree on whether section 1982 requires discriminatory intent (an open question left for another day).
