# Chiles v Salazar (2026)

## Memory Jogger

> Conversion Therapy Can't Be Labeled "Conduct." A Colorado law banned licensed counselors from using talk therapy to change a minor's sexual orientation or gender identity while permitting affirmation-oriented therapy -- a textbook viewpoint distinction. The Court held 8-1 that speech does not lose First Amendment protection simply because the government labels it a "treatment" or "therapeutic modality," and remanded for strict scrutiny review.

---

## Facts

- Kaley Chiles is a licensed mental-health counselor in Colorado who uses traditional talk therapy -- including conversation aimed at helping clients reduce same-sex attraction or gender dysphoria consistent with their religious beliefs
- Colorado's Medical Care Treatment Law (MCTL) prohibited licensed health-care providers from using "conversion therapy" on minors, defined as any practice that seeks to change a minor's sexual orientation or gender identity, while expressly permitting therapy that affirms those identities
- The law applied specifically to licensed professionals; violations could result in disciplinary action and license revocation
- Ms. Chiles brought an as-applied First Amendment challenge, arguing the law discriminated against her speech based on viewpoint by permitting affirmation-oriented counseling while banning change-oriented counseling
- The Tenth Circuit held the law regulated professional conduct with only an incidental effect on speech and therefore did not trigger heightened First Amendment scrutiny

---

## Procedural History

- Ms. Chiles challenged the MCTL in the U.S. District Court for the District of Colorado, which granted summary judgment against her
- The Tenth Circuit affirmed, relying on a "professional speech" doctrine and the speech-incident-to-conduct rationale to apply only rational-basis review
- The Supreme Court granted certiorari (No. 24-539) to resolve a circuit split on the First Amendment status of professional counseling regulations
- The Supreme Court reversed and remanded (8-1), March 31, 2026

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority (8-1):** Gorsuch, J. (joined by Roberts, C.J., Thomas, Alito, Sotomayor, Kagan, Kavanaugh, and Barrett, JJ.)
- **Concurrence:** Kagan, J. (joined by Sotomayor, J.) -- agreed the law was viewpoint-discriminatory but noted that content-based but viewpoint-neutral regulations of professional speech would raise different and harder questions
- **Dissent:** Jackson, J. -- argued the law regulates harmful medical treatment, not speech, and that states' police powers to protect minors from ineffective and harmful medical practices remain intact regardless of whether those practices involve verbal communication

---

## Holding

A state law that prohibits licensed counselors from using talk therapy to change a minor's sexual orientation or gender identity, while permitting therapy that affirms those identities, constitutes viewpoint discrimination under the First Amendment. Speech does not lose First Amendment protection merely because the government labels it a "treatment" or "therapeutic modality," and there is no diminished category of "professional speech" exempt from ordinary First Amendment scrutiny. The lower courts erred by failing to apply strict scrutiny, and the case was remanded for application of that standard.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Gorsuch's majority began from the settled principle that laws regulating speech based on its "communicative content" are presumptively unconstitutional and trigger strict scrutiny, with viewpoint discrimination -- favoring one side of a debate -- representing the most egregious form. Colorado's MCTL facially discriminated on viewpoint: it permitted affirmation-oriented counseling while forbidding change-oriented counseling, singling out a disfavored perspective. The majority rejected Colorado's three escape routes. First, it rejected the argument that Ms. Chiles's talk therapy was mere "conduct" exempt from First Amendment analysis -- speech does not transform into unprotected conduct simply because the government affixes the label "treatment." Second, it rejected the notion that licensed professionals operate in a zone of diminished speech protection, citing NIFLA v. Becerra (2018). Third, it rejected the speech-incident-to-conduct doctrine because the MCTL directly targeted the content of Ms. Chiles's speech, not independently unlawful conduct with incidental expressive effects. The Court remanded for application of strict scrutiny.

## Key Points

- Reaffirms there is no freestanding "professional speech" doctrine that diminishes First Amendment protection for licensed professionals (following NIFLA v. Becerra)
- Clarifies that courts must look to whether a law targets communicative content, not whether the government describes the regulated activity as "treatment" or "conduct"
- The Kagan concurrence signals that content-based but viewpoint-neutral regulations of professional speech remain an open question -- strict scrutiny may not always apply
- The 8-1 vote is unusual: the alignment of Sotomayor and Kagan with the conservative bloc reflects how deeply the viewpoint-discrimination principle cuts across ideological lines
- Jackson's lone dissent preserves space for a future Court to revisit the professional-speech question if the political valence of the therapy at issue were reversed
