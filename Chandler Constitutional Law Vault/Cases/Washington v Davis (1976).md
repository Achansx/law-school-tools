# Washington v Davis (1976)

## Memory Jogger

> Disparate racial impact alone does not establish an equal protection violation; a plaintiff must prove discriminatory purpose -- that the government acted with the intent to discriminate on the basis of race -- establishing the foundational rule separating constitutional anti-discrimination law from the broader statutory standard under Title VII.

---

## Facts

The District of Columbia Metropolitan Police Department required applicants to pass a written personnel test (Test 21) that measured verbal ability, vocabulary, reading, and comprehension. A significantly higher percentage of Black applicants than white applicants failed Test 21. Two Black applicants who failed the test sued, arguing that the test's racially disparate impact violated the equal protection component of the Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause (Washington, D.C. being a federal entity) and Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964.

The government argued that Test 21 was rationally related to the legitimate goal of identifying applicants likely to succeed in the Police Department's training program and had been validated against training program performance.

---

## Procedural History

The District Court granted summary judgment for the government, holding that Test 21 was neutral on its face and bore a rational relationship to the training program. The Court of Appeals reversed, holding that the test's racially disproportionate impact was sufficient to establish an equal protection violation without proof of discriminatory intent. The Supreme Court granted certiorari.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority:** White (joined by Burger, Stewart, Blackmun, Powell, and Rehnquist -- 7-2)
- **Concurrence:** Stevens (joined by Stewart) -- agreed on the constitutional standard but emphasized that intent can often be inferred from circumstantial evidence
- **Dissent:** Brennan (joined by Marshall) -- would have held that the disproportionate impact, combined with the government's failure to demonstrate job-relatedness, established an equal protection violation

---

## Holding

The Equal Protection Clause does not prohibit laws or official actions that are racially neutral on their face but have a racially disproportionate impact. A plaintiff challenging a facially neutral law or practice under the Equal Protection Clause must prove discriminatory purpose -- that is, that a discriminatory intent or motive was a motivating factor in the governmental decision. Disproportionate impact is not irrelevant -- it may be probative evidence of intent -- but it is not itself the constitutional violation. Because Test 21 was rationally related to a legitimate governmental purpose and plaintiffs had not established discriminatory intent, the equal protection claim failed. The Title VII claim was also rejected on the record before the Court.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Justice White's opinion establishes the central divide in race discrimination law: the gap between what the Constitution requires and what federal statutes require. Title VII, as construed in Griggs v Duke Power Co (1971), permits disparate impact claims -- an employer whose facially neutral practice has a racially disproportionate effect must justify it by business necessity. The constitutional equal protection standard is stricter in one sense (it requires proof of intent) but covers a broader range of government actors (federal, state, and local, not just employers).

White grounded the intent requirement in the structure of the Equal Protection Clause: "disproportionate impact is not irrelevant, but it is not the sole touchstone of an invidious racial discrimination forbidden by the Constitution." If disproportionate impact alone triggered strict scrutiny, an enormous range of facially neutral laws -- tax codes, welfare regulations, licensing requirements -- would be subject to the most demanding constitutional review because virtually all such laws produce racially disparate outcomes. The intent requirement cabins constitutional review to cases of deliberate discrimination.

White did not hold intent to be an all-or-nothing showing. The opinion acknowledged that intent can be inferred from circumstantial evidence, including the magnitude of the impact, the historical background, departures from normal procedural sequence, and legislative or administrative history. This opening for circumstantial proof became the Arlington Heights factors in Village of Arlington Heights v Metropolitan Housing Development Corp (1977).

On the Title VII claim, the Court found that Test 21 had been validated against performance in the police training program -- enough to satisfy the statutory standard on the record before it, though the Court noted the question might look different on fuller development.

## Key Quotations

> "Disproportionate impact is not irrelevant, but it is not the sole touchstone of an invidious racial discrimination forbidden by the Constitution. Unexplained cases may exist. Necessarily, an invidious discriminatory purpose may often be inferred from the totality of the relevant facts, including the fact, if it is true, that the law bears more heavily on one race than another." (Justice White)

## Key Points

- Establishes that the Equal Protection Clause requires proof of discriminatory intent, not merely discriminatory impact -- the foundational rule for all constitutional race discrimination claims.
- Creates the gap between constitutional law (intent required) and Title VII (disparate impact sufficient), a gap that has defined employment discrimination litigation ever since.
- Impact evidence is not irrelevant: it can be probative of intent as part of the totality of circumstances, setting up the Arlington Heights factors (1977).
- Stevens's concurrence gestures at a practical convergence: when impact is large and unexplained, intent may be inferred -- the distinction collapses somewhat at the edges.
- Brennan and Marshall's dissent would have merged the constitutional and Title VII standards, dramatically expanding equal protection review of facially neutral laws.
- Closely paired with Village of Arlington Heights v Metropolitan Housing Development Corp (1977), which formalized the multi-factor circumstantial intent inquiry.
