# United States v Carolene Products (1938)

## Memory Jogger

> Economic regulations get a deferential presumption of constitutionality, but Footnote 4 signals that legislation touching specific constitutional prohibitions, the political process, or discrete and insular minorities may warrant more searching judicial scrutiny.

---

## Facts

The Filled Milk Act of 1923 prohibited the interstate shipment of "filled milk" -- skim milk compounded with non-dairy fats (such as coconut oil) to resemble whole milk. Congress enacted the statute based on concerns that filled milk was injurious to public health because purchasers would believe they were buying nutritious whole milk. Carolene Products Company challenged the indictment for shipping filled milk in interstate commerce, arguing the statute exceeded Congress's commerce power and violated due process by arbitrarily interfering with a legitimate business.

---

## Procedural History

The indictment was dismissed in the district court, which ruled the Act unconstitutional. The government appealed directly to the Supreme Court under the Criminal Appeals Act.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority:** Stone (joined by Hughes, Brandeis, Roberts, Cardozo)
- **Concurrence:** Black (in judgment only; without opinion)
- **Dissent:** McReynolds (arguing the Act was an arbitrary exercise of the commerce power)
- **Recused:** Butler

---

## Holding

The Filled Milk Act is constitutional. Regulatory legislation affecting ordinary commercial transactions is not unconstitutional unless it is of such a character as to preclude the assumption that it rests on some rational basis within the knowledge and experience of legislators. The existence of supporting facts is to be presumed. The Court remanded for trial on whether the Act was a reasonable exercise of the commerce power.

In the most consequential footnote in constitutional law history (Footnote 4), Justice Stone suggested that a narrower presumption of constitutionality may apply when: (1) legislation appears on its face to violate a specific constitutional prohibition (such as those in the first ten amendments); (2) legislation restricts those political processes which can ordinarily bring about repeal of undesirable laws; or (3) legislation is directed at particular religious, national, or racial minorities, where prejudice against "discrete and insular minorities" may curtail the political safeguards that normally protect minorities.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Writing in 1938, Justice Stone consolidated the post-Lochner settlement in constitutional law. Where the Lochner-era Court had used substantive due process to second-guess economic legislation on policy grounds, Stone articulated a deferential posture: if any rational basis for the law can be identified -- based on facts made known or generally assumed -- the court should presume constitutionality and sustain the law. The inquiry is not whether the legislature chose wisely, but whether it could have reasonably believed the law addressed a real evil.

Stone's insight was structural. The democratic process is the ordinary corrective for unwise economic legislation; people dissatisfied with opticians' regulations or filled-milk bans can lobby their legislatures. Courts need not second-guess policy outcomes when the political process functions normally.

Footnote 4 turned this structural reasoning around. If a law either bypasses constitutional prohibitions, shuts down the political process itself, or targets a group that systematic prejudice leaves unable to protect itself through ordinary politics, the structural justification for deference collapses. Those are precisely the situations where judicial intervention is warranted because the democratic safety valve is absent or corrupted.

### Concurrence (Black)

Justice Black concurred in the judgment without writing an opinion, and his concurrence is usually read together with Black's broader body of work during his first term. Black was the first Roosevelt appointee (1937) and, through his later dissents and concurrences in cases like Adamson v California (1947) and his majority in Griffin v Illinois (1956), consistently pressed for a constitutional method anchored in enumerated text rather than open-textured doctrinal frameworks. Read that way, Black's silent concurrence is significant for what it does not endorse: Stone's Paragraph 1 of Footnote Four — the specific-prohibitions paragraph — sits comfortably with Black's text-first method because it ties heightened scrutiny to violations of identifiable constitutional provisions (the first ten amendments, incorporated against the states through the Fourteenth). But Paragraphs 2 and 3 (political-process restrictions and "discrete and insular minorities") rest on a structural or representation-reinforcement rationale that is not grounded in any specific constitutional text, and Black was consistently wary of judicial doctrines that permit the Court to invalidate legislation on structural rather than textual grounds. His decision to join only the judgment is therefore best read as a signal that the deferential-presumption holding was uncontroversial for him, while Footnote Four's non-textual second and third paragraphs were more than he wished to sign. The reservation is also consistent with Black's lifelong skepticism of substantive due process in any form, a skepticism he carried into his dissent in Griswold v Connecticut (1965) more than two decades later.

### Dissent (McReynolds)

Justice McReynolds dissented without a written opinion, consistent with his habit during his final years on the Court. Reconstructing his position requires reading his contemporaneous opinions — especially his New Deal dissents (the Gold Clause Cases, Railroad Retirement Board v Alton, Morehead v New York ex rel Tipaldo) and his Lochner-era commitments. McReynolds would have argued three points. First, the Filled Milk Act was not a genuine health regulation but a protectionist statute backed by the dairy industry; the legislative record that Stone accepts as establishing "rational basis" was an exercise in industry-captured fact-finding rather than independent legislative judgment. Second, the "presumption of constitutionality" Stone articulates amounts to a judicial abdication: if the legislature's own articulated reasons are accepted as presumptively sufficient, the Fifth Amendment's Due Process protection for economic activity becomes a dead letter in commerce regulation. Third, the statute's substantive interference with a lawful and nutritious food product (filled milk was cheaper and, for many consumers, nutritionally comparable to whole milk) exceeded the police power's legitimate reach into commerce.

On the merits: McReynolds's substantive objection had more factual support than the majority acknowledged. Modern economic historians (Geoffrey Miller's "The True Story of Carolene Products," 1987 Supreme Court Review; Daniel Gifford) have shown that the Filled Milk Act was drafted by and for the dairy lobby, that filled milk was nutritionally adequate when fortified with vitamins A and D (which the 1923 statute did not require or consider), and that the Act's practical effect was to protect dairy-industry profits by suppressing a cheaper substitute used disproportionately by poor and rural consumers. That history suggests the statute was a textbook case of rent-seeking legislation dressed in public-health clothing, and that the factual assumption on which Stone built the "rational basis" presumption was, in fact, wrong. McReynolds's dissenting instinct — skepticism of legislation that claims public-health purposes while serving private industry interests — is therefore defensible on the particular facts, even if his broader Lochner-era commitments have not aged well. The irony is that Carolene Products, the case most associated with deferring to legislative rationality, was decided on facts that a less deferential Court would likely have seen through.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

**Hypothetical 1: State ban on the sale of raw, unpasteurized cow's milk for human consumption.** A state legislature prohibits retail sale of raw milk, citing CDC data linking raw milk to bacterial outbreaks. A group of small dairies and consumers sue, arguing the ban arbitrarily restricts a lawful agricultural product and a consumer preference rooted in nutritional and cultural traditions.

**Why the same result.** This hypothetical tracks Carolene Products almost exactly: a state legislature acts on a plausible public-health rationale (documented foodborne-illness risk), and the challenge would be an economic-regulation challenge under substantive due process. Under Carolene Products's rational-basis presumption, the question is not whether the legislature's factual judgment is correct but whether any rational basis for that judgment is conceivable. The CDC data provides an unambiguous rational basis, and the court need not evaluate the competing studies that raw-milk advocates would offer. Post-1938 economic-regulation cases (Williamson v Lee Optical, FCC v Beach Communications, Heller v Doe) make the analysis straightforward.

**Hypothetical 2: Federal prohibition on the interstate sale of a specific class of industrial food additive (e.g., partially hydrogenated oils).** Congress, citing metabolic research linking trans fats to cardiovascular disease, bans the interstate shipment of foods containing partially hydrogenated oils. A coalition of food manufacturers challenges the ban under the Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause and argues the regulation exceeds Congress's commerce power.

**Why the same result.** The structural parallels to Carolene Products are precise: a federal commerce-clause regulation of an ordinary commercial product, supported by health-related legislative findings, targeted at interstate movement. Under Stone's framework, the presumption of constitutionality attaches; the FDA's scientific record and congressional findings supply the rational basis; and the challenge fails both on substantive due process grounds and under the post-Wickard commerce-power analysis. The case is easier than Carolene Products itself because, unlike the filled-milk legislative record (which did not seriously engage the possibility that fortified filled milk was nutritionally adequate), the trans-fats record rests on decades of peer-reviewed cardiovascular research.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

**Hypothetical 1: State law requiring that all commercial butcher shops display a sign indicating whether they process animals using kosher or halal methods.** The legislature argues the signage requirement protects consumer information. A Jewish and Muslim coalition sues, arguing the statute singles out religious minorities for state-compelled disclosure and signals those minorities as outsiders to majority commercial practice.

**Why a different result.** Paragraph 1 of Footnote Four (legislation within specific constitutional prohibitions, including the First Amendment's Free Exercise and Establishment Clauses) and Paragraph 3 ("discrete and insular minorities") both apply. The law classifies on the basis of religious practice and imposes a compelled-speech obligation that tracks religious identity; even if the legislature articulates a consumer-information rationale, the heightened scrutiny triggered by Footnote Four defeats the rational-basis presumption. The post-Carolene Products architecture treats this as a Free Exercise / compelled-speech case rather than an economic regulation, and strict scrutiny applies. The rational-basis presumption Stone announced for "ordinary commercial transactions" expressly does not reach legislation that targets religious groups.

**Hypothetical 2: State law imposing a "literacy and civics competency certificate" as a prerequisite to voter registration, administered by local county clerks with wide discretion.** The legislature argues the certificate ensures informed electorate participation. Voting-rights plaintiffs sue, documenting that the certificate is administered such that Black and Hispanic applicants fail at five times the rate of white applicants.

**Why a different result.** Paragraph 2 of Footnote Four (legislation restricting "those political processes which can ordinarily bring about repeal of undesirable legislation") is the core exception. The statute is a voting-process restriction — indeed, it is a paradigm of the Jim Crow-era literacy tests that Footnote Four was written to identify as outside the rational-basis presumption. Paragraph 3 independently supports heightened scrutiny because the statute's disparate-impact pattern disadvantages racial minorities. Under Carolene Products's framework and its Harper v Virginia Board of Elections (1966) and Shelby County v Holder (2013) descendants, the statute fails: the political-process distortion is doubly a democratic-accountability problem (the law targets the very mechanism by which it could be repealed) and an equal-protection problem.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

**Hypothetical: State law imposing a special tax on companies headquartered in the state that publish socially conservative political content on their corporate websites.** A state legislature in a politically progressive state enacts a 2% gross-receipts surtax on corporations that host socially conservative political commentary on any corporate-branded platform. A trade association of such companies sues, arguing the classification targets a politically unpopular viewpoint and its speakers in violation of Footnote Four.

**Why genuinely unclear.** The hypothetical surfaces a core ambiguity in Footnote Four's third paragraph. Socially conservative speakers in a progressive state may be politically unpopular and numerically in the minority in that jurisdiction, but they are neither "discrete" (readily identifiable and separable from the broader population) nor "insular" (socially and politically isolated) in the way Stone's footnote contemplated — categories coined with racial, religious, and national-origin minorities in mind. The Ackerman critique (Bruce Ackerman's Beyond Carolene Products, 1985) bites hardest here: most politically unpopular groups are not insular and in fact participate in cross-state legislative coalitions through national organizations, making the "curtailment of political processes" premise doubtful. On the other hand, Paragraph 1 (specific constitutional prohibitions) plainly applies because the statute is a viewpoint-discriminatory tax on speech that runs afoul of Minneapolis Star Tribune v Minnesota (1983) and Arkansas Writers' Project v Ragland (1987) — but that analysis rests on First Amendment doctrine, not on the "discrete and insular minorities" framework. The hypothetical is fence-sitting specifically on Paragraph 3: whether Footnote Four's representation-reinforcement rationale extends to political ideology as a classification, a question the Court has declined to resolve cleanly (see Police Dept v Mosley, Rosenberger v Rector, and more recent political-speech cases).

## Critique

**The "discrete and insular minorities" concept under strain.** Bruce Ackerman's foundational critique (Beyond Carolene Products, 98 Harv. L. Rev. 713 (1985)) argued that the minorities Footnote Four most clearly protects — racial, religious, national-origin — are not in fact the most politically powerless. Discrete and insular groups can organize, mobilize, and bargain legislatively; the genuinely powerless are diffuse and anonymous groups (welfare recipients, the chronically ill, prisoners, undocumented workers) whose members do not readily identify as a group at all and therefore cannot form the coalitions that discrete, insular minorities can. Ackerman's move inverted Stone's intuition: discreteness and insularity may be a source of political strength, not weakness, and the real representation-reinforcement case is for anonymous diffuse groups. The post-1985 Court has not adopted Ackerman's reframing, but his critique explains why modern equal-protection doctrine has struggled to extend suspect-class status beyond the original racial/religious/national-origin core (sex gets intermediate scrutiny; sexual orientation has never formally been classified; economic class has been decisively rejected under San Antonio v Rodriguez).

**The tautology problem.** A related critique is that Footnote Four's three categories function as conclusions rather than triggers. The Court applies heightened scrutiny when it deems a classification suspect, and it deems a classification suspect under the Footnote Four framework — but the framework gives little independent traction for determining when a group qualifies. John Hart Ely's Democracy and Distrust (1980) gave the representation-reinforcement reading of Footnote Four its most sophisticated defense, arguing that the three paragraphs form a unified theory of judicial review as a corrective for political-process failure. Critics (Laurence Tribe, Mark Tushnet, Michael Klarman) have responded that Ely's theory understates the inherently value-laden judgment of which classifications count as process-distorting, and that the theory cannot account for the Court's refusal to apply heightened scrutiny to economic-class classifications that have at least as strong a process-distortion case as some classifications that do receive heightened review. The Footnote therefore does real work as a heuristic, but it cannot fully substitute for the underlying normative judgment about which groups merit special judicial protection.

**The filled-milk historical irony.** The most-cited footnote in constitutional law sits in a decision upholding a piece of protectionist rent-seeking legislation. Geoffrey Miller's archival work (The True Story of Carolene Products, 1987 Supreme Court Review) shows that the Filled Milk Act was drafted at the National Dairy Association's instance, that the legislative hearings cited no independent scientific evidence of filled milk's inadequacy, that filled milk fortified with vitamins was nutritionally adequate, and that the Act's main beneficiaries were dairy producers competing against a cheaper substitute used disproportionately by poor and rural consumers. Stone's deference to the "presumption of constitutionality" was therefore deference to dairy-industry lobbying dressed up as public-health regulation. The irony runs two ways: first, the very decision that built the architecture of modern judicial review of minority-protective legislation was itself a case study in majoritarian capture of regulatory policy to the detriment of a diffuse, non-organized consumer group (low-income consumers of cheaper milk products). Second, the result shows the limit of Carolene Products's own logic: the Court's deferential rational-basis review did not even require that the legislative findings match the factual record, so industry-captured rent-seeking legislation sailed through the very doctrine Stone invented to distinguish benign economic regulation from legislation warranting judicial skepticism.

**Progressive reading.** Progressive scholars (Owen Fiss, Robin West, Frank Michelman) have celebrated Footnote Four as the doctrinal blueprint for the Warren Court's subsequent civil-rights revolution. On this reading, the three paragraphs are not a limitation on judicial review but a roadmap for affirmatively using judicial review to protect marginalized groups from majoritarian politics. The Brown v Board of Education (1954), Baker v Carr (1962), Reynolds v Sims (1964), and Loving v Virginia (1967) lines are all cashing in Footnote Four promissory notes about race, political process, and suspect classification. The critique from this camp is not that Footnote Four is too interventionist but that it is not interventionist enough: its promise of heightened scrutiny for discrete and insular minorities has been systematically underenforced, particularly for economic and class-based classifications.

**Originalist / textualist critique.** Originalist scholars (Raoul Berger, more recently Mike Rappaport, Bradford Clark) have questioned whether Footnote Four's representation-reinforcement theory has any textual grounding in the Fourteenth Amendment. The Amendment's language speaks of "equal protection of the laws," not of judicial solicitude for "discrete and insular minorities." The argument is that Footnote Four is judicial common-lawmaking, constructing a doctrine of tiered scrutiny out of whole cloth to fill the gap left by the collapse of Lochner-era due-process review. Stone's footnote, on this reading, is not a principled application of the Fourteenth Amendment but a pragmatic solution to the post-1937 problem of how the Court could remain relevant while having abandoned economic due process. The counter-argument (Jack Balkin, Akhil Amar) is that the Fourteenth Amendment was understood at ratification to protect precisely the kinds of minorities Stone identified — the Amendment's Republican drafters were acutely aware that newly enfranchised Black citizens were a "discrete and insular minority" facing hostile Southern majorities — so Footnote Four's Paragraph 3 has stronger originalist grounding than critics allow.

**Chandler's framing.** Professor Chandler presents the Carolene Products paradox as a defining feature of post-1937 constitutional law. Stone's opinion is simultaneously the most deferential document the Court has produced on economic regulation (the "presumption of constitutionality" that eventually becomes Williamson's near-irrebuttable presumption) and the most interventionist (the three-paragraph blueprint for modern tiered scrutiny that drives everything from Brown to Obergefell). That paradox, Chandler emphasizes, is structural rather than accidental: by concentrating judicial scrutiny on Footnote Four's three categories, the Court had to purchase heightened scrutiny in some domains with radical deference in others. The deal has held for nearly a century, but it is not obvious that the two halves remain in equilibrium; recent economic-regulation cases (NFIB v Sebelius on the Commerce Clause side, Epic Systems on the labor side) suggest erosion at the deferential edge, while Dobbs (2022) has complicated the heightened-scrutiny side of the deal.

**Exam framing.** The standard Con Law exam application is to pair Carolene Products with West Coast Hotel (1937) as the two-step of the 1937 revolution: West Coast Hotel redefines liberty to permit protective legislation, Carolene Products redefines scrutiny to sort cases into the two-track system. Chandler stresses that the exam-relevant move is to use Footnote Four as the analytical lens for modern-day fact patterns: once a plaintiff identifies a classification that fits one of the three paragraphs, the rational-basis presumption shifts to some heightened scrutiny, and the case is analyzed under that scrutiny rather than under the default deferential rule. The mistake to avoid is treating the footnote as a generic scrutiny-triggering mantra without carefully applying its particular categories.

## Key Points

- Establishes the rational basis presumption as the default rule for economic and commercial regulations after Lochner's fall.
- Footnote 4 is the architectural blueprint for modern tiered scrutiny: rational basis for most laws, heightened scrutiny for specific prohibitions/political-process restrictions/suspect classifications.
- The three-paragraph Footnote 4 framework is the most-cited footnote in constitutional law and drives modern equal protection doctrine.
- "Discrete and insular minorities" becomes the conceptual foundation for suspect-classification doctrine in race, national origin, and (debated) sex cases.
- Professor Chandler emphasizes the paradox: Stone's opinion is simultaneously the most deferential (rational basis) and the most interventionist (Footnote 4) document in post-New Deal constitutional law.
