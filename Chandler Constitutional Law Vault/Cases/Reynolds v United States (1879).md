# Reynolds v United States (1879)

## Memory Jogger

> The First Amendment protects religious belief absolutely but leaves Congress free to regulate religiously motivated conduct -- polygamy is not a constitutional defense to a federal bigamy charge.

---

## Facts

George Reynolds, a member of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints (Mormon Church) residing in the Utah Territory, was charged with bigamy under a federal statute that made polygamy a crime in the Territories. At trial, Reynolds proved that his faith was a sincere Mormon belief that male church members had an affirmative duty, when circumstances permitted, to practice polygamy -- a duty enjoined by church scripture and a revelation to founder Joseph Smith -- and that failure to do so when circumstances allowed would be punished by damnation in the life to come. Reynolds had received permission from recognized church authorities to enter into a polygamous marriage. The federal statute under which he was charged applied to all persons residing in the Territories and in places over which the United States had exclusive control.

---

## Procedural History

Reynolds was convicted in the District Court for the Territory of Utah. He appealed, arguing (1) that the Morrill Anti-Bigamy Act of 1862 violated the Free Exercise Clause because polygamy was a religious duty under his faith, and (2) that various evidentiary rulings -- including the admission of prior testimony from his first wife who was absent and the manner in which the jury was charged -- constituted reversible error. The Supreme Court granted review and affirmed the conviction on all grounds.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority:** Chief Justice Waite (unanimous; all participating justices joined)
- **Concurrence:** None
- **Dissent:** None

---

## Holding

The Free Exercise Clause protects religious belief and opinion absolutely, but does not extend to protect religiously motivated conduct or practices that violate valid criminal laws. Congress has plenary legislative power over the Territories and may prohibit polygamy, and a sincere religious duty to practice polygamy does not constitute a defense to a federal bigamy charge. To allow religious belief to excuse criminal conduct would make the professed doctrines of religious belief superior to the law and permit every citizen to become a law unto himself.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Chief Justice Waite's unanimous opinion distinguishes between belief and conduct as the organizing principle of Free Exercise jurisprudence. Drawing on Jefferson's Virginia Act for Establishing Religious Freedom and Madison's Memorial and Remonstrance, the Court holds that "religion" within the meaning of the First Amendment encompasses the duty one owes to one's Creator as a matter of personal conscience -- and that duty lies beyond civil government's cognizance. But the moment religion is translated into overt acts, the state may regulate. Polygamy, Waite reasons, is not a matter of opinion but an overt act subversive of the foundational institution of society: monogamous marriage. Congress, exercising its plenary authority over the Utah Territory under Article IV, Section 3, plainly had power to prohibit it. Allowing a religious motive to negate criminal intent would reduce law to a dead letter whenever a defendant could produce sincere theological justification for the forbidden act.

### Concurrence

No separate concurrences were filed; Chief Justice Waite wrote for a unanimous Court. The absence of concurrences is itself analytically significant. A unanimous bench in 1879 spanning justices with quite different views on state power signals that the belief-conduct distinction was not contested within the Court, even if the philosophical premises underlying it (Jefferson's wall-of-separation metaphor, the opinion's characterization of polygamy) later drew extensive scholarly critique. The unanimity reflects the political context as much as the doctrinal one: the federal government and both major political parties had committed to eliminating Mormon polygamy in the Utah Territory as a condition of Utah's eventual statehood, and the Court's opinion fits inside a broader federal campaign that included the Morrill Anti-Bigamy Act (1862), the Edmunds Act (1882), and the Edmunds-Tucker Act (1887), culminating in the 1890 Manifesto by which the LDS Church formally renounced the practice.

### Dissent

No dissents were filed. What the modern reader finds dissent-worthy about Reynolds — the opinion's uncited ethnographic claim that polygamy is a practice "almost exclusively" associated with non-Western peoples, its use of Jefferson's private letter to the Danbury Baptists as authoritative constitutional commentary rather than personal correspondence, and its premise that the state can sort religiously motivated conduct into categories of constitutionally tolerable and intolerable based on the conduct's social-utility assessment — passed without internal disagreement. The absence of dissent is itself a useful historical datum: the belief-conduct distinction now looks philosophically contested, but in 1879 it appeared uncontroversial even to a Court that could disagree sharply about other issues. Later scholarly critique has focused on three points: (a) the category of "belief" is much narrower than the Free Exercise Clause's text protecting "the exercise" of religion, which includes overt acts; (b) Jefferson's wall-of-separation metaphor was his own constitutional gloss, not the ratifying framers' text, and treating it as dispositive flattens the actual drafting debates; (c) the opinion's characterization of polygamy as foreign and civilizationally hostile embeds specific Protestant and Anglo-American assumptions about marriage into constitutional doctrine. Smith (1990) retreated from Reynolds's ethnographic framing but kept the core belief-conduct rule.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

1. **Hypo:** A member of a religious sect believes ritual sacrifice of animals is required by his faith and performs such a sacrifice in violation of a facially neutral anti-cruelty statute that applies to all killing of animals outside licensed slaughterhouses. **Why:** Animal sacrifice is conduct, not belief, and the statute applies to everyone regardless of motivation. Reynolds' belief-conduct distinction provides no defense: the Free Exercise Clause "was left free to reach actions which were in violation of social duties or subversive of good order" (Waite). Smith (1990) later generalized this result into the rule that neutral and generally applicable laws do not require religious exemptions. The result diverges from Lukumi only if the statute turns out to target religious practice on its face or in effect, which the facially neutral, generally applicable hypothetical excludes.

2. **Hypo:** A religious commune whose members believe in communal, faith-based child-rearing withholds children from state-required schooling; the state prosecutes the parents under a neutral compulsory-education statute. **Why:** The free exercise claim goes to conduct (withholding children from school), not belief, and Reynolds's categorical rule would allow the prosecution to proceed. Wisconsin v Yoder (1972) complicates the picture by recognizing a hybrid-rights exception for Amish families combining free exercise with parental liberty, but Smith (1990) later narrowed Yoder to its specific hybrid-rights facts and reaffirmed Reynolds's general rule for free-exercise-only claims. On the uncomplicated Reynolds analysis, the conduct falls squarely on the state-regulable side of the belief-conduct line.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

3. **Hypo:** Congress enacts a statute that specifically targets and criminalizes Mormon prayer services inside the Utah Territory while leaving similar prayer practices of other denominations untouched. **Why:** The statute directly regulates what Reynolds identifies as the protected sphere — religious belief and opinion expressed in worship — and its non-neutral targeting of a specific sect activates the full protection of the Free Exercise Clause. Church of Lukumi Babalu Aye v City of Hialeah (1993) later crystallized the modern form of this rule: a law that is not neutral and generally applicable, and that targets religious practice specifically, receives strict scrutiny and almost always fails. Reynolds's belief side of the distinction is preserved intact under Smith and Lukumi; what Smith narrowed was the conduct side's exemption doctrine, not the belief side's absolute protection.

4. **Hypo:** Congress enacts a statute regulating how religious organizations select and ordain clergy, with no civil-law analog to the conduct (not bigamy, not fraud, not tax evasion). **Why:** Purely ecclesiastical matters of internal church governance sit close to the protected "opinion" side of Reynolds's distinction because they concern the church's identity and belief rather than external conduct that harms third parties or violates ordinary civil duties. The modern doctrinal expression is the ministerial exception, which Hosanna-Tabor Evangelical Lutheran Church and School v EEOC (2012) grounds in the Religion Clauses rather than in Reynolds's belief-conduct distinction; the ministerial exception holds that civil anti-discrimination laws cannot be applied to the selection of religious ministers. Reynolds's underlying intuition — that internal religious belief and its expression in church governance are beyond state regulation — persists, but the modern doctrinal machinery (structural immunity, the ministerial exception, the church-autonomy cases) is more sophisticated than Reynolds's simple belief-conduct binary.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

5. **Hypo:** A defendant belongs to a small religious sect whose peyote-sacrament ceremony is structurally identical to the Native American Church rite that a federal statute exempts by name from controlled-substance enforcement, but the sect lacks formal NAC affiliation and so falls outside the exemption. The defendant is prosecuted; the Church is not. **Why (unclear):** Employment Division v Smith (1990) held that a neutral and generally applicable drug law does not require religious exemptions under Reynolds's belief-conduct rule, which would seem to foreclose a constitutional defense. But Smith was itself a response to a complicated legal landscape: Congress passed the Religious Freedom Restoration Act (RFRA) in 1993 to restore a compelling-interest test for federal actions, and City of Boerne v Flores (1997) invalidated RFRA as applied to states but left it operative as to federal law. The hypo's exemption for NAC specifically creates an additional wrinkle: once the government has decided to exempt some religious practice but not others, the question is whether the refusal to extend the exemption is neutral and generally applicable within Smith's framework, or whether the selective exemption itself creates a targeting problem under Lukumi's logic. Reynolds's categorical rule produces a clean answer (no exemption required); RFRA plus Lukumi's equal-treatment logic complicates the clean answer substantially. A well-briefed version of this case probably wins on RFRA grounds if the prosecution is federal, loses under Smith if RFRA does not apply, and might win under Lukumi if the selective exemption can be characterized as targeting.

## Critique

**Progressive / civil-libertarian critique.** The opinion's "civilization" framing embeds specifically nineteenth-century Protestant assumptions about acceptable marital structure into constitutional doctrine. Waite writes as though monogamous marriage is a universal social foundation transcending any particular religious tradition, but the opinion's dismissal of polygamy as a practice "almost exclusively known" to non-Western societies makes the cultural premise explicit: the Court is defending a specific civilizational model and calling it natural law. The progressive objection is that the belief-conduct line, applied to a practice that harms no third party and that consenting adults pursue as a religious duty, is not a neutral constitutional principle but a vehicle for the majoritarian suppression of minority religious practice. The same belief-conduct line used to prosecute Mormons could have been used to suppress Quaker pacifism, Seventh-day Adventist sabbatarianism, or Jehovah's Witness refusal of blood transfusions; the fact that it was not used consistently against other dissenting religious practices reveals its selective application to disfavored groups.

**Originalist / textualist critique.** The Free Exercise Clause protects "the exercise" of religion, and "exercise" is inherently conduct-oriented: religion exists in practice, not merely in belief. Reynolds's move to read "religion" as equivalent to "opinion" (following Jefferson's private correspondence rather than the constitutional text) is textually suspect because it effectively reads "exercise" out of the clause. Originalist commentators (Michael McConnell most prominently) have argued that the 1791 ratifying public understood the Free Exercise Clause to include at least some protection for religiously motivated conduct, typically through religious-exemption practices that the states had already developed. Waite's reliance on Jefferson's wall-of-separation letter is doubly problematic: Jefferson was not at the Constitutional Convention, the letter is a personal gloss written in 1802, and Jefferson's own views on religious establishment were more idiosyncratic than the opinion's treatment suggests. Smith (1990) in effect accepted some of the originalist critique of the opinion's broader language while preserving Reynolds's core belief-conduct rule by a different route (the neutral-and-generally-applicable framework).

**Doctrinal status.** Reynolds survived Smith in the sense that the belief-conduct distinction and the rule that generally applicable laws can reach religiously motivated conduct are both still good law. What has changed is the surrounding doctrinal framework: Smith's neutral-and-generally-applicable rule governs the conduct side, Lukumi's targeting analysis carves out non-neutral laws for strict scrutiny, Hosanna-Tabor's ministerial exception insulates internal religious governance, and RFRA (federal) plus state-RFRAs provide a statutory compelling-interest test that partly restores the pre-Smith framework. Reynolds's underlying claim — that religious motive cannot, as such, excuse criminal conduct that the state has power to regulate — is intact; the more interesting modern questions concern how generously to read "targeting" under Lukumi and Fulton, not whether Reynolds's core remains.

## Key Points

- Establishes the belief-conduct distinction as the foundational Free Exercise framework, predating modern scrutiny tiers.
- Later superseded by Employment Division v. Smith's general-applicability rule, but Reynolds remains the historical baseline for the proposition that religion cannot excuse criminal conduct.
- The Court's characterization of polygamy as a practice "almost exclusively known to Asiatic and African peoples" reflects the 19th-century cultural assumptions embedded in the ruling.
- Note that the "Caution" Midpage treatment reflects doctrinal evolution: Smith adopted a different rationale, and modern scholars dispute whether Reynolds' reasoning survives Smith.
