# City of Boerne v Flores (1997)

## Memory Jogger

**The RFRA Case.** Congress tried to restore the Sherbert compelling-interest test for Free Exercise after Employment Division v. Smith — but went too far. Boerne says Congress cannot use its Section 5 enforcement power to create new substantive constitutional rights or to expand existing ones; it can only *remediate* and *prevent* violations of rights already recognized by the Court. The test is **congruence and proportionality**: is the legislative remedy proportionate to the constitutional harm it is preventing?

## Facts

- St. Peter Catholic Church in Boerne, Texas, sought a building permit to expand its facility to accommodate a growing congregation
- The City of Boerne denied the permit under a local historic preservation ordinance; the church's building was located in a historic district
- Archbishop Flores sued, invoking the Religious Freedom Restoration Act (RFRA), 42 U.S.C. §2000bb, which Congress enacted in 1993 in direct response to *Employment Division v. Smith* (1990)
- RFRA required the government — federal, state, and local — to satisfy a compelling governmental interest and use the least restrictive means before substantially burdening a person's free exercise of religion
- RFRA purported to apply regardless of whether the governmental actor was federal or state/local

## Procedural History

- District court: dismissed Flores's RFRA claim; held RFRA unconstitutional
- Fifth Circuit: reversed; upheld RFRA as valid legislation under Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment
- Supreme Court: granted certiorari; reversed Fifth Circuit

## Judicial Votes

- **6-3 decision** reversing the Fifth Circuit
- **Majority**: Kennedy (joined by Rehnquist CJ, Stevens, Thomas, Ginsburg, Scalia) — RFRA exceeds Congress's Section 5 power
- **Concurrence**: Stevens — RFRA also violates the Establishment Clause (not joined by others)
- **Concurrence**: Scalia — reaffirms Employment Division v. Smith (responding to O'Connor/Souter)
- **Dissents**: O'Connor, Souter, Breyer — RFRA is valid Section 5 legislation; would revisit Smith

## Holding

RFRA is unconstitutional as applied to states. Congress's Section 5 enforcement power under the Fourteenth Amendment is **remedial and preventive**, not substantive. Congress may not use Section 5 to define the substance of constitutional rights — that is exclusively the Court's role under Marbury. A valid Section 5 law must satisfy the **congruence and proportionality test**: there must be a congruence and proportionality between the injury to be prevented or remedied and the means adopted to that end.

RFRA fails congruence and proportionality because: (1) it covered far more conduct than any documented record of state religious discrimination could justify; (2) it operated as a substantial departure from ordinary First Amendment law (compelling interest test) applied to ordinary regulatory burdens; (3) its broad reach across all levels of government and all forms of legislation was disproportionate to any pattern of constitutional violations by the states.

## Analysis

### Majority (Kennedy)

Kennedy's opinion rests on a textual, historical, and structural argument that Section 5 is a remedial enforcement power, not a substantive redefinition power. Textually, Section 5 says Congress shall have power "to enforce" the Amendment's substantive provisions; it does not say Congress may "define" or "determine" what those provisions mean. Kennedy treats the switch from "enforce" to "define" as constitutionally decisive: "Congress does not enforce a constitutional right by changing what the right is. It has been given the power 'to enforce,' not the power to determine what constitutes a constitutional violation."

Historically, Kennedy walks through the 39th Congress's rejection of Representative Bingham's original draft, which would have granted Congress plenary authority to "make all laws which shall be necessary and proper to secure ... equal protection in the rights of life, liberty, and property." That draft was pulled precisely because it gave Congress substantive legislative power over the states; the final Amendment replaced it with self-executing prohibitions plus a narrower enforcement clause. For Kennedy, the legislative history "confirms the remedial, rather than substantive, nature of the Enforcement Clause."

Structurally, Kennedy grounds the limitation in Marbury: "The power to interpret the Constitution in a case or controversy remains in the Judiciary." If Congress could alter the meaning of constitutional rights by simple majority vote, Marbury's judicial-supremacy-in-interpretation principle would be gutted and the Amendment's restrictions would cease to be, in any meaningful sense, the "provisions of the Fourteenth Amendment."

Kennedy then announces the governing test: "There must be a congruence and proportionality between the injury to be prevented or remedied and the means adopted to that end. Lacking such a connection, legislation may become substantive in operation and effect." Courts applying Boerne ask three questions. First, what constitutional right is Congress enforcing (as defined by the Court)? Second, what record of state violations of that right did Congress amass? Third, are the statute's means proportionate to that record? RFRA fails each link: it targets incidental burdens rather than purposeful discrimination, Congress's record showed "anecdotal evidence" rather than a pattern of state religious persecution, and RFRA's compelling-interest-plus-least-restrictive-means regime is "the most demanding test known to constitutional law" — vastly more stringent than Smith's baseline.

Kennedy distinguishes Katzenbach v Morgan by rereading its alternative holding narrowly. Morgan upheld Section 4(e) of the 1965 Voting Rights Act, which barred New York from imposing an English-literacy test on Puerto Rican voters. Kennedy reframes Morgan as remedial: Congress was protecting Puerto Rican voters from "discrimination in governmental services" and using voting leverage as the remedy. Morgan's arguably broader language (that Congress may expand substantive Fourteenth Amendment rights) is not a "necessary" or even "best" reading, Kennedy says, and any such suggestion is displaced by the congruence-and-proportionality framework Boerne now establishes.

### Concurrence (Stevens)

Stevens writes alone to add an independent Establishment Clause ground for the judgment. In his view, RFRA is not merely an ultra vires exercise of Section 5: it affirmatively prefers religious believers over everyone else. By granting observant churches, synagogues, and mosques a compelling-interest shield against every neutral regulation, RFRA "has provided the Church with a legal weapon that no atheist or agnostic can obtain" — a government-issued exemption regime that privileges religion over irreligion and favors one side of the religious-secular divide. Stevens treats this as a violation of the neutrality command that sits at the core of both Religion Clauses. Even if Congress could validly legislate under Section 5 to protect Free Exercise, it could not do so in a form that itself fails Establishment Clause scrutiny. No other Justice joins this argument, and it is offered as an alternative holding rather than the rationale for the judgment.

### Concurrence (Scalia)

Scalia's brief concurrence, joined by Stevens in relevant part, responds directly to Justice O'Connor's dissent. O'Connor argues that the Free Exercise Clause was originally understood to require exemptions from generally applicable laws; Scalia disagrees on the history. He surveys late-eighteenth- and early-nineteenth-century state practice and concludes that religious exemptions from neutral laws were, when granted, legislatively carved rather than constitutionally compelled. Founding-era state religious-liberty clauses that tracked the federal Free Exercise Clause were construed to protect belief and worship, not to exempt religious conduct from otherwise valid general regulation. Accordingly, Scalia defends Employment Division v Smith as historically grounded: the original understanding of the Free Exercise Clause is consistent with Smith's neutral-laws-of-general-applicability rule, and is incompatible with the broad judge-made exemption regime that RFRA attempted to constitutionalize. Scalia also notes that even if the historical record were ambiguous, stare decisis weighs heavily in Smith's favor after seven years.

### Dissent (O'Connor, Souter, Breyer)

O'Connor (joined by Breyer in part, with Souter and Breyer writing separately but agreeing in substance) would grant rehearing in Smith and reconsider the neutral-laws rule. Her core argument is historical: the Framing-era understanding of Free Exercise, as reflected in early state constitutions, colonial charters, and the writings of Madison and Jefferson, presupposed that religious conscience could override otherwise valid secular laws when the two conflicted. She canvasses state courts that granted exemptions for Sabbatarians from military drill requirements, for Quaker and Mennonite conscientious objectors, and for religious minorities from oath-taking. On that reading, Smith represented a break with rather than a continuation of original meaning, and the pre-Smith Sherbert-Yoder framework was the historically faithful doctrine.

From that premise, O'Connor argues that Congress was entitled to disagree with the Smith Court's interpretation of Free Exercise, and that RFRA is a valid Section 5 measure because the "right" Congress is enforcing is the Free Exercise Clause as the Framers understood it, not as Smith re-defined it. This is the hardest point for the majority: if Congress's interpretation of a constitutional right tracks the original meaning more faithfully than the Court's, is Section 5 really preserved by forcing Congress to defer to a judicial construction it rationally believes to be wrong? O'Connor's answer is no; Boerne's majority answer is that the Court's interpretation, right or wrong, is what Section 5 allows Congress to enforce. Souter writes separately expressing doubts about Smith without committing to overrule it; Breyer joins O'Connor on the merits but not her call for reargument.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

1. **Hypo:** Congress enacts a "Religious Land Use Restoration Act" requiring every state and local zoning authority to grant a compelling-interest exemption to any religious institution burdened by a generally applicable zoning rule. The congressional record shows three documented instances of zoning denials to churches nationwide. **Why:** This is RFRA redrafted with a slightly narrower caption and an even thinner record. Congress is again overriding neutral laws of general applicability without a documented pattern of purposeful state religious discrimination. The statute fails congruence and proportionality for exactly the reasons RFRA did in Boerne: the remedy is far broader than the demonstrated injury.

2. **Hypo:** Congress enacts the "Patent Remedy Act" permitting patent infringement suits against state universities for any infringement, however accidental, and expressly abrogating state sovereign immunity. Legislative record contains two instances of alleged state infringement, neither involving constitutional due process violations. **Why:** This is essentially Florida Prepaid v College Savings Bank (1999). Under Boerne's congruence-and-proportionality framework, Congress cannot abrogate state sovereign immunity for patent claims through Section 5 when the record shows no pattern of due process violations by the states. The remedy (across-the-board abrogation) is massively disproportionate to the injury Congress documented.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

3. **Hypo:** Congress reauthorizes the Voting Rights Act preclearance coverage formula after amassing a 15,000-page record documenting decades of racially discriminatory voting practices in covered jurisdictions. **Why:** This is the paradigm case for valid Section 5 legislation. Congress is enforcing the Fifteenth Amendment's direct prohibition on racial voting discrimination, against a deep evidentiary record of exactly that discrimination. The remedy (preclearance) is narrowly targeted to jurisdictions with documented violations. The statute easily satisfies congruence and proportionality as the VRA did in South Carolina v Katzenbach — a case Kennedy treats as the model for valid Section 5 legislation. Note: Shelby County (2013) ultimately struck the coverage formula on different grounds, but under Boerne's framework the reauthorization was valid.

4. **Hypo:** Congress enacts the Family and Medical Leave Act, guaranteeing twelve weeks of unpaid leave for serious medical conditions or family caregiving, and abrogating state sovereign immunity for employees' suits. Legislative record documents sex-based stereotyping in leave policies, with states granting more generous parental leave to mothers than fathers. **Why:** This is Nevada v Hibbs (2003). The Court upheld FMLA's self-care provision under Section 5 because Congress documented sex discrimination in state family-leave practices, and the remedy (uniform unpaid leave) was congruent and proportionate to breaking the gender-stereotyping pattern. Sex classifications already trigger intermediate scrutiny, so Congress was enforcing a right the Court already recognized, not expanding it.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

5. **Hypo:** Congress enacts the Americans with Disabilities Act Title I (employment), abrogating state sovereign immunity and requiring states to make reasonable accommodations for disabled employees. Legislative record contains hundreds of anecdotes of disability discrimination by state employers. **Why:** This is Board of Trustees v Garrett (2001), which held Title I invalid as applied to states under Boerne. The case is hard because disability discrimination is real, the record was substantial, and Congress made findings. But under Cleburne v Cleburne Living Center (1985), disability classifications get only rational-basis review — so Congress was arguably attempting to enforce a heightened standard the Court had not recognized. Garrett is the outer limit of Boerne's remedial-only principle: anecdotal evidence plus a substantive expansion above the constitutional floor fails the test, while parallel provisions of ADA Title II (public services) survived in Tennessee v Lane (2004) because they implicated the fundamental right of court access. This is the fence-sitter because the fact patterns look similar and the outcomes turn on how strictly a court calibrates "congruence" to the constitutional baseline.

## Critique

**Progressive critique (departmentalism and democratic legitimacy):** The progressive line, developed by scholars like Robert Post, Reva Siegel, and Larry Kramer, argues that Boerne erases an important American tradition of coordinate constitutional interpretation in which Congress, the President, and the public also have legitimate roles in constructing constitutional meaning. Kennedy's flat claim that the Court is the "ultimate expositor" of the Constitution — drawing on Cooper v Aaron's gloss of Marbury — overstates judicial supremacy and collapses the distinction between *judicial review* (the power to decline to enforce an unconstitutional act) and *judicial exclusivity* (the power to monopolize constitutional meaning). RFRA was itself a response to a 97-3 Senate vote and a nearly unanimous House vote rejecting Smith; Congress was acting on a considered institutional disagreement with the Court about the meaning of Free Exercise, not a bare power grab. The remedial/substantive distinction also collapses on close inspection: every prophylactic remedy necessarily *is* a substantive change in governing law (the Voting Rights Act's ban on literacy tests bans some tests that would, on a case-by-case basis, be constitutional), so Kennedy's line reduces to judicial discretion about which "expansions" to tolerate. The practical effect has been to hollow out Section 5 as a tool for protecting disfavored minorities (Garrett, Kimel) while leaving the Spending Clause (South Dakota v Dole) and Commerce Clause as the only robust vehicles — which disproportionately weakens federal remedies for state-level rights violations.

**Originalist/textualist critique (Congress's Section 5 authority cuts both ways):** The originalist line, developed by scholars like Michael McConnell and Akhil Amar (who reach opposite conclusions from each other), finds Kennedy's history thin in both directions. On one hand, McConnell has argued that the original public meaning of Free Exercise did require exemptions, so Smith itself was the original mistake and RFRA was a congressional attempt to restore the correct reading — which makes Boerne doubly wrong on originalist grounds. On the other hand, a textualist reading of Section 5 ("The Congress shall have power to enforce, by appropriate legislation, the provisions of this article") can cut either way: "appropriate" could be read as a McCulloch-style necessary-and-proper standard that leaves Congress wide latitude, or as a narrow remedial qualifier as Kennedy claims. Scalia's own concurrence illustrates the tension — he defends Smith on originalist grounds but accepts Kennedy's narrow reading of Section 5, whereas a more consistent Madisonian position might allow Congress broader enforcement discretion in exchange for judicial restraint about what the underlying rights mean. Boerne's congruence-and-proportionality test is also notably atextual: the Court invents a balancing framework to police Section 5 legislation without pointing to any constitutional provision that authorizes it, which is precisely the judicial activism originalists typically denounce when it appears elsewhere in doctrine.

## Key Points

- Congress's Section 5 power is **remedial and preventive**, not substantive; it enforces constitutional rights as defined by the Court, it does not create new ones
- The test for validity of Section 5 legislation: **congruence and proportionality** between the constitutional injury and the legislative remedy
- RFRA failed because Congress's legislative record showed insufficient documented state religious discrimination to justify the sweeping override of all generally applicable laws
- RFRA remains valid as applied to the *federal* government (Fifth Amendment due process; confirmed in *Gonzales v. O Centro*, 2006)
- Boerne is the foundation for subsequent Section 5 cases: *Florida Prepaid* (patent infringement), *Garrett* (ADA disability discrimination), *Lane* (courthouse access under ADA), *Hibbs* (FMLA gender discrimination)
- Distinguish: Congress may regulate under the Spending Clause or Commerce Clause without congruence-and-proportionality, but those powers have their own limits

## Connections

- [[Cases/Employment Division v Smith (1990)]] — the Free Exercise holding that prompted Congress to enact RFRA; Boerne strikes down RFRA, restoring Smith's neutral-law standard
- [[Cases/Sherbert v Verner (1963)|Sherbert v Verner (1963)]] — the pre-Smith compelling-interest framework RFRA was drafted to restore; Boerne holds that Congress cannot legislate the Sherbert test back into force against the states under Section 5 because doing so is substantive redefinition rather than remedial enforcement of Free Exercise as the Court has construed it. Sherbert therefore remains statutorily live against the federal government via RFRA but reverts to its pre-1993 constitutional footprint (preserved only in Smith's individualized-exemption and hybrid-rights carve-outs) at the state level.
- [[Cases/Katzenbach v Morgan (1966)]] — earlier Section 5 case suggesting Congress might have independent power to expand constitutional rights; Kennedy in Boerne limits Morgan to remedial enforcement
- [[Cases/Marbury v Madison (1803)]] — Kennedy invokes Marbury: the Court, not Congress, is the ultimate expositor of the Constitution's meaning
- [[Cases/Board of Trustees of University of Alabama v Garrett (2001)|Board of Trustees v Garrett (2001)]] — applying Boerne's congruence-and-proportionality test to the ADA (disability discrimination); Congress's record insufficient
- [[Cases/Nevada Department of Human Resources v Hibbs (2003)|Nevada v Hibbs (2003)]] — applying Boerne's test to FMLA (sex discrimination); upheld because Congress had adequate record of gender discrimination in leave policies
- [[Cases/Saenz v Roe (1999)|Saenz v Roe (1999)]] — decided two years after Boerne and supplies one of the rare substantive Privileges or Immunities Clause rights that Congress could attempt to enforce under Section 5. Any durational-residency statute Congress might enact to protect interstate travel would have to satisfy Boerne's congruence and proportionality test measured against the constitutional floor Stevens identifies for new residents in Saenz. The pairing illustrates Kennedy's core distinction: Saenz establishes the substantive right, and Boerne sets the legislative ceiling on prophylactic enforcement of that right.
- [[Cases/City of Rome v United States (1980)|City of Rome v United States (1980)]] — invoked by Kennedy as the paradigm of proportionate remedial legislation: the Voting Rights Act's preclearance regime was targeted at jurisdictions with a documented history of racial discrimination in voting and subject to expiration, making the remedy congruent and proportional to the documented constitutional injury. Boerne contrasts RFRA's nationwide, permanent sweep across every generally applicable law as disproportionate by comparison.
- [[Cases/Florida Prepaid Postsecondary Education Expense Board v College Savings Bank (1999)|Florida Prepaid Postsecondary Education Expense Board v College Savings Bank (1999)]] — the first downstream application of Boerne's congruence-and-proportionality test to a Section 5 abrogation of state sovereign immunity; the Patent Remedy Act fails because the legislative record documents no pattern of constitutional infringement and the across-the-board abrogation is therefore disproportionate. Reverses the relationship in the abrogation chain: Boerne supplies the standard; Florida Prepaid is the first to apply that standard against an Article I-derived abrogation that Congress sought to rebrand under Section 5.
- [[Cases/Allen v Cooper (2020)|Allen v Cooper (2020)]] — applies Boerne and Florida Prepaid's framework to the Copyright Remedy Clarification Act and reaches the same result twenty years later: a thin legislative record cannot justify nationwide abrogation under Section 5. Allen treats Boerne's congruence-and-proportionality test as the controlling framework and Florida Prepaid as the precedent it follows under stare decisis.
- [[Cases/Seminole Tribe of Florida v Florida (1996)|Seminole Tribe of Florida v Florida (1996)]] — decided the prior Term and supplies the predicate Boerne presumes: Article I cannot abrogate state sovereign immunity, so Section 5 is the only available constitutional vehicle. Boerne then sets the substantive ceiling on what Section 5 itself can do.
- [[Cases/Church of Lukumi Babalu Aye v City of Hialeah (1993)|Church of Lukumi Babalu Aye v City of Hialeah (1993)]] — Smith and Lukumi together define the constitutional Free Exercise floor that Boerne treats as fixed: neutral and generally applicable laws survive rational-basis review, while non-neutral or non-generally-applicable laws still trigger strict scrutiny. Boerne holds that Congress cannot raise that floor against the states via Section 5; RFRA survives only as applied to federal action. Lukumi therefore continues to protect the narrow strict-scrutiny channel after Boerne even where RFRA does not reach.
