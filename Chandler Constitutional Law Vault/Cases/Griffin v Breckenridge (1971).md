# Griffin v Breckenridge (1971)

## Memory Jogger

> The Court holds that 42 U.S.C. section 1985(3) creates a civil cause of action for victims of purely private, racially motivated conspiracies, requiring no state action, with constitutional authority grounded in the Thirteenth Amendment and the right to interstate travel.

---

## Facts

Robert Griffin, a Black man, and his companions were driving through Mississippi when Breckenridge and other private individuals (not state officers) mistakenly believed Griffin was a civil rights worker. Breckenridge and his co-conspirators stopped the car, assaulted the occupants, and inflicted serious physical injuries, acting on racial animus and a belief that the victims were engaged in civil rights activities in the area.

Griffin brought suit under 42 U.S.C. section 1985(3), which provides a civil remedy against persons who conspire to deprive any person or class of persons of the equal protection of the laws or of equal privileges and immunities. A prior Supreme Court decision, Collins v Hardyman (1951), had construed the statute to require state involvement, reasoning that the Fourteenth Amendment's state-action requirement implicitly limited the statute's reach to conspiracies involving state officers.

The question was whether section 1985(3) reached purely private conspiracies motivated by racial animus, and if so, whether Congress had constitutional authority to reach such conduct.

---

## Procedural History

The district court dismissed Griffin's complaint under Collins v Hardyman's state-action limitation. The Fifth Circuit affirmed. The Supreme Court reversed, overruling Collins v Hardyman and upholding the statute as applied to purely private conspiracies.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority:** Stewart (joined by all participating Justices -- unanimous)
- **No dissent**

---

## Holding

42 U.S.C. section 1985(3) reaches purely private conspiracies and does not require state action. To state a claim, a plaintiff must allege: (1) a conspiracy or going in disguise; (2) for the purpose of depriving a person or class of equal protection or privileges; (3) an act in furtherance of the conspiracy; and (4) injury to person or property or deprivation of a federal right. The conspiracy must be motivated by some racial or otherwise class-based invidiously discriminatory animus. Congress had authority to enact this statute under Section 2 of the Thirteenth Amendment (for racial conspiracies) and under its power to protect the right of interstate travel.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Justice Stewart's unanimous opinion began by overruling Collins v Hardyman on statutory interpretation grounds: the text of section 1985(3) facially encompasses private actors ("two or more persons"), and the "going in disguise" clause -- which has nothing to do with official conduct -- confirms that the statute was designed to reach private marauders like the Ku Klux Klan. Collins's state-action gloss was an atextual judicial imposition.

On the elements, Stewart held that the conspiracy must be animated by "invidiously discriminatory animus" -- some racial or class-based hostility -- to avoid converting section 1985(3) into a general federal tort law for all private wrongs. This is not a state-action requirement but an intent requirement.

For constitutional authority, Stewart relied on Jones v Alfred H Mayer Co: Congress has power under Section 2 of the Thirteenth Amendment to determine what constitute badges and incidents of slavery and to prohibit racially motivated private conspiracies to deprive Black citizens of basic rights as such a badge. Additionally, Congress may protect the right of interstate travel -- a constitutional right assertable against private as well as governmental interference -- as an independent basis for the statute.

## Key Points

- Overrules Collins v Hardyman: section 1985(3) does not require state action and reaches purely private conspiracies.
- The four-element test: (1) conspiracy or going in disguise; (2) depriving purpose (equal protection or privileges); (3) act in furtherance; (4) injury or deprivation of federal right.
- The conspiracy must be motivated by "some racial, or perhaps otherwise class-based, invidiously discriminatory animus" -- a significant substantive element that limits the statute's scope.
- Constitutional authority: Thirteenth Amendment Section 2 (badges and incidents of slavery) and Congress's power to protect the right to interstate travel.
- This case, along with Jones v Alfred H Mayer Co, marks the high-water mark of congressional enforcement power under the Thirteenth Amendment against private actors.
