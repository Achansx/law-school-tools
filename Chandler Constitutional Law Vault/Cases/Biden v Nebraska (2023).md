# Biden v Nebraska (2023)

## Memory Jogger

> The HEROES Act's grant of authority to "waive or modify" statutory provisions does not authorize the Secretary of Education to cancel approximately $430 billion in federal student loan debt, and the major questions doctrine requires clear congressional authorization for agency action of such staggering economic and political significance.

---

## Facts

The Higher Education Relief Opportunities for Students (HEROES) Act of 2003 authorizes the Secretary of Education to "waive or modify any statutory or regulatory provision applicable to" federal financial aid programs in connection with a national emergency, so that recipients are not placed in a worse position financially because of the emergency. In August 2022, the Secretary invoked this authority in connection with the COVID-19 national emergency and announced a debt-cancellation program that would forgive up to $10,000 in federal student loan debt for borrowers earning less than $125,000 per year, and up to $20,000 for Pell Grant recipients. The Congressional Budget Office estimated the program's cost at roughly $430 billion, affecting approximately 43 million borrowers.

Six States (Nebraska, Missouri, Arkansas, Iowa, Kansas, South Carolina) sued to enjoin the program. Missouri's theory of standing rested on harm to the Missouri Higher Education Loan Authority (MOHELA), a state-created nonprofit corporation that services federal student loans and would lose approximately $44 million per year in servicing fees under the cancellation plan. The States argued that the HEROES Act did not authorize mass cancellation of loan principal, that the program violated the major questions doctrine set out in West Virginia v EPA (2022), and that the program exceeded any permissible delegation of congressional authority.

---

## Procedural History

The District Court dismissed for lack of standing. The Eighth Circuit reversed on standing (finding injury to MOHELA) and granted a preliminary injunction. The Supreme Court granted certiorari before judgment and also stayed a separate district court order from the Northern District of Texas that had likewise vacated the program on different grounds (Department of Education v Brown, decided the same day on standing grounds).

The Supreme Court affirmed on both standing and the merits, permanently blocking the debt-cancellation program.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority:** Roberts, C.J. (joined by Thomas, Alito, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh, Barrett) — 6-3
- **Concurrence:** Barrett
- **Dissent:** Kagan (joined by Sotomayor, Jackson)

---

## Holding

Missouri has Article III standing because MOHELA, a state-created public instrumentality supervised by Missouri and performing an essential public function, would suffer concrete financial injury, and that injury is a direct injury to the State for standing purposes. On the merits, the HEROES Act's authority to "waive or modify" applicable statutory and regulatory provisions permits only modest adjustments or minor additions to existing provisions, not the basic and fundamental transformation of the federal student-loan scheme that mass cancellation of roughly $430 billion in principal would entail. The major questions doctrine applies: where an agency asserts authority of staggering economic and political significance, clear congressional authorization is required, and the HEROES Act's general "waive or modify" language does not supply it. The doctrine applies to agency programs conferring monetary benefits just as it applies to programs imposing regulatory obligations.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion (Roberts, C.J.)

Chief Justice Roberts began with standing, treating MOHELA's structural relationship to Missouri as controlling. Missouri created MOHELA, designated it an "instrumentality" of the State, supervises its board, and relies on it to perform the public function of facilitating student access to higher-education finance. Harm to MOHELA in the performance of its public function is thus a direct injury to Missouri, and Missouri did not need to demonstrate that MOHELA itself chose to sue.

On the merits, Roberts parsed the text of the HEROES Act. The statutory verb "modify" carries "a connotation of increment or limitation" and means "to change moderately or in minor fashion," as the Court held in MCI v AT&T. The Secretary's debt-cancellation program was not a modest change; it was a fundamental restructuring of the federal student-loan program that eliminated roughly $430 billion in obligations for 43 million borrowers, nearly half of all federal student-loan principal. "Waive" offered no better textual hook: waiver suspends the operation of a requirement, whereas cancellation permanently extinguishes the underlying obligation.

Roberts then applied the major questions doctrine articulated in West Virginia v EPA. The program's "staggering" economic and political significance, the unprecedented invocation of the HEROES Act to this magnitude, Congress's ongoing and contested policy debate over student-loan forgiveness, and the mismatch between the generic "waive or modify" language and a policy of this scale all demanded clear congressional authorization. The Court rejected the argument that major questions analysis should not apply to government-benefit programs; King v Burwell had already applied analogous reasoning to ACA subsidies, and Congress's Appropriations Clause authority over the purse is precisely where major questions concerns are sharpest.

### Concurrence (Barrett)

Justice Barrett joined the majority in full but wrote separately to reframe the major questions doctrine against the scholarly criticism, voiced most sharply in Justice Kagan's *West Virginia v EPA* dissent, that the doctrine operates as a freestanding clear-statement rule dressed up in textualist clothing. Barrett opens by conceding the stakes: if the doctrine is a strong-form substantive canon, textualists "should give a textualist pause," because such canons advance values external to the statute and override the text's most natural reading. She recounts the two principal academic characterizations that worry her (Sunstein's "nondelegation canon" and Heinzerling's "power canon"), and accepts the premise that newly minted strong-form canons that overenforce Article I would be in tension with the constitutional structure they purport to serve.

Her interpretive move is to relocate the doctrine inside ordinary textualism. The major questions doctrine, Barrett argues, is not a thumb on the scale against agency power; it is a contextual clue about how a reasonable speaker of statutory English understands delegations. She develops this through her "babysitter hypothetical": when a parent tells a babysitter "make sure the kids have fun," a reasonable listener would not treat that as authority to take the children on an overnight trip to an amusement park, even though the text literally permits it. Context supplies implicit limits even in the absence of express ones. The constitutional structure, Barrett says, is part of the context a reasonable interpreter brings to statutory delegations: because Article I vests "[a]ll legislative Powers" in Congress, a reasonable interpreter "would expect [Congress] to make the big-time policy calls itself, rather than pawning them off to another branch."

Barrett insists that this move does not collapse into the substantive-canon view she rejects. Treating structural expectations as interpretive context is different from imposing a normative rule that tells Congress to speak more clearly than it otherwise would. On her account, the doctrine simply recovers the "most natural" reading of ambiguous delegations; it is what a careful textualist would have done all along, now labeled. The concurrence thus positions the doctrine as descriptive rather than prescriptive, which matters because it neutralizes the Kagan critique that textualists abandon the text whenever it would yield results they disfavor. Critics have responded that Barrett's babysitter analogy proves too much (parents are not in a separation-of-powers relationship with babysitters) and that her reframing still leaves the doctrine's triggering conditions (economic/political significance, novelty, past practice) doing substantive work that ordinary textualism does not.

### Dissent (Kagan, joined by Sotomayor, Jackson)

Justice Kagan's dissent is structured around a role-confusion diagnosis: the majority, she argues, oversteps at every doctrinal checkpoint because it has mistaken itself for a national policymaker. The dissent's standing section is devastatingly specific. MOHELA is legally and financially independent from Missouri; Missouri law expressly gives MOHELA the capacity to "sue and be sued," and MOHELA is not a party, an amicus, or "even a rooting bystander" in this litigation. The State Attorney General had to file "sunshine law" demands on MOHELA to extract its loan-servicing documentation because MOHELA would not cooperate voluntarily. Kagan's central move is the bedrock-principle point: "a plaintiff cannot ride on someone else's injury." MOHELA's financial harm is not Missouri's; the state treasury will not be one dollar poorer under the Secretary's plan. Missouri is, in Kagan's framing, the paradigmatic ideological plaintiff. The majority's opinion, she writes, is "the classic case of the Court manipulating standing" precisely to reach a merits question it wants to answer, borrowing her own language from *Massachusetts v EPA* back at Chief Justice Roberts.

On the merits, Kagan reads "waive or modify" as a phrase, not two severed verbs. The pairing authorizes the Secretary to work along a spectrum from modest to substantial, because "waive" already nullifies requirements; pairing "modify" with "waive" must contemplate something more than de minimis adjustment or the conjunction is surplusage. Past HEROES Act invocations were "more modest because the precipitating emergencies were more modest": the COVID emergency produced over $5 trillion in federal relief, so proportionate agency response is precisely what a statute targeted at "national emergencies" anticipates. Her scale-matching argument ("imagine asking the enacting Congress: Can the Secretary use his powers to give borrowers more relief when an emergency has inflicted greater harm?") is meant to show that the majority's reading produces a HEROES Act that shrinks in usefulness as emergencies grow in severity.

The dissent's signature passage is the role-confusion coda: Congress is democratic, the President is accountable, but "this Court? It is, by design, as detached as possible from the body politic." The majority's willingness to adjudicate the six States' "generalized grievance" and then apply a "heightened-specificity requirement" to cabin the statute's plain scope together "substitute[] [the Court] for Congress and the Executive Branch in making national policy." That, Kagan says, is "a danger to a democratic order." The opinion ends on her trademark three-word close: "With all respect, I dissent." The rhetorical force of the dissent has already made it a staple of the anti-major-questions literature, with progressive administrative-law scholars reading it as the fullest articulation of the case that the doctrine is a raw judicial power grab dressed up in textualist grammar.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

1. **The "necessary rules" climate program.** EPA invokes a generic Clean Air Act provision authorizing "such rules as may be necessary" to implement the statute, and announces a nationwide cap-and-trade program reshaping the electricity sector. *Why: the textual hook is thinner than the HEROES Act's "waive or modify," the economic scale ($1T+) exceeds Biden v Nebraska, and every major questions indicator (political salience, novelty, congressional debate) is present. This is essentially the West Virginia v EPA fact pattern; Biden v Nebraska reinforces it.*

2. **Across-the-board interest elimination.** The Secretary invokes the HEROES Act to reduce interest rates to zero permanently on the entire federal student-loan portfolio, projected cost $300B+. *Why: Roberts's "modest adjustments and additions" reading of "modify" forecloses permanent structural reworking of the federal loan scheme. Like principal cancellation, zero-interest is not an adjustment to an existing provision; it is a replacement of the statutory interest-rate regime. The fact that "interest rate" is a dollar-valued number rather than a forgiveness figure does not change the character of the rewrite.*

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

3. **Short-term payment pause during a fresh emergency.** The Secretary, in response to a newly declared natural-disaster emergency, extends the existing loan-payment pause by six months. *Why: Roberts expressly preserves "modest adjustments" space, and past HEROES Act invocations had extended payment deadlines and reduced filing requirements without triggering major-questions concerns. Roberts lists these as canonical examples of permissible modification. Kagan's scale-matching argument would also support the outcome on the dissent's terms: a modest emergency produces a modest response.*

4. **Congress speaks clearly.** Congress enacts a statute expressly authorizing "the Secretary, in connection with the national emergency declared on [date], to cancel up to $20,000 in federal student loan principal per borrower." *Why: the clear-statement barrier the major questions doctrine imposes is satisfied. The doctrine is not a prohibition on agency action of economic magnitude; it is a requirement that Congress expressly sign off. Barrett's reframing (context plus text) and Kagan's dissent (text alone) both agree that explicit authorization resolves the case.*

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

5. **PSLF expansion by rule.** The Secretary invokes 20 U.S.C. §1087e(m) (Public Service Loan Forgiveness) to expand "public service" to cover any non-profit or government employment for borrowers on income-driven repayment plans, effectively forgiving $60B for roughly three million borrowers. *Why unclear: (a) the textual authority is more specific than the HEROES Act's generic "waive or modify," (b) the program is smaller than Biden v Nebraska's $430B but larger than Alabama Association of Realtors's $50B eviction moratorium, (c) PSLF has been the subject of ongoing congressional debate, and (d) the "basic and consequential tradeoffs" inquiry cuts both ways since this operates within an existing forgiveness program rather than inventing a new one. This is exactly the sort of mid-scale benefits program whose major-questions status Barrett's "contextual" framework and Kagan's "text-plain" framework would likely resolve differently.*

## Critique

**Progressive / administrative-law critique (Sunstein, Heinzerling, Kagan-aligned scholars).** The major questions doctrine, on this account, is a strong-form substantive canon masquerading as ordinary textualism. Its triggering conditions (economic significance, political salience, novelty, past practice) are not textual features of any statute; they are normative judgments about when Congress *should* have spoken more clearly. The doctrine therefore displaces the statute's most natural reading whenever the Court thinks the agency action is too big. Gillian Metzger and other administrative-law scholars have argued that the doctrine disproportionately disadvantages progressive regulatory programs that respond to emergent problems (climate, pandemic, inequality) because modern regulatory challenges typically require larger-scale agency action than the specific delegations Congress drafted in the 1970s contemplated. Kagan's dissent crystallizes this critique: the doctrine, she writes, "magically appear[s] as [a] get-out-of-text-free card" whenever ordinary textualism would frustrate the majority's policy preferences. The critique has bite against Barrett's concurrence because even her "contextual" version still requires the Court to decide when context tips a delegation into major-questions territory — a judgment call that, critics say, is precisely what textualism is supposed to avoid.

**Originalist / separation-of-powers defense (Alito, Gorsuch, Barrett in parts; also Hamburger, Lawson).** The defense rests on Article I's Vesting Clause ("[a]ll legislative Powers herein granted shall be vested in a Congress"). On this view, the non-delegation doctrine properly understood forbids Congress from transferring core legislative power to agencies without intelligible principles, and the major questions doctrine is a second-best prophylactic: it forces Congress to legislate the big questions itself by withholding judicial construction of ambiguous delegations to reach large outcomes. The Roberts opinion cites the Appropriations Clause as an independent reason for heightened scrutiny of benefits programs: the power of the purse is structurally congressional, and agency-initiated mass spending raises the constitutional stakes rather than lowering them. Philip Hamburger's "Is Administrative Law Unlawful?" thesis supplies the background theory: bureaucratic lawmaking threatens the accountability structures that representative legislation is designed to preserve. On this defense, Biden v Nebraska does Article I work that the non-delegation doctrine (moribund since the 1935 *Schechter*/*Panama Refining* cases) fails to do.

**Standing doctrine critique.** Kagan's MOHELA analysis is powerful but also potentially double-edged. Recognizing that a State can sue on injuries to its public instrumentalities expands state standing beyond traditional parens patriae, proprietary-interest, and sovereign-interest categories. The doctrine may boomerang on plaintiff States in future litigation: future federal programs affecting state-created public entities (utility authorities, hospital districts, port authorities) become easier to challenge, but so do actions of state-created entities that the state's political branches would prefer not to fight. Jonathan Adler has also noted that the MOHELA theory creates an awkward asymmetry: associations cannot sue on members' behalf unless at least one member has standing, yet Missouri sued on behalf of an entity that had capacity to sue itself and chose not to.

**Practical-workaround critique.** The Biden administration subsequently pivoted to the Higher Education Act's income-driven-repayment authority (20 U.S.C. §1087e(e)) as an alternative vehicle for debt relief, and launched a new regulatory proposal through the negotiated-rulemaking process in fall 2023. The fact that substantial relief remained achievable through a different statutory vehicle has led some commentators to ask whether the major questions doctrine simply channels agency action toward denser, more procedurally intensive pathways without actually reducing the aggregate regulatory output — a sort of doctrinal whack-a-mole that increases transaction costs without serving separation-of-powers values. Whether this observation undercuts or vindicates the doctrine depends on whether one values the additional process for its own sake or thinks it merely delays inevitable outcomes.

## Key Points

- Most significant post-West Virginia v EPA application of the major questions doctrine, confirming that the doctrine applies to benefits programs as well as regulatory mandates.
- Standing holding treats state-created public instrumentalities as extensions of the State for Article III purposes, potentially expanding state-standing doctrine beyond traditional parens patriae or proprietary-interest theories.
- Interpretive holding adopts MCI v AT&T's "incremental or minor" reading of "modify" as the default understanding of modification authority in administrative law.
- Rejects the Government's argument that major questions analysis should be softer for benefits programs, invoking the Appropriations Clause as a reason for stricter rather than more lenient review.
- Foreshadows continued friction over the major questions doctrine's status as a textual or substantive canon, an issue Barrett's concurrence addresses at length.
