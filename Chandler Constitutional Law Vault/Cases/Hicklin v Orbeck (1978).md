# Hicklin v Orbeck (1978)

## Memory Jogger

> Alaska's "Alaska Hire" law requiring that residents be hired in preference to nonresidents for all oil-and-gas employment violates the Article IV Privileges and Immunities Clause because Alaska did not show that nonresidents were a peculiar source of the evil the law was designed to address.

---

## Facts

The Alaska Hire Act required employers in the state's oil-and-gas industry, including contractors and subcontractors working on pipelines and related facilities, to give hiring preference to Alaska residents over qualified nonresidents. The statute applied whenever the work "would not otherwise be done absent the employment." The state defended the law as addressing its unusually high unemployment rate among residents, particularly among Alaska Natives in rural regions, and as advancing a state interest in the economic benefits flowing from the state's ownership of the mineral resources being exploited.

Nonresident workers denied employment challenged the statute under the Article IV Privileges and Immunities Clause, which provides that "The Citizens of each State shall be entitled to all Privileges and Immunities of Citizens in the several States."

---

## Procedural History

The Alaska Supreme Court upheld the statute in part, sustaining the resident-preference requirement. The nonresident workers appealed to the U.S. Supreme Court, which noted probable jurisdiction.

---

## Judicial Votes

- **Majority:** Brennan (unanimous, 9-0)

---

## Holding

The Alaska Hire Act violates the Article IV Privileges and Immunities Clause. A state may discriminate against nonresidents in the pursuit of a common calling only if (1) nonresidents are a peculiar source of the evil the statute addresses, and (2) the discrimination bears a substantial relationship to that evil. Alaska failed both prongs: nonresident workers were not shown to be the cause of Alaska's unemployment, and the blanket preference across the entire oil-and-gas industry was not tailored to address the identified problem. The state's ownership of the resources being exploited does not exempt the hiring scheme from the clause, because the benefits the statute distributes are not a direct distribution of the state's proprietary interest but rather labor opportunities throughout the private economy that the state's mineral grants support.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Justice Brennan's unanimous opinion applies the Toomer v Witsell framework to Alaska Hire. The Article IV Privileges and Immunities Clause forbids a state from discriminating against nonresidents in the pursuit of a common calling unless the state shows (1) nonresidents constitute a peculiar source of the evil the statute addresses and (2) the discrimination is substantially related to that evil. Alaska's record failed both requirements. The state identified high in-state unemployment but did not show that nonresident workers caused it: much of the unemployment lay in rural villages where the oil-and-gas jobs were geographically inaccessible to those residents. The statute's blanket preference for all Alaska residents, regardless of training, skill, or location, was therefore overbroad relative to the asserted problem. The opinion rejects Alaska's "market participant" style argument that state ownership of the oil-and-gas resources being extracted permits the state to condition private employment decisions, holding that the benefit being distributed was labor opportunity, not a direct distribution of the state's proprietary interest in its mineral wealth.

## Key Points

- Refines the Toomer two-part test: peculiar source of the evil + substantial relationship.
- Alaska's mineral-ownership argument failed because the employment benefits were not a direct proprietary distribution.
- Frames the distinction between state-as-regulator (subject to Art IV P&I) and state-as-market-participant (not subject).
- Common calling/livelihood is a protected privilege under Article IV; recreational activities typically are not.
- Chandler emphasis: Hicklin is the canonical modern articulation of the Article IV P&I clause's nonresident-employment protection.
