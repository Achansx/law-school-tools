# United States Department of Agriculture v Moreno (1973)

## Memory Jogger

> Congress amended the Food Stamp Act to exclude households with any unrelated member; the Court struck it down because the legislative record revealed the true purpose was to harm "hippies," not to prevent fraud, and a bare desire to harm a politically unpopular group is not a legitimate government interest.

---

## Facts

The Food Stamp Act of 1964, as amended in 1971, defined "household" in § 3(e) to exclude from the program any household containing a person not related by blood, marriage, or adoption to at least one other member of the household. The amendment was passed following congressional floor statements expressing concern about "hippie communes" obtaining food stamps. The legislative history showed multiple legislators describing the amendment as intended to prevent "undesirable" persons from benefiting from the program.

Appellee Jacinta Moreno was a 56-year-old diabetic woman who shared a home with Ermina Sanchez and her three children; Sanchez was not related to Moreno. Because their household contained an "unrelated person," all members were disqualified from food stamp assistance despite individually meeting the income requirements. Similar households of unrelated individuals whose members were otherwise financially eligible were also excluded. The named appellees included a variety of households -- elderly individuals sharing quarters with younger unrelated caregivers, college-aged adults sharing apartments -- all excluded solely because of the unrelated-member provision rather than any measure of need or fraud risk.

---

## Procedural History

Appellees brought suit in the United States District Court for the District of Columbia challenging § 3(e) as unconstitutional. The three-judge district court agreed, holding that the "unrelated person" exclusion violated the equal protection component of the Fifth Amendment Due Process Clause because it bore no rational relationship to any legitimate government interest. The Government appealed directly to the Supreme Court. The Court noted probable jurisdiction and affirmed.

## Holding

Section 3(e) of the Food Stamp Act, which excluded from the program any household containing a member unrelated to all other household members, violates the equal protection component of the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment. Under traditional rational basis review, a legislative classification must be rationally related to a legitimate government interest. The exclusion fails this test for two reasons: first, the asserted justifications (preventing fraud, deterring "voluntary poverty") are not rationally served by the provision, which is both overbroad and easily evaded; second, and more fundamentally, the true motivation was legislative animus toward "hippies" -- a politically unpopular group -- and a bare congressional desire to harm a politically unpopular group cannot constitute a legitimate governmental interest under the Equal Protection guarantee.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Justice Brennan applied traditional rational basis review but found that the unrelated-person provision failed it. The Government offered two justifications: preventing fraud in the program and deterring households composed of "voluntarily poor" individuals. Brennan found both unavailing. The Act already contained independent anti-fraud provisions (§ 5(c) denying benefits to able-bodied adults who refuse employment, and §§ 14(b) and (c) imposing criminal penalties for fraudulent use), making it difficult to argue the unrelated-person rule rationally targeted those same concerns. The provision was also easily circumvented by anyone determined to commit fraud, while falling heavily on genuinely needy households who happened to lack family ties. Most pointedly, Brennan invoked the legislative history to establish that the real purpose was to exclude "hippies" -- and held that a bare congressional desire to harm a politically unpopular group cannot constitute a legitimate governmental interest for constitutional purposes.

### Dissent (Rehnquist, joined by Burger)

Justice Rehnquist's dissent rested on classical rational-basis deference. He treated the legislative-history evidence of animus as legally inert: under the conceivable-basis standard, a court is not free to rummage through floor speeches to invalidate a statute that can be squared with any plausible legitimate purpose. The asserted fraud-prevention rationale, in his view, was independently sufficient. Households that shared expenses without a family relationship would be more difficult to police for income misreporting, and Congress was entitled to draw a line at the household-composition level even if the line was over- and under-inclusive in operation. The majority's willingness to look behind the statute, Rehnquist warned, would in time blur the line between rational basis and heightened scrutiny and license judges to second-guess Congress whenever floor statements were inflammatory.

### Doctrinal Trajectory: Animus and "Rational Basis with Bite"

Moreno is best understood as the opening move in what scholars call the "rational basis with bite" line. Brennan never claimed to apply heightened scrutiny — the opinion is meticulously cast in conceivable-basis vocabulary — yet the operative move was to declare animus a non-cognizable interest, which functions as a categorical bar rather than a tier-of-scrutiny adjustment. The doctrine resurfaced in **City of Cleburne v Cleburne Living Center (1985)**, where the Court invalidated a zoning ordinance excluding group homes for persons with intellectual disabilities; in **Romer v Evans (1996)**, which struck down Colorado's Amendment 2 because its breadth and political-process effects defied any non-animus explanation; and in **United States v Windsor (2013)**, where DOMA's classification was held to lack any legitimate purpose given the legislative record's marriage-protection rhetoric. Each of these decisions cites Moreno's "bare desire to harm" formulation as the doctrinal anchor. The animus principle remains formally rational-basis, but its empirical effect is to invalidate classifications that ordinary rational-basis review would have sustained — a point Rehnquist's dissent anticipated and that critics of the doctrine have continued to press.

## Hypothetical Applications

### Same-Side (Would come out the same way)

1. **State excludes "transient adults" from emergency-shelter assistance.** A state legislature, debating the rule on the floor, repeatedly disparages "drifters" and "career homeless" as undeserving; the eligibility cutoff turns explicitly on whether an applicant has a six-month state address. Result: Same as Moreno. The legislative record supplies the animus showing, the cutoff burdens an identifiable group, and the asserted administrative-convenience rationale is not rationally served (the burden falls on those most in need of the program). Why: animus toward an identifiable politically powerless group cannot constitute a legitimate interest under Brennan's rule, and the asserted alternative rationale is too weak to absorb the classification.

2. **Federal grant program denies funding to households with adopted children of a different race than the parents.** The legislative record contains repeated statements that "transracial families" are not "real families." Result: Same as Moreno. The classification names a politically disfavored group (here a definitionally narrow class), and the legislative record removes any conceivable-basis cover for the rule. Why: the animus principle controls regardless of whether the disfavored group is large or small; what matters is the bare desire to disadvantage them.

### Opposite-Side (Would come out differently)

3. **State limits emergency rental assistance to households resident in the state for at least 12 months, with no animus showing.** The legislative record contains contemporaneously documented concerns about welfare-driven migration and program solvency, with no derogatory statements about any group. Result: Different. The Court would treat the durational-residence cutoff as economically rational: states may protect program solvency, and migration concerns supply the conceivable basis. Why: without an animus showing in the legislative record, traditional rational basis is genuinely deferential. (Compare **Shapiro v Thompson** for cases where durational requirements burden the right to travel; the present hypo assumes no fundamental-rights doctrine is in play.)

4. **Congress restricts food stamps to U.S. citizens and lawful permanent residents.** The justification is documented administrability, federal-state cost allocation, and national-resources concerns; no derogatory statements about non-citizens appear in the record. Result: Different. Citizenship-based federal classifications are reviewed under rational basis (per **Mathews v Diaz**), and the asserted fiscal and administrative interests are sufficient. Why: the classification draws on a recognized federal interest in regulating immigration benefits, and there is no animus record to disqualify the asserted rationale.

### Fence-Sitter (Genuinely unclear)

5. **State limits a child-care subsidy to households of married couples; legislative history contains a handful of statements that single mothers should "make better choices," but the bulk of the record cites administrative simplicity and per-household budgeting.** Result: Genuinely contested. The case turns on whether the marriage requirement is bare animus or a permissible (if blunt) administrative rule. Why: Moreno requires more than scattered intemperate statements; courts must assess whether the dominant rationale is the asserted legitimate one or whether the animus statements better explain the legislative move. Compare **Cleburne** (where stigma-based zoning could not be salvaged by general administrative rationales) with cases like **Romer's** dissent (Scalia, J.), which insisted that floor-speech rhetoric should not undo statutes that can be squared with conceivable interests. The right answer depends on a careful read of the legislative record's center of gravity.

## Critique

### Progressive critique

Progressive scholars largely embrace Moreno's animus principle as a necessary corrective to the conceivable-basis standard's near-irrebuttable deference. The argument runs: if rational basis truly required courts to invent post-hoc justifications for any classification, the equal-protection guarantee would be empty wherever a legislature could mumble the word "fraud." Moreno restored a floor: a classification rooted in identifiable animus toward a politically powerless group fails not because the asserted rationale is implausible in the abstract but because the actual legislative purpose is constitutionally illegitimate. This reading aligns Moreno with the Carolene Products footnote-four solicitude for "discrete and insular minorities" (here, a politically marginal class lacking access to ordinary majoritarian protection) without forcing the Court to formally adopt a heightened tier of scrutiny that would invite political backlash. The doctrinal extension to Cleburne, Romer, and Windsor is, in this view, the principle vindicating itself across new contexts.

### Originalist / textualist critique

The originalist objection (most clearly articulated in the Cleburne and Romer dissents and traceable in spirit to Rehnquist's Moreno dissent) is that the animus inquiry is doctrinally unstable and institutionally suspect. Three points carry the weight: (1) **No textual hook** — the Equal Protection Clause does not distinguish between "permissible" and "impermissible" purposes; the Court's animus doctrine grafts a purposive overlay onto a clause that on its face guarantees equal application of laws. (2) **Indeterminate evidence** — legislative history is selective, episodic, and easily cherry-picked; treating floor speeches as constitutional evidence converts the judicial role from law application to motive psychoanalysis. (3) **Slope to heightened scrutiny without democratic warrant** — by allowing animus findings to invalidate statutes that satisfy traditional rational basis, the Court effectively adds a tier of scrutiny without explaining why the new tier should apply to some groups (hippies, persons with intellectual disabilities, gay and lesbian citizens) and not others (the elderly, the wealthy, smokers). The originalist answer is that classifications should be tested by whether they can be rationally tied to a legitimate end, full stop — and motive should be left to the political process.

### Open questions

- How dominant must animus be in the legislative record before it disqualifies an otherwise-rational classification? Moreno does not resolve whether a single animus-tinged speech is enough or whether the record must show animus as the principal rationale.
- Does Moreno apply to executive-branch classifications adopted by regulation rather than statute, where there is no "legislative history" in the conventional sense? Lower courts have split.
- How does the animus principle interact with **Trump v Hawaii (2018)**, where the Court declined to apply Moreno-style animus inquiry to executive-branch national-security classifications?

## Key Points

- Moreno is the leading case establishing that legislative **animus** toward a politically unpopular group is not a legitimate government interest under rational basis review.
- The decision is the first in what courts and scholars call the **"rational basis with bite"** trilogy (Moreno 1973, Cleburne 1985, Romer 1996), in which the Court formally applied rational basis but looked more carefully at the actual legislative purpose than the conceivable-basis standard would suggest.
- Brennan did not purport to apply a heightened standard -- the opinion insists it is applying traditional rational basis. But using the legislative history to identify animus represents a departure from the default rule that the Court will not inquire into actual legislative motive.
- The animus principle from Moreno was explicitly cited and extended in **Romer v Evans** (1996) (Colorado Amendment 2 struck down as lacking rational basis given its scope and the evident animus toward gay and lesbian citizens) and in **United States v Windsor** (2013).
- Moreno is also a significant example of **Fifth Amendment equal protection** analysis, confirming that the equal protection component of the Fifth Amendment Due Process Clause constrains federal classifications in the same way the Fourteenth Amendment constrains state ones.
