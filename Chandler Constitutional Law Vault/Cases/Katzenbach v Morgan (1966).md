# Katzenbach v Morgan (1966)

## Memory Jogger

> The Court upholds Section 4(e) of the Voting Rights Act, holding that Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment is a positive grant of legislative power measured by the McCulloch standard, enabling Congress to override state literacy tests for Puerto Rican citizens even without a prior judicial finding of unconstitutionality.

---

## Facts

Section 4(e) of the Voting Rights Act of 1965 provided that no person who had completed sixth grade in an American-flag school where instruction was conducted in a language other than English could be denied the right to vote based on an inability to read or write English. The provision was aimed primarily at Puerto Rican residents of New York City who had received their education in Spanish-language schools in Puerto Rico.

New York State maintained an English literacy requirement for voter registration. The registered voters of New York City filed suit challenging Section 4(e) as beyond Congress's constitutional power and as an infringement of the state's authority to set voter qualifications. The United States and several Puerto Rican civil rights organizations intervened to defend the statute.

The constitutional question was whether Congress could exercise its Section 5 enforcement power to override a state voter qualification that the judiciary had not independently determined to be unconstitutional under the Fourteenth Amendment.

---

## Procedural History

A three-judge district court in the Southern District of New York held Section 4(e) unconstitutional, concluding that Congress lacked power to prohibit enforcement of the New York literacy requirement absent a prior judicial determination that the requirement itself violated the Fourteenth Amendment. The United States appealed directly to the Supreme Court. The Court reversed.

## Holding

Section 4(e) of the Voting Rights Act of 1965 is a valid exercise of Congress's power under Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment. Section 5 is a positive grant of legislative power, measured by the McCulloch v Maryland standard: Congress may enact any means appropriate and plainly adapted to enforcing the Amendment's guarantees, so long as the means are not prohibited by the Constitution. Congress does not need a prior judicial finding of unconstitutionality before legislating under Section 5; it is enough that the Court can perceive a rational basis upon which Congress might have resolved the constitutional question in favor of enacting the provision.

---

## Analysis

### Majority Opinion

Justice Brennan's majority adopted the McCulloch v Maryland standard as the measure of "appropriate legislation" under Section 5, drawing an analogy between the Necessary and Proper Clause's relationship to Article I powers and Section 5's relationship to the Fourteenth Amendment's substantive guarantees. The question was not whether Congress must wait for the Court to find the state law unconstitutional, but whether Congress could itself perceive a rational basis for concluding that the English literacy requirement denied equal protection to Puerto Rican voters.

Brennan identified two possible bases. First, Congress could rationally have concluded that Puerto Rican voters, denied the ballot because of their language, were thereby denied effective political participation and the ability to obtain public services, which could constitute invidious discrimination in the provision of governmental benefits. Second, Congress could rationally have concluded that granting the franchise to Puerto Rican citizens was an appropriate means of enabling them to secure non-discriminatory treatment from state and local authorities. It was for Congress, not the courts, to weigh these competing considerations and determine whether federal intervention was warranted.

The majority also addressed the "ratchet" limitation: Section 5 grants power to enforce the Amendment's guarantees, not to dilute or restrict them. Congress may "ratchet up" equal protection but may not "ratchet down."

## Key Points

- Adopts the McCulloch v Maryland necessary-and-proper standard as the measure of "appropriate legislation" under Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment.
- Congress does not need a prior judicial finding that a state law is unconstitutional before using Section 5 to override it; a "perceivable rational basis" for Congress's judgment suffices.
- The ratchet limitation: Section 5 empowers Congress to enforce (expand) the Amendment's guarantees but not to restrict or dilute them.
- Later limited by City of Boerne v Flores (1997), which imposed a congruence-and-proportionality requirement for Section 5 remedial legislation.
- Harlan's dissent is the intellectual predecessor to the City of Boerne test.
